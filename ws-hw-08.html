    From explanation to treatment

    In Chapter 1 you met the monoamine hypothesis: the idea that MDD is linked to lower availability of monoamine neurotransmitters, especially serotonin, at the synapse. If that explanation is right, a treatment that makes more serotonin available should reduce symptoms. That is the logic behind the most widely prescribed group of antidepressants, the selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs). Examples include fluoxetine (sold as Prozac), sertraline, citalopram, escitalopram and paroxetine.

    The IB asks for two things here: the explanation of a treatment (how it is thought to work) and its effectiveness (how well it works). Explanation alone will not reach the top markbands.

    A note on safety. This chapter describes how a class of medicines works and what research has found. It is not medical advice. Decisions about medication are always made with a doctor, who looks at the whole person, not an average from a study. Nobody should start, change or stop a medicine without medical advice, and nobody should ever take a medicine prescribed for someone else.

    How SSRIs work: reuptake inhibition

    Neurons communicate by neurotransmission. Here is what normally happens at a serotonin synapse:

    1. An electrical signal (an action potential) travels down the presynaptic neuron to its terminal.
    2. The signal causes small sacs called vesicles to release serotonin into the synaptic cleft, the tiny gap between two neurons.
    3. Serotonin binds to receptors on the postsynaptic neuron. This makes the next neuron more or less likely to fire.
    4. The signal is then switched off. Most serotonin is pulled back into the presynaptic neuron by a protein called the serotonin transporter. This is reuptake. Some serotonin is also broken down by an enzyme.

    An SSRI attaches to the serotonin transporter and blocks it. Reuptake slows down, so serotonin stays in the synaptic cleft for longer and can keep binding to postsynaptic receptors. The word selective means the drug acts mainly on serotonin reuptake. Older antidepressants, such as the tricyclics, affect several neurotransmitter systems at once, which is one reason they tend to cause more side effects.

    The time-lag puzzle

    An SSRI starts blocking reuptake within hours of the first tablet. Yet the NHS advises that antidepressants usually take one to two weeks to start having an effect, and the full benefit can take longer. If the problem were simply "too little serotonin", why would mood not improve straight away?

    Researchers think the benefit comes from slower changes that follow. Receptors may gradually adjust, or SSRIs may support neuroplasticity, the brain's ability to form and strengthen connections. These hypotheses are still being tested.

    The time lag suggests that SSRIs do more than "top up" serotonin. It also links to a wider point. In 2022, an umbrella review by Moncrieff and colleagues found no consistent evidence that depression is caused by low serotonin. Even if SSRIs help, that would not prove low serotonin caused the depression. Paracetamol can ease a headache, but headaches are not caused by a lack of paracetamol. Arguing backwards from a treatment to a cause is called the treatment aetiology fallacy.

    How do we know if a treatment works? Measuring effectiveness

    People with depression often improve over time, with or without treatment, and some improve because they expect to. So "people got better after taking the drug" tells us very little. Researchers need a fair comparison.

    TermWhat it meansWhy it matters
    Randomised controlled trial (RCT)Participants are randomly allocated to receive the drug or a comparison, such as a placebo.Random allocation spreads other differences (age, severity, life events) across groups, so a difference in outcome can be linked to the drug.
    PlaceboA tablet that looks identical to the real drug but has no active ingredient.It controls for expectation, attention and natural recovery. In depression trials, many people on placebo improve.
    Double-blindNeither the participants nor the researchers rating them know who is taking the drug.It reduces expectation effects and researcher bias.
    Rating scaleA standard measure of symptoms, such as the Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (HRSD), scored by a trained clinician in an interview.It turns symptoms into numbers that can be compared before and after treatment.
    ResponseUsually a fall of at least 50% in a person's rating scale score.It counts how many people improved a lot, rather than reporting only an average.
    RemissionA score low enough to count as minimal or no symptoms.A person can "respond" and still have many symptoms. Remission is a higher bar.
    Effect sizeHow big the difference between drug and placebo is, in scale points or in a standardised form.A difference can be statistically significant (unlikely to be chance) and still be too small to matter much to patients.
    Meta-analysisA study that combines the results of many trials using statistics.Larger combined samples give a more precise estimate, but only if all the relevant trials are included.

    There is a weakness inside the double-blind design. SSRIs have side effects, and a placebo usually does not. Some participants may notice side effects and correctly guess they are on the real drug. If they then expect to improve, part of the drug's advantage could be an expectation effect. Researchers call this breaking the blind.

    Side effects, in general terms

    SSRIs are usually offered before older antidepressants because their side effects are generally easier to manage. Common ones include feeling sick, headaches, changes in sleep and appetite, and feeling restless or anxious, especially in the first weeks. Many ease after a couple of weeks. Doctors see people often when they first start, and monitor young people especially closely. Stopping suddenly can cause withdrawal symptoms, so stopping is planned with the doctor. For under-18s, the NHS advises that antidepressants are usually not recommended for depression, although they are sometimes used alongside other treatment.

    Publication bias: the missing trials

    A meta-analysis is only as good as the trials that go into it. To sell an antidepressant in the United States, a company must submit its trials to the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), including those that did not go well. Journals only publish what is sent to them and accepted. Turner et al. (2008) compared 74 trials registered with the FDA with what appeared in medical journals. The FDA judged 51% of the trials to be positive. In the published literature, 94% of trials appeared positive, because most negative trials were never published or were written up in a way that made them look positive. Meta-analyses of published studies overestimated the effect size by about a third.

    This is publication bias: studies with positive results are more likely to be published than studies with negative results. It is why Kirsch and colleagues went to the FDA for their data.

    Key study 1: Kirsch et al. (2008), the FDA trial data

    AimTo find out how much newer antidepressants improve depression compared with placebo, using the trials submitted to the FDA for licensing (published and unpublished), and whether the benefit depends on how severe the depression is at the start.
    ParticipantsData from 35 placebo-controlled trials (out of 47 that were identified) with 5,133 patients with depression (3,292 given a drug and 1,841 given a placebo). The trials tested four drugs: fluoxetine and paroxetine, which are SSRIs, and venlafaxine and nefazodone, which are not.
    ProcedureA meta-analysis. The researchers used the trial data the drug companies had submitted to the FDA, including trials never published in journals. The outcome was improvement on the HRSD. They compared the drug and placebo groups against the criterion for clinical significance used by NICE, the body that advises the NHS in England: a difference of at least 3 HRSD points, or a standardised effect size of 0.5. They then tested whether the drug and placebo difference changed with the patients' starting (baseline) severity.
    FindingsBoth groups improved a lot. The drugs beat placebo, but the average difference was 1.80 HRSD points, below the NICE criterion. Almost all the trials involved patients in the very severe range at the start. Only one sample was in the moderate range, and there were no trials of mild depression. The drug and placebo difference grew with severity: it was close to zero for moderate depression, small for very severe depression, and reached the NICE criterion only at the upper end of the very severe range (a starting HRSD score of about 28 or more). This was mainly because the most severely depressed patients responded less to placebo, not because they responded more to the drug.
    ConclusionFor most patients in these trials, the advantage of the drugs over placebo was statistically real but too small to meet a common standard of clinical significance. Kirsch and colleagues argued that there was little evidence to support prescribing them to any but the most severely depressed patients, unless other treatments had failed.
    What happened nextCritics argued that the 3-point criterion is arbitrary, and that an average can hide people who benefit a lot. Stone et al. (2022) analysed 232 FDA trials with 73,388 participants. The average difference was similar, 1.75 points, but people on the drug were more likely to show a large improvement (24.5% compared with 9.6% on placebo). The authors estimated that about 15% of participants had a substantial benefit beyond the placebo effect, and called for research to find out who they are.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example of research linked to neurotransmission); Paper 1 Section B; Paper 1 Section C on measurement, bias or responsibility.
    Evaluate it withBias: using the full FDA dataset avoids the publication bias that affects meta-analyses of journal articles. Measurement: the result depends on a single rating scale and a threshold for "clinically significant" that not everyone accepts, and side effects may have broken the blind. Perspective: an average over thousands of patients cannot show who benefits, and only two of the four drugs were SSRIs, so the findings do not apply to SSRIs alone.

    Key study 2: Cipriani et al. (2018), 21 antidepressants compared

    AimTo compare and rank 21 antidepressants on efficacy (how well they reduce symptoms) and acceptability (how many people stay on them) for the short-term treatment of adults with MDD.
    ParticipantsData from 522 double-blind randomised controlled trials with 116,477 adults with MDD. Trials were excluded if 20% or more of participants had bipolar disorder, psychotic depression or depression that had not responded to earlier treatment.
    ProcedureA systematic review and network meta-analysis. The researchers searched databases, drug regulators' websites and trial registers up to January 2016, and obtained unpublished information for over half of the trials. A network meta-analysis combines direct comparisons (drug versus placebo, drug versus drug) with indirect ones, so drugs never tested against each other can still be compared. Efficacy was measured as response (a fall of at least 50% on a rating scale) after eight weeks. Acceptability was measured as the number of people who dropped out for any reason.
    FindingsAll 21 antidepressants were more effective than placebo. Odds ratios ranged from 2.13 for amitriptyline (an older drug) to 1.37 for reboxetine. An odds ratio of 2 means the odds of responding were twice as high on the drug as on placebo. Among SSRIs, escitalopram and paroxetine were among the more effective drugs, and fluoxetine was among the less effective but also among the best tolerated. The authors described the effects as modest. About 78% of the trials were funded by drug companies, although industry funding was not linked to large differences in results. Most trials were judged to have a moderate risk of bias.
    ConclusionAntidepressants, including SSRIs, are more effective than placebo for the short-term treatment of adults with MDD, and they differ in how effective and how acceptable they are. The authors stated that, because the effects are modest, not everyone will respond.
    What happened nextMoncrieff (2018) argued that the overall effect in this review, a standardised mean difference of about 0.3, is roughly 2 points on the HRSD, similar to Kirsch et al. She also argued that turning scores into "responders" and "non-responders" can make a small difference in average scores look larger.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section B (evidence that SSRIs are more effective than placebo); Paper 1 Section C on measurement, bias or change.
    Evaluate it withMeasurement: efficacy was measured as response at eight weeks, so the study tells us about short-term change, not long-term recovery or relapse. Bias: most trials were funded by drug companies, and some unpublished data may still be missing, although the researchers searched regulators and registers. Perspective: the trials were of adults, so the results cannot simply be applied to 16 and 17 year olds.

    The debate about effect size

    Kirsch et al. and Cipriani et al. are often presented as opposite sides, but both found that the drugs beat placebo and that the average advantage is small to modest. They disagree mainly about how to describe that size, and which measure is fairest.

    QuestionKirsch et al. (2008)Cipriani et al. (2018)
    Data35 FDA trials, four drugs522 trials, 21 drugs
    Main measureAverage difference in HRSD pointsOdds of response (at least 50% improvement) at eight weeks
    Main findingDrugs beat placebo by 1.80 points, below the NICE criterion except in the most severe casesAll 21 drugs beat placebo; effects described as modest

    Three points help you weigh the debate:

    • Averages hide spread. An average of about 2 points could mean everyone improves a little, or some improve a lot and others not at all. Stone et al. (2022) suggest the second, so the question is moving from "Do SSRIs work?" to "For whom?"
    • Short trials. Most trials last about eight weeks, so they do not test relapse prevention.
    • Efficacy is not effectiveness. Trials use selected patients under controlled conditions, so results in everyday practice may differ.

    How useful are SSRIs as a treatment for MDD?

    Strengths. SSRIs are supported by a very large body of double-blind, placebo-controlled trials, and the largest analyses agree that they outperform placebo. Their action at the synapse is well understood, even if the route from synapse to mood is not. They are easier to tolerate than older antidepressants and can be offered alongside talking therapy. For some patients, especially those with very severe depression, the benefit appears to be large.

    Limitations. The average advantage over placebo is small, and experts disagree about whether it is clinically meaningful for most patients. The trials are short, mostly industry-funded and mostly of adults, and side effects may break the blind. The time lag shows we do not fully understand how SSRIs work, and their success does not prove that low serotonin causes MDD. Finally, a biological treatment alone does not address the stressors and thinking patterns that the diathesis-stress model and cognitive models also describe.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to the biological treatment of depression
    MeasurementWhether SSRIs "work" depends on the measure: average HRSD points, response, remission or a threshold for clinical significance.
    CausalityRCTs allow causal claims about the drug's effect, but a treatment working does not prove what caused the disorder (the treatment aetiology fallacy).
    BiasPublication bias made antidepressants look more effective in journals than in the full FDA record, and broken blinding may bias trial results.
    ChangeImprovement takes weeks, which suggests slow changes in the brain, and the question in research has changed from "Do they work?" to "For whom?"
    PerspectiveResearchers, doctors, patients and drug companies can read the same effect size as small, modest or meaningful.
    ResponsibilityResearchers, companies, journals and reporters share responsibility for how evidence reaches patients, and doctors are responsible for decisions with each patient.
