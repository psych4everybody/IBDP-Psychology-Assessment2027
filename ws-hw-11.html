    Part A: Interviews, a recap

    What is an interview?

    An interview is a research method in which a researcher asks a participant questions and records the answers. Most interviews in psychology collect qualitative data: words, explanations and stories rather than numbers. They are used when the researcher wants to understand how people experience something, what it means to them and why they act as they do.

    In an interview, the researcher is part of the measuring instrument. The way a question is worded, the interviewer's tone, and even a nod or a frown can change what the participant says. This is the main strength of interviews (the researcher can follow the participant's thinking) and the main risk (the researcher can shape the data without meaning to).

    Back to the warm-up. Question A is a leading question: it tells the participant what answer is expected. Question B is a closed question: it allows only "yes" or "no", so you learn very little about how the student copes. Question C is an open question: the participant chooses what to talk about, in their own words.

    Types of interview

    TypeWhat it meansStrengthLimitation
    StructuredThe same questions in the same order for every participant, often with fixed answer options. No follow-up questions.Easy to compare participants and to repeat; less room for interviewer biasLittle depth; the participant cannot explain or raise new ideas
    Semi-structuredAn interview schedule of core open questions asked to everyone, plus probes and follow-up questions that the interviewer chooses during the interviewBalances comparability (same core questions) with depth (probes)Interviews differ a little, so they are harder to compare; probes can lead if the interviewer is careless
    UnstructuredA topic and perhaps one opening question; the conversation follows the participantVery rich data; the participant sets the agendaHard to compare or repeat; strongly shaped by the interviewer
    Focus groupA group interview, usually several participants with a facilitator, in which participants respond to each other as well as to the researcherShows shared views and how people discuss a topic togetherDominant speakers can take over; participants may say what the group expects; less confidentiality

    Building questions

    An interview schedule is the planned list of questions and probes. A good schedule:

    • starts with easy questions that put the participant at ease;
    • uses open questions ("Tell me about...", "How did you...", "What happened when...") for the main topic;
    • plans probes for each main question, such as "Can you give me an example?", "What did you do next?" or "What was that like for you?";
    • avoids leading questions that suggest an answer, double-barrelled questions that ask two things at once, and jargon the participant may not know (a participant will not know what "emotion-focused coping" means);
    • ends with a closing question, such as "Is there anything else you would like to tell me?", so the participant can add what the schedule missed.

    Researchers pilot the schedule with one or two people first. A pilot shows which questions confuse people, which ones lead, and how long the interview takes.

    Sampling for interviews

    Interview studies usually use small samples, because each interview takes a long time to run, transcribe and analyse. The aim is depth, not numbers.

    • Purposive sampling: participants are chosen because they have the experience the research question is about. This is the most common choice for interviews.
    • Opportunity sampling: participants who are available at the time. Quick, but they may not represent the population.
    • Snowball sampling: participants suggest other people with the same experience. Useful for hard-to-reach groups, but participants tend to know each other and think alike.
    • Self-selected (volunteer) sampling: people respond to an invitation. Volunteers may be more confident or more interested in the topic than others.

    Because samples are small and not random, interview findings are not generalised to a whole population in the way survey findings are. Instead, the researcher describes the sample and setting in detail, so that readers can judge whether the findings may transfer to other settings.

    Analysing interview data

    Interviews are usually audio recorded and then transcribed: every word is typed out, so the analysis is based on what the participant said, not what the interviewer remembers. Two common ways of analysing transcripts are:

    • Inductive content analysis: categories are built from the data upwards. The researcher does not start with a list of categories. They read, code and group the data until categories emerge.
    • Thematic analysis: codes are grouped into themes: patterns of meaning across the data that relate to the research question.

    A researcher can also code deductively: they start with categories from an existing theory and sort the data into them. Our practical did both, in that order. The inductive analysis came first, so that the theory would not steer what we noticed. The deductive coding with Lazarus and Folkman's framework came second.

    Credibility: how do we know the findings are trustworthy?

    Qualitative researchers use credibility in place of internal validity. Credibility asks whether the findings are a fair picture of what the participants experienced. Common ways to support it:

    • Member checking: returning the transcript, or a summary of the analysis, to participants so they can confirm it is accurate. Lincoln and Guba (1985) recommended it as a way of making qualitative research more rigorous. A limitation: checking a transcript only shows that it is accurate, not that the researcher's analysis is fair (Birt et al., 2016).
    • Researcher reflexivity: the researcher reflects on how their own background, expectations and feelings might affect the questions they ask and the way they interpret answers, and writes these reflections down.
    • Researcher triangulation: two or more researchers code the same transcript independently, then compare and discuss differences.
    • Rich description and quotations: reporting the participants' own words, so readers can see how each category was formed.

    Ethics in interviews

    Interviews raise particular ethical issues. Participants talk about their own lives, often in detail, and their voices are recorded. Researchers must gain informed consent, including separate consent to be recorded; protect confidentiality by using pseudonyms and removing details that could identify someone; respect the right to withdraw, including the right to skip a question; and protect participants from psychological harm, especially when the topic is personal. In the UAE, recording a person's private conversation without consent is an offence under Article 44 of Federal Decree-Law No. 34 of 2021, so recording consent is a legal requirement as well as an ethical one.

    How useful are interviews?

    Strengths. Interviews give rich, detailed data in the participant's own words. Open questions and probes let the researcher explore reasons and meanings, and discover ideas the researcher had not thought of. They suit topics, such as coping, where people's thoughts and feelings matter as much as what they do.

    Limitations. Samples are small and usually not random, so findings cannot be generalised in a statistical way. Participants may give socially desirable answers, especially to a peer interviewer. Interviewers can lead, and researchers can see what they expect to see when they analyse. Transcribing and coding take a long time.

    Real-life examples: interviews outside the classroom

    • Patient experience. Hospitals and health services interview patients about their care to find out what helped and what did not. Open questions reveal problems that a rating scale would miss, such as feeling rushed or not understanding instructions.
    • Designing an app. Before a Dubai start-up launches a revision or well-being app, its designers may interview a small group of students about how they plan their week. The designers do not know yet which problems matter, so they listen first and count later.
    • School counselling. When a school counsellor meets a Grade 12 student about applications, the conversation uses open questions and careful follow-ups. This is not research, but the same skills apply: do not lead, listen, and check that you have understood.

    Part B: Our class practical, coping with university applications

    Where the coding framework comes from

    Lazarus and Folkman (1984) described stress as a transaction between a person and a situation. The person appraises the situation (is this a threat?) and their own resources (can I cope?). They then distinguished two broad types of coping:

    • Problem-focused coping targets the cause of the stress in practical ways, for example making a plan, managing time or asking for information.
    • Emotion-focused coping tries to reduce the negative feelings that the stress produces, without changing the situation itself, for example distraction, looking at the situation more positively, or seeking comfort.

    The same behaviour can serve either purpose. Talking to a friend is problem-focused if you ask for advice about your personal statement, and emotion-focused if you want reassurance. So we coded each response by its function (what it was for), not by its form.

    Key study: Folkman and Lazarus (1985), coping with a college exam

    AimTo find out whether emotions and coping change across the stages of one stressful event: a university midterm exam.
    ParticipantsUndergraduate students taking a midterm exam in one of their university courses.
    ProcedureA longitudinal field study with repeated measures: nothing was manipulated, and the same students completed questionnaires at three stages: two days before the exam (anticipation), five days after the exam but before grades were announced (waiting), and two days after grades were announced (outcome). At each stage they rated a list of 15 positive and negative emotions and completed a version of the Ways of Coping questionnaire, which asks how much the person used each of a list of coping thoughts and actions.
    FindingsEmotions and coping changed significantly across the three stages. Before the exam, most students felt threat and challenge emotions at the same time. On average, students used combinations of most of the problem-focused and emotion-focused coping strategies at every stage. Problem-focused coping and emphasising the positive were more prominent before the exam; distancing (trying not to think about it) was more prominent while waiting for grades.
    ConclusionA stressful event is an unfolding process, not a single moment. People rarely use only one type of coping; the mix changes as the situation changes.
    Use it forPaper 2 Section A, Question 1 (where our coding framework came from); Question 3 (it is a questionnaire study on the same topic as our interview); Paper 1 on stress and health.
    Evaluate it withMeasurement: a questionnaire measured coping quickly and in the same way at each stage, but only with items the researchers had chosen. Change: repeated measurement showed coping changing over time, which a single interview cannot show. Bias: self-report may be shaped by memory and by what students think they should do.

    Our practical asked a similar question about a different stressful process: university applications. But we used interviews, so participants could describe their coping in their own words before we applied the framework.

    Our practical: the write-up

    AimTo explore how students at our school cope with the process of applying to university.
    Research questionHow do Grade 12 students at an international school in the UAE describe the ways they cope with university applications, and to what extent are these strategies problem-focused or emotion-focused?
    Method and designA semi-structured interview: one-to-one, face to face, with an interview schedule of open questions and planned probes that our class constructed.
    SamplingPurposive sampling: participants were Grade 12 students (DP or AP) who were applying to university this year and do not take psychology. They have the experience the research question is about, and because they do not study psychology they are less likely to know about coping theory or guess what we were looking for. Within that group, we interviewed students who were available and agreed, so the sample also has features of an opportunity sample.
    DataQualitative: audio recordings, transcribed word for word.
    AnalysisInductive content analysis of the transcripts, then deductive coding of coping responses as problem-focused or emotion-focused using Lazarus and Folkman's framework.

    How we built the interview schedule

    Our class constructed its own schedule. These were the rules we worked to:

    1. Start from the research question. Each main question had to help answer it. If a question was only interesting, it was cut.
    2. Open questions only for the main topic. Use "Tell me about...", "How...", "What...". Avoid questions that can be answered "yes" or "no".
    3. Plan probes. For each main question, write two or three probes in advance: "Can you give me an example?", "What did you do then?", "How did that help, if it did?"
    4. Remove leading words. Do not use "stressful", "hard" or "overwhelming" in a question, because they tell the participant how they should feel. Let the participant choose the words.
    5. No theory words. Never ask "Do you use emotion-focused coping?" The participant does not know the term, and the question tells them what we are looking for.
    6. One idea per question. "How do you plan your time and how do your parents help?" is two questions.
    7. Keep it respectful. No questions about grades, family finances, or which universities rejected the participant.
    8. Pilot it. Try the schedule with a classmate, time it, and rewrite any question that confused them or seemed to lead.

    Example of the format: one main question with probes

    Main question: "Think about a time in the application process when you had a lot to do. Tell me about what you did."

    Probes: "Can you give me an example?" / "What happened next?" / "Did that change how you felt, or what you did?" / "Is there anyone you went to at that point?"

    Procedure, step by step

    1. In class, we wrote, piloted and revised the interview schedule.
    2. We invited Grade 12 students (DP or AP) who were applying to university and do not take psychology, gave them the consent form, and agreed a time and a quiet room at school.
    3. At the start of each interview, the interviewer checked that the consent form was signed, including the recording consent, reminded the participant of the right to skip questions and to stop, and asked if they had questions.
    4. The interviewer started the audio recording and said the date and the participant's pseudonym, not their name.
    5. The interviewer asked the opening question, then the main questions in order, using probes where needed, and finished with the closing question.
    6. The interviewer stopped the recording, thanked the participant, explained what would happen to the recording and transcript, and reminded them how to contact the school counsellor.
    7. The interviewer recorded the interview date, so the recording would be deleted 7 days later.
    8. The recording was transcribed and checked, the transcript was offered to the participant, and the recording was deleted on day 7.
    9. Transcripts were analysed with inductive content analysis, then coded for problem-focused and emotion-focused coping.

    Transcription

    Because the recording is deleted after 7 days, the transcript had to be complete and checked within that week. After day 7, the transcript is the only data.

    • Type every word the participant and interviewer said, including hesitations and repeated words, so that nothing is "tidied up" into what we expected.
    • Label speakers as "I" (interviewer) and the pseudonym, for example "Noor".
    • Number the lines, so every quotation can be traced to the transcript.
    • Replace names of people, teachers and anything else that could identify the participant with a neutral label in square brackets, for example [teacher] or [university].
    • Listen to the recording once more against the transcript to correct errors before the recording is deleted.

    Coding step by step: inductive content analysis

    We followed the inductive content analysis process described by Elo and Kyngäs (2008). It has three phases: preparation, organising and reporting.

    StepWhat we did
    1. Choose the unit of analysisWe decided what counts as one piece of data. We used meaning units: a phrase or sentence that expresses one idea about applications or coping.
    2. Read and get to know the dataEach coder read every transcript at least twice before writing any codes, to get a sense of the whole.
    3. Open codingOn the third reading, we wrote short notes and headings in the margin next to each meaning unit, describing what it was about, for example "makes a deadline list" or "talks to sister".
    4. Coding sheetWe copied every heading onto a coding sheet (below), with the line number and the participant's exact words.
    5. GroupingWe grouped similar headings together and collapsed headings that meant the same thing, to reduce their number.
    6. CategorisationWe decided, by discussion, which groups belonged in the same category, and named each category in words that describe its content, for example "breaking the process into small steps".
    7. AbstractionWe grouped subcategories into categories, and categories into a small number of main categories that describe the topic as a whole.
    8. ReportWe wrote up each main category with a short definition, how many participants it appeared in, and one or two quotations as evidence.

    Inductive means the categories come from the data. During steps 1 to 7, we did not use the words "problem-focused" or "emotion-focused".

    Coding step by step: problem-focused and emotion-focused coping

    Only after the inductive categories were complete did we apply Lazarus and Folkman's framework. This second stage is deductive: the categories come from theory.

    1. Agree the definitions. Problem-focused (PF): the response aims to change or manage the cause of the stress. Emotion-focused (EF): the response aims to reduce the negative feelings, without changing the situation.
    2. Pick out coping responses. Go back to the coding sheet and mark every meaning unit that describes something the participant did or thought in order to deal with applications.
    3. Code by function. For each one, ask: what was this for? Write PF, EF, both, or unclear in the last column. "I asked my sister to read my essay and tell me what to fix" is PF. "I called my sister so I would stop worrying" is EF.
    4. Code independently, then compare. Two coders coded each transcript separately, then discussed every disagreement and agreed a final code. Units that stayed unclear were kept as "unclear", not forced into a box.
    5. Summarise. Count how many PF and EF responses each participant described, and note which inductive categories were mostly PF and which were mostly EF.
    6. Look for what does not fit. Note any inductive category that does not fit the framework well. This is where the inductive stage earns its place.

    "I booked a meeting with the counsellor to check the requirements.""I tell myself that wherever I end up, it will be fine."

    Credibility in our practical

    • Member checking. Each participant was offered their transcript, or a short summary of what they had said, and asked whether it was accurate. Because of the 7-day deletion rule, this was done within the week, while the recording could still be checked against any correction.
    • Researcher reflexivity. We are students who will apply to university next year. Our own worries could shape which probes we asked and which parts of a transcript we noticed. Before interviewing, each of us wrote down what we expected to hear; after coding, we looked back to see whether our expectations had steered us.
    • Two coders. Each transcript was coded by two students independently, and disagreements were discussed.
    • Quotations. Every category was supported by participants' own words, with line numbers.

    Ethics in our practical

    Ethical guidelineWhat we did
    Informed consentParticipants read and signed a consent form that explained the aim, what taking part involved, recording, confidentiality, withdrawal and support.
    Consent to recordingRecording consent was explicit and separate on the form. No interview was recorded without it. The recording was deleted 7 days after the interview date.
    ConfidentialityPseudonyms in all transcripts and write-ups; names, teachers, and other identifying details replaced in square brackets; transcripts seen only by the interviewers and the teacher. In a small Grade 12 group, even a course name could identify someone, so we removed those too.
    Right to withdrawParticipants could skip any question, stop the interview at any time, and withdraw their data afterwards up to the date stated on the form.
    Sensitivity of the topicUniversity applications can be a source of real pressure. Questions avoided grades, rejections and family finances. Interviewers were ready to pause, move on, or stop if a participant seemed upset, and not to push with probes.
    Signposting supportThe consent form and the end of every interview told participants how to contact the school counsellor.
    DebriefAt the end, participants were thanked, told what would happen to their data, and given the chance to ask questions.

    How good was our interview?

    Strengths. Open questions and probes gave detailed accounts of coping in the participants' own words. Because the inductive analysis came first, we could find strategies the framework does not name. The two-stage analysis lets us say both what students described and how it fits an established theory. Member checking, two coders and reflexive notes support credibility.

    Limitations. The sample was small and came from one school, so findings may not transfer to students elsewhere. Participants were interviewed by younger students from the same school, which may have increased socially desirable answers, for example sounding more organised than they felt. We were inexperienced interviewers, and a probe can lead even when a schedule is careful. Coping was described at one point in time, so we cannot see how it changed across the application process, as Folkman and Lazarus (1985) could.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to our class practical
    MeasurementCoping was not measured with numbers but captured in participants' own words, then coded. The quality of the "measurement" depends on the schedule, the accuracy of the transcript, and clear coding definitions.
    CausalityAn interview cannot show that a coping strategy caused a participant to feel better. It can show what participants believe helped them, and why.
    BiasResearcher bias (leading probes, seeing what we expected when coding), social desirability, and a small purposive sample from one school. Reflexivity, two coders and member checking reduce but do not remove it.
    ChangeCoping changes across a stressful process (Folkman and Lazarus, 1985). A single interview captures one moment; repeated interviews would show change.
    PerspectiveInterviews put the participant's perspective first. The deductive stage then adds the researcher's theoretical perspective, which is why we kept the two stages separate.
    ResponsibilityResearchers are responsible for the well-being and privacy of participants: explicit recording consent, deletion after 7 days, pseudonyms, and signposting the school counsellor.

    Using AI to learn

    Try one of these after you have attempted the chapter yourself:

    • "Give me eight interview questions about how students cope with exams, one at a time. Some should be leading, closed or double-barrelled. For each, ask me what is wrong with it, if anything, and wait for my answer."
    • "Read me ten short invented quotes from students about coping with deadlines, one at a time. For each, ask me whether it is problem-focused or emotion-focused coping and why. Tell me if my reason is about function or only about the behaviour."
    • "Here is my answer to Paper 2 Section A Question 3 comparing an interview with a questionnaire. Tell me which of my points are similarities and which are differences, and ask me two questions that would make the comparison more detailed. Do not rewrite it."

    Remember: check every study an AI tool names against this chapter or your textbook, because these tools sometimes invent studies and figures. Never paste in a transcript, a participant's words or anyone's name, and do not use AI to draft or mark your IA.
