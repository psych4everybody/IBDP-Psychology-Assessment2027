    Memory is a reconstruction, not a recording

    It is tempting to think of memory as a video camera: something happens, it is recorded, and later we press play. Research suggests that this is not how memory works. When we recall an event, we rebuild it from the pieces we stored, and we fill the gaps with what we expect to have happened. This is called reconstructive memory.

    What guides the rebuilding? A large part of the answer is schemas. A schema is a mental framework of knowledge and expectations about a person, an object, an event or a situation, built up from past experience. You have a schema for a classroom, for a wedding, for a visit to the doctor, and for how a story is supposed to go. Schema theory proposes that schemas affect memory at two points:

    • Encoding. Schemas direct attention. Details that fit a schema are easier to understand and store. Details that do not fit may be ignored, or stored in a changed form that makes more sense.
    • Retrieval. When parts of a memory are missing, schemas supply what "should" have been there. The result feels like a memory, even though some of it was built at the moment of recall.

    This is where culture comes in. Many of our schemas are learned from the people around us, so people who grow up in different cultural settings can bring different schemas to the same event. If schemas shape memory, culture should shape memory too. In the warm-up, the details that were unfamiliar to you (birch bark, seal skins, an invented village name) were probably the ones you dropped or changed. Details that fit your schemas, such as a boat, the sea and a lantern, were more likely to survive.

    Where do cultural schemas come from? Enculturation

    Enculturation is the process by which a person learns the norms, values, knowledge and practices of their own culture. It happens mostly without formal teaching: through family conversations, stories, religious and national celebrations, school, media and everyday routines. Enculturation does not only teach us facts. It also teaches us what is worth noticing, what is worth talking about, and how to tell a story about the past.

    One clear example is the way parents talk with young children about shared events, sometimes called reminiscing. In one study, Qi Wang and colleagues (2000) recorded mothers talking with their 3-year-olds. The American mothers in the sample tended to expand on what the child said and build up a detailed, personal story. The Chinese mothers in the sample more often asked and repeated factual questions, and showed more concern with moral rules and expected behaviour. Wang argued that these early conversations shape how children later remember their own lives. A child who is often asked "How did you feel?" may learn to store and recall the emotional, personal side of events. A child who is often asked "What should you have done?" may learn to store the social lesson of the event.

    Enculturation is a sociocultural idea, but its effect here is cognitive: it changes the schemas people use to encode and retrieve information.

    Cultural dimensions: a possible explanation

    Cultural dimensions are ways of describing and comparing the shared values of cultural groups. The best known come from Geert Hofstede, and the dimension used most in memory research is individualism and collectivism.

    • In more individualist cultures, people tend to see themselves as independent, and personal goals, feelings and achievements are emphasised.
    • In more collectivist cultures, people tend to see themselves as connected to others, and family and group roles, harmony and duty are emphasised.

    Researchers have suggested that these values may affect autobiographical memory (memory for events in your own life). If your culture values the unique individual, you may rehearse and recall events in which you were the main character, with your own feelings at the centre. If your culture values relationships and group roles, you may recall events in terms of the people you were with and the routines you shared.

    Be careful with this idea. Cultural dimensions describe averages across large groups. They do not tell you how any one person thinks or remembers. Most countries contain many cultures, and many people, including many students at international schools in the UAE, grow up between two or three cultures at once. Culture is also not the same as nationality. Treat cultural dimensions as a possible explanation that has to be tested, not as a label for people.

    Real-life examples

    • Heritage stories. At a heritage village in Abu Dhabi, a guide tells visitors a story about the pearl diving season. A visiting family repeats it to relatives at home, who repeat it again. Each retelling is shaped by the teller's own schemas, so details that are unfamiliar to them, such as the names of the crew roles on a dhow, may be dropped or replaced with something closer to their own experience. This is the same process Bartlett studied in serial reproduction.
    • A new student's first assembly. Leila has just moved to Sharjah and attends her first UAE National Day assembly. A week later she describes it to a friend abroad. She remembers the flags and the speeches clearly, because they fit her schema for a school celebration. She cannot recall the traditional performance well, so she describes it as "a kind of dance", using the closest schema she has.
    • Remembering Eid. Ask a class to describe one childhood memory of Eid. Some students describe one specific moment about themselves ("the year I got my first watch"). Others describe the family routine ("we always visited my grandmother first"). Neither is a better memory. They may reflect, in part, how each family talks about the past.

    Key study 1: Bartlett (1932), The War of the Ghosts

    AimTo investigate how people remember meaningful material, and in particular how a story from an unfamiliar culture changes when it is recalled.
    ParticipantsCambridge students, colleagues and other residents of Cambridge in England.
    ProcedureParticipants read "The War of the Ghosts", a Native American folk tale, twice at normal reading speed. Bartlett chose it because its jumps and gaps seem to lack logic to English readers. Repeated reproduction: each participant wrote the story from memory about 15 minutes later, and then again at increasing intervals (in one case, about ten years later). Serial reproduction: one person's version of the story was given to the next person to read and recall, like a chain. The method was informal. Delays were not the same for everyone, Bartlett did not use statistical tests, and he described the changes in his own words.
    FindingsThe story became shorter and simpler, and the biggest changes happened in the first reproduction. Details were left out (omission), and unfamiliar details were turned into familiar ones (familiarisation): "canoes" became "boats" and "hunting seals" became "fishing". Strange events were explained in a way that made sense to the participant (rationalisation): "something black" coming out of a man's mouth was often recalled as his breath, and once as his soul. Names such as Egulac and Kalama were changed and then dropped. In serial reproduction, many supernatural details were lost or replaced by elements familiar in English culture, and the changes grew along the chain.
    ConclusionRemembering is reconstructive. People fit new material into their existing, culturally learned schemas, and this distorts what they recall.
    What happened nextGauld and Stephenson (1967) found that giving participants strict instructions to avoid errors markedly reduced the errors they made. They argued that some "distortions" may be guesses that fill gaps, rather than true changes in memory. Bergman and Roediger (1999) followed Bartlett's procedure closely, testing participants after 15 minutes, one week and (where possible) six months. They found that recall got worse over time and that rationalisations and distortions increased, so the main pattern did replicate.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example for schema theory or enculturation); Paper 1 Section B; Paper 1 Section C on bias or measurement.
    Evaluate it withBias: all participants came from one culture, and there was no comparison group from the culture the story came from, so the role of culture is inferred rather than tested. Bartlett also judged the changes himself. Measurement: the changes were described, not scored, and Gauld and Stephenson suggest that some "errors" measure guessing or effort rather than memory. Causality: no variable was manipulated, so the study shows what happens to unfamiliar material but cannot show that culture caused each change.

    Key study 2: Wang (2001), earliest childhood memories

    AimTo investigate whether culture affects adults' earliest childhood memories and how they describe themselves.
    Participants119 American and 137 Chinese college students.
    ProcedureA quasi-experiment: the independent variable was the participants' culture, which was not manipulated. Participants described their earliest childhood memory and also described themselves. The memories were compared across the two groups on the age of the memory and on features such as length, specificity, emotional content and whether the self or the group was at the centre.
    FindingsOn average, the American students' earliest memories dated from about age 3½, and the Chinese students' earliest memories from about six months later. American students more often gave long, specific, emotionally detailed memories with themselves as the main character. Chinese students more often gave brief accounts of general, routine events that centred on shared activities with others, and that were emotionally neutral.
    ConclusionCulture shapes autobiographical memory. Wang linked the two styles to individual-focused and group-oriented values in the two cultures, and argued that memory and the sense of self develop together.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example for cultural dimensions or enculturation); Paper 1 Section B; Paper 1 Section C on bias, causality or perspective; Paper 2 Section B practice (a quasi-experiment).
    Evaluate it withCausality: culture was not manipulated and participants were not randomly allocated, so other differences between the groups could explain the results. Bias: college students may not represent everyone in either culture, and a scoring system that counts length, detail and emotion may favour one culture's style of remembering. Perspective: the study compares two cultures from the outside (an etic approach), so it says little about what a "good" memory means within each culture.

    Studying memory across cultures: emic and etic approaches

    When researchers study memory in more than one culture, they have to decide where to start.

    • An etic approach studies a behaviour from outside a culture. It uses ideas and measures that are assumed to apply everywhere, so that cultures can be compared on the same scale. Wang (2001) is an example: the same task and the same features were used for both groups.
    • An emic approach studies a behaviour from inside a culture. It starts with the meanings, categories and practices that matter to people in that culture, often through observation, interviews and local researchers. It gives a richer picture of one culture but makes comparison harder.
    FeatureEtic approachEmic approach
    Starting pointConcepts assumed to be universalConcepts that matter within the culture
    Typical methodsThe same test or task given in several culturesObservation, interviews, local knowledge
    Main strengthAllows direct comparisonMore valid within the culture
    Main riskAn "imposed etic": a measure from one culture is treated as universalFindings are hard to compare or generalise

    The risk of cultural bias in memory tests

    A memory test is never culture-free. It uses material, a language and a scoring system, and each of these can favour one group.

    • Material. Bartlett's story was unfamiliar to his English participants, which was the point of the study. A test that uses objects, names or story structures from one culture will make people from another culture look worse at remembering, when they are really less familiar with the content.
    • Scoring. If a test gives more credit to long, detailed, emotional memories, it may reward one culture's way of telling a memory. A brief memory about a family routine is not a weaker memory. It may be a different kind of memory.
    • Interpretation. When a group scores lower on a test designed elsewhere, it is easy to conclude that the group has a weaker memory. The more careful conclusion is that the test may be measuring familiarity, or a cultural style, as well as memory.

    Researchers reduce this bias by using material that is familiar to every group, piloting tests in each culture, working with researchers from each culture, translating and back-translating instructions, and combining etic comparisons with emic research.

    How useful is research on cultural factors in memory?

    Strengths. The research shows that memory is not a fixed, universal machine that works the same way in everyone. It links a cognitive process (memory) to sociocultural factors (schemas learned through enculturation), which gives a fuller explanation than either level alone. Bartlett's main finding has been replicated by Bergman and Roediger (1999), and Wang's findings fit with work on how parents talk to children about the past. The ideas also have practical uses, for example in designing fairer tests and in interviewing witnesses from different cultural backgrounds.

    Limitations. Culture cannot be manipulated, so most studies are quasi-experiments or correlations, and they cannot show that culture causes a difference. Cultural dimensions are usually assumed rather than measured in the participants themselves. Many studies compare only two groups, often American and East Asian university students, which leaves out most of the world's cultures. Measures designed in one culture may carry cultural bias. Finally, Bartlett's informal method means some changes may reflect guessing rather than memory.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to cultural factors in memory
    MeasurementWhat counts as a "good" or "accurate" memory depends on how it is scored, and scoring systems may reflect one culture's style.
    CausalityCulture cannot be randomly allocated, so studies such as Wang (2001) show differences between groups but cannot prove that culture caused them.
    BiasSchemas bias what we recall, and memory tests and samples can be culturally biased.
    ChangeMemories change each time they are reconstructed, and people who move between cultures may develop new schemas.
    PerspectiveEmic and etic approaches give different views of the same behaviour, and the explanation joins cognitive and sociocultural levels.
    ResponsibilityResearchers comparing cultures must avoid stereotyping groups or presenting a difference in style as a weakness.

    Rung 4: One more question

    The finding: In Wang (2001), Chinese students' earliest childhood memories dated from about six months later, on average, than American students' memories, and they were briefer and less focused on the self.

    A first reaction: "So the Chinese students have weaker memories of their childhood." Before you accept that, ask one more question.

    • Does the age of someone's earliest memory, or the length of their account, tell you how well memory works, or something else?
    • Who decided that a long, emotional, self-focused memory is the standard to compare against?
    • Were the two groups alike in every way except culture, and what else (such as language, schooling or how families talk about the past) could differ?
