    What is theory of mind?

    Cognitive models

    Theory of mind is the ability to understand that people have mental states, such as beliefs, desires, intentions, knowledge and emotions, and that these mental states guide what they do. It also includes understanding that other people's mental states can be different from your own, and different from reality.

    It is called a "theory" because we never see another person's mind. We only see what they do and say, and from that we build an explanation. When your friend runs back into the classroom and searches her desk, you see someone who wants something and believes it is in her desk. That explanation is your theory of her mind.

    Theory of mind is a good example of a cognitive model. A cognitive model is a simplified description of a mental process that explains how information is represented and used, and makes predictions about behaviour. The theory of mind model proposes that children come to represent other people's mental states as separate from the world itself. They can hold two things in mind at once: "the marble is in the box" (reality) and "Sally thinks the marble is in the basket" (Sally's belief). The model predicts that a child who can do this will be able to predict behaviour based on a mistaken belief. False-belief tasks test that prediction.

    In the warm-up, you could set aside what you knew and think about what Rashid knows. Many 3-year-olds find this hard. They answer from what they know, and may even report that they always thought the box held paper clips.

    False-belief tasks

    Why do researchers focus on false beliefs? If someone's belief is true, a child can predict their behaviour just by thinking about reality, without thinking about the person's mind at all. A false belief separates the two. If a child predicts that Sally will look where she thinks the marble is, rather than where it really is, the child must be using a representation of Sally's belief.

    There are two classic types of false-belief task.

    • Location change (unexpected transfer). An object is moved while a character is away. The child is asked where the character will look for it. Wimmer and Perner (1983) used a story about a boy called Maxi, whose chocolate was moved from one cupboard to another while he was out. Children aged 3 to 4 usually said Maxi would look where the chocolate really was; from around 4 to 5, most said he would look where he left it. The Sally-Anne task (Key study 1) uses the same design.
    • Unexpected contents. A child discovers that a familiar box holds something unexpected, and is asked what another person will think is inside, as in the warm-up.

    Good false-belief tasks also include control questions. Reality and memory questions check that the child followed the story. If a child answers both correctly but fails the belief question, the failure is less likely to be caused by poor memory or attention.

    How theory of mind develops across childhood

    Theory of mind does not appear overnight. It builds in steps, and passing a false-belief task is only one of them. The ages below are approximate and vary between children and cultures.

    StepApproximate ageWhat the child can do
    Joint attentionTowards the end of the first yearFollows another person's gaze or pointing and looks at the same thing, sharing attention with them.
    Pretend playSecond yearUses one object as if it were another (a block as a phone), keeping pretence separate from reality.
    Understanding desiresAround 18 monthsUnderstands that another person can want something different from what the child wants (Repacholi and Gopnik, 1997).
    Knowledge accessAround 3 to 4 yearsUnderstands that someone who has not seen inside a box does not know what is in it.
    False beliefAround 4 to 5 yearsPredicts that a person will act on a belief that the child knows is wrong.
    Later skillsPrimary school years and beyondUnderstands beliefs about beliefs ("she thinks that he thinks..."), hidden emotions, white lies and sarcasm, developing into adolescence.

    The big picture comes from Wellman, Cross and Watson (2001), a meta-analysis of 178 studies of false-belief tasks. As preschool children got older, they moved from below-chance to above-chance performance. Some task changes made the tasks easier, but did not remove the age pattern. The researchers concluded that this is a real conceptual change in the preschool years, not just a side effect of task design.

    How theory of mind supports social cognition

    Social cognition is how we think about ourselves and other people. Theory of mind is at its centre, because social life depends on working out what others know, want and intend.

    • Communication. To explain something well, you have to judge what your listener already knows.
    • Secrets and deception. Keeping a surprise or playing hide-and-seek well requires you to understand that another person can be kept from knowing something.
    • Learning from others. Understanding that some people know more than you helps children choose whom to learn from.
    • Understanding emotions. Knowing that a friend is upset because she thinks she was left out, even if she was not, needs theory of mind.
    • Thinking about your own thinking. Mariam in the warm-up could not report her own earlier false belief. Understanding that your own beliefs can change, and can be wrong, is part of the same development.

    Real-life examples

    • Keeping an Eid surprise. Hamdan, aged 5, helps his older sister in Sharjah hide a gift for their grandmother. To keep the secret, he has to understand that his grandmother does not know about the gift. A 3-year-old cousin may tell her straight away, not to be naughty, but because keeping a secret depends on thinking about what someone else knows.
    • Storytime in a KG class. A KG teacher in Abu Dhabi stops a story and asks, "Where does the rabbit think his carrot is?" Questions like this get children talking about thinking, knowing and wanting, and research on language (see below) suggests this kind of talk may help theory of mind to grow.

    Key study 1: Baron-Cohen, Leslie and Frith (1985), the Sally-Anne task

    A note on language: the original paper used terms that are now out of date. This chapter uses "autistic children", which many autistic people prefer, and "children with Down syndrome".

    AimTo test whether autistic children have difficulty understanding false beliefs, and whether any difficulty is specific to autism rather than a result of general learning difficulties.
    Participants61 children in three groups: 20 autistic children (mean age 11 years 11 months; mean verbal mental age 5 years 5 months), 14 children with Down syndrome (mean age 10 years 11 months; mean verbal mental age 2 years 11 months) and 27 typically developing preschool children (mean age 4 years 5 months). The Down syndrome group had a lower mental age than the autistic group, so it controlled for general learning difficulties.
    ProcedureEach child was tested individually with two dolls. Sally put a marble in her basket and left. Anne moved it to her box. Sally came back. The child was asked a naming question (which doll is which), the belief question ("Where will Sally look for her marble?"), a reality question ("Where is the marble really?") and a memory question ("Where was the marble in the beginning?"). There were two trials. In the second, the marble was moved to the experimenter's pocket.
    FindingsAll children answered the naming, reality and memory questions correctly. On the belief question, 23 of 27 typically developing children (85%) and 12 of 14 children with Down syndrome (86%) passed on both trials. 16 of the 20 autistic children (80%) failed on both trials, and every one of them pointed to where the marble really was. 4 autistic children passed.
    ConclusionThe researchers concluded that the autistic children, as a group, did not use a theory of mind to predict Sally's behaviour, and that this difficulty could not be explained by general learning difficulties, because the Down syndrome group performed almost as well as the preschool children.
    What happened nextLater research questioned the original interpretation. Happé (1995) pooled earlier data and found that autistic children needed a much higher verbal mental age to pass false-belief tasks (around 9 years for a 50% chance of passing, compared with about 4 years for typically developing children), so language plays a large part. Gernsbacher and Yergeau (2019) argued that the claim that autistic people lack a theory of mind is not supported: difficulties are not found in all autistic people, some findings have not replicated, and theory of mind tasks relate poorly to each other and to everyday social behaviour. Milton (2012) proposed the double empathy problem: when people with very different experiences of the world interact, both sides can struggle to understand each other, so difficulties run in both directions.

    Evaluate it withMeasurement: theory of mind was inferred from one pointing response on a task that also needs language; Happé's findings suggest verbal ability affects the result. Causality: this compares existing groups, so it shows a difference but not its cause. Perspective: autism is studied from the outside, with a test designed by non-autistic researchers; the double empathy problem offers another view. Bias: 20 autistic children is a small sample, and 4 of them passed, so the result does not describe every autistic child.

    Key study 2: Repacholi and Gopnik (1997), broccoli and crackers

    AimTo investigate whether 14- and 18-month-old children understand that another person can have a desire that is different from their own.
    Participants159 children in the final sample: 81 aged about 14 months and 78 aged about 18 months, mostly from middle-class families. Others were recruited but excluded, for example for fussiness.
    ProcedureEach child tasted two foods: Goldfish crackers and broccoli. Most preferred the crackers. The experimenter then tasted each food and showed a clear reaction with her face and voice: happiness to one food and disgust to the other. In the matched condition, she liked the crackers and disliked the broccoli, like most children. In the mismatched condition, she showed disgust at the crackers and happiness at the broccoli. She then held out her hand between the bowls and asked, "Can you give me some?" without looking at either food.
    FindingsMost 14-month-olds (68%) did not respond to the request at all. Those who responded mostly offered crackers, even in the mismatched condition, and were no better than chance. Among 18-month-olds who responded, 73% gave the experimenter the food she liked, in both conditions. In the mismatched condition, that meant giving her broccoli, the food they did not prefer.
    ConclusionBy 18 months, children can use a person's emotional reactions to work out what that person wants, even when it differs from what the child wants. This is an early step in theory of mind: understanding desires comes well before understanding false beliefs.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section B (a scenario about theory of mind); Paper 1 Section C (theory of mind); Paper 1 Section B; Paper 1 Section C on change or measurement; Paper 2 Section B practice (an experiment with two independent variables: age and condition).
    Evaluate it withMeasurement: the task needs no words from the child, but most 14-month-olds gave no response, so we cannot tell whether they did not understand desires or were shy with a stranger. Change: the change between 14 and 18 months comes from comparing different children (cross-sectional), not following the same children over time. Causality: because the experimenter's reaction was the only planned difference between the matched and mismatched conditions, the design supports the claim that her reaction, not the child's own preference, guided the 18-month-olds' choices. Bias: the sample was mostly from middle-class families.

    What do false-belief tasks really measure?

    To pass a false-belief task, a child must follow a story, remember where the object was, understand the question, and hold back the urge to point to where the object really is. So when a 3-year-old fails, there are several possible explanations.

    • Language. Classic tasks depend on words like "think". Astington and Jenkins (1999) tested 59 three-year-olds three times over seven months. Earlier language skills predicted later theory of mind performance, but earlier theory of mind did not predict later language. A meta-analysis by Milligan, Astington and Dack (2007) also found a reliable link between language ability and false-belief understanding. This fits Happé's (1995) finding about verbal ability.
    • Task demands. The child knows where the marble really is, and that knowledge pulls the answer towards the box. Failing may partly reflect difficulty in holding back a known answer, rather than a lack of understanding.
    • Implicit understanding. Onishi and Baillargeon (2005) tested 56 infants aged 15 months using looking time. An actor hid a toy in a green box, and the toy then moved to a yellow box while she could not see. Infants looked longer when she reached into the yellow box, where the toy really was, as if surprised. This suggests some understanding of false belief long before children pass verbal tasks. However, when Kulke and colleagues (2018) tried to repeat two other implicit looking tasks, the findings did not replicate in children, and the two tasks did not correlate with each other. Implicit measures are promising but not settled.

    Does theory of mind develop at the same time in every culture?

    If theory of mind is universal, the timing should be similar everywhere. Callaghan et al. (2005) gave the same location-change task to 267 children aged 2.5 to 6 in five countries: Canada, India, Peru, Samoa and Thailand. In all five, children moved from failing to passing between about 3 and 5. This supports a universal timeline.

    Other studies complicate the picture. Mayer and Träuble (2013) studied more than 300 children in Samoa: most did not pass before about age 8, and about a third of 10- to 13-year-olds still failed. Shahaeian et al. (2011) compared 77 Australian and 58 Iranian children aged 3 to 6 on a five-step theory of mind scale. Overall success was similar, but the order of the steps differed. Iranian children understood knowledge access before they understood that people can hold different opinions; Australian children showed the reverse order. So culture may shape the timing and sequence of development. It also shows why an emic view matters: a task designed in one culture may not mean the same thing in another, especially once it is translated.

    Behavioural desire task (broccoli and crackers)Gives an object to a personDesire understanding by 18 monthsNo words needed from the childMany young children do not respond at all

    How useful is theory of mind for understanding human development?

    Strengths. Theory of mind explains a wide range of social behaviour with one idea: children learn that minds represent the world, and can represent it wrongly. It makes testable predictions, and the main age pattern is reliable across 178 studies (Wellman et al., 2001) and several cultures (Callaghan et al., 2005). It links development and cognition, since the same ability helps children communicate, keep secrets, learn from others and think about their own beliefs. It also has practical uses, such as encouraging adults to talk with young children about thoughts and feelings.

    Limitations. Theory of mind is never observed directly, and different tasks give very different ages, from 15 months to 5 years. A child may fail because of language or task demands rather than a lack of understanding. Timing and sequence vary between cultures, so the "4 to 5 years" milestone is not universal. The claim that autistic people lack a theory of mind is now widely challenged, which shows the risk of big conclusions from a single task.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to theory of mind
    MeasurementTheory of mind is inferred from pointing, giving or looking. Different measures give different ages.
    CausalityRepacholi and Gopnik changed only the experimenter's reaction between two conditions, which supports a causal claim. Baron-Cohen et al. compared existing groups, so they could show a difference but not its cause.
    BiasSmall samples, mostly Western middle-class families and tasks designed in one culture limit how far findings generalise.
    ChangeTheory of mind develops in steps, from desires at around 18 months to false beliefs at around 4 to 5. The order of steps can differ between cultures.
    PerspectiveTheory of mind is a cognitive explanation. Sociocultural research adds language and culture, and the double empathy problem includes autistic people's own view.
    ResponsibilityResearchers must describe autistic people accurately and respectfully; claims that a group "lacks" an ability can shape how people are treated.
