    What counts as an environmental factor?

    An environmental factor is anything outside the person that may raise or lower their risk of a mental health disorder: what happens to us (life events), the conditions we live in (chronic stress), the people around us (social support and networks), what we learn from others (social learning), the groups we belong to (social identity), and what happened to us as children (childhood adversity).

    Environmental factors rarely act alone. In the diathesis-stress model, a person carries a vulnerability (a diathesis), such as a genetic risk or a negative thinking style, and an environmental stressor then triggers the disorder. Most people who face a difficult event do not become depressed. This chapter asks why some people do.

    One research problem runs through the whole chapter. You cannot randomly assign people to lose a job or to have a difficult childhood. So almost all the evidence is correlational: researchers measure the environment, measure depression, and look for a link. Two questions follow. How well can we measure these factors? And can a link ever show cause?

    Life events and chronic stress

    A life event is a specific change at a specific time, such as a bereavement, losing a job or moving country. Chronic stress lasts for months or years, such as long-term money worries or caring for an ill relative. Brown and Harris (1978) called this a difficulty.

    Not every event has the same effect. The events most linked to depression are severe: they carry a long-lasting threat, often the loss of something central, such as a relationship, a role or a plan for the future.

    Brown and Harris called a severe event or major difficulty shortly before the onset of depression a provoking agent. Vulnerability factors do not cause depression on their own, but make it more likely when a provoking agent arrives. The most important was the lack of a close, confiding relationship.

    Social support and social networks

    Social support is the help and comfort other people give us. A social network is the web of people we are connected to, and the people they are connected to in turn.

    The social environment could matter in two ways. The buffering idea says support protects us when stress arrives: a confidant helps you make sense of a loss, so it is less likely to turn into hopelessness. The network idea says moods and ways of coping may pass between connected people. Rosenquist, Fowler and Christakis (2011) tested the second idea.

    Groups may matter as well as friends. Cruwys et al. (2013) analysed data from over 4,000 adults aged over 50 in England. Belonging to more social groups predicted lower depression later, especially for people who were already depressed. Among depressed participants with no group memberships, joining one group was linked to a 24% lower risk of relapse, and joining three groups to a 63% lower risk. This is correlational, but longitudinal: group membership was measured before the later depression.

    Social learning theory as an explanation

    Social learning theory (Bandura) proposes that we learn behaviour by observing models: we attend to what they do, remember it, and reproduce it when motivated. Seeing a model rewarded makes copying more likely (vicarious reinforcement).

    Applied to depression, social learning theory suggests that a child who often watches a parent respond to setbacks with withdrawal, self-blame or hopeless statements ("there is no point trying") may learn these responses as a way of coping. Over time, the learned pattern could become part of the negative thinking style described by cognitive models, making the child more vulnerable when stress arrives. Friends may also model ways of coping for each other, which could explain part of the network finding.

    Be careful with this in an exam. It is a plausible application, but hard to test. Children of a depressed parent also share that parent's genes and household stressors, so a parent-child link does not show learning by observation.

    Social identity theory as an explanation

    Social identity theory (Tajfel and Turner) proposes that part of our self-concept comes from the groups we belong to, such as a family, team, school or community. Valued groups give us self-esteem and a sense of who we are.

    Applied to depression, social identity theory suggests two things. First, losing a group can mean losing part of the self. Moving country, losing a job or leaving a team removes a source of identity, not just company, and a person may become more open to the negative self-evaluation of the cognitive triad ("I am worthless"). Second, joining groups can protect against depression when the group becomes part of who the person is, an idea called the social cure. Cruwys et al. (2013) counted group memberships but did not measure how strongly people identified with each group, so they support the idea without testing the identity part directly.

    Childhood adversity

    Childhood adversity means serious difficulties experienced in childhood. The best-known research is the Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACE) Study (Felitti et al., 1998). About 9,500 adults who had a medical check-up with a large health organisation in San Diego, USA, completed a questionnaire about seven categories of childhood experience. These included experiences of abuse and kinds of household difficulty, such as living with a family member who had a mental illness or who had been in prison.

    More than half reported at least one category. The researchers found a graded relationship (a dose-response relationship): the more categories a person reported, the higher their risk of a range of adult health problems. Compared with people who reported none, people who reported four or more categories had a 4 to 12 times greater risk for a group of outcomes that included depression. The 4 to 12 range covers several outcomes together, so do not quote "12 times" as the figure for depression.

    How could childhood adversity raise the risk? Biologically, long-lasting stress in childhood may change how the stress response system develops. Cognitively, early experiences may shape schemas such as "I am not safe". A graded relationship is strong evidence, but it is still correlational, and ACEs were reported by adults looking back many years.

    How do researchers measure environmental factors?

    Every claim in this chapter depends on measuring two things: the environment and depression.

    • Life event checklists. The Social Readjustment Rating Scale (Holmes and Rahe, 1967) lists 43 life events, each scored in "life change units"; death of a spouse scores highest, at 100. Checklists are quick, but they give everyone the same score for an event (as in the warm-up), do not separate wanted from unwanted changes, and a person who is already depressed may report events differently.
    • Life event interviews. The Life Events and Difficulties Schedule (LEDS), developed by Brown and Harris, is a long semi-structured interview that collects the story of each event. Raters then judge how threatening it would be for an average person in the same circumstances, without using how the person actually reacted. It is slow, but it captures what an event meant in that person's life.
    • Depression questionnaires. The CES-D (Radloff, 1977) is a depression questionnaire with 20 items about how often a person felt certain ways in the past week, with a total score from 0 to 60. A score of 16 or more is often used to indicate depression. It is a screening tool, not a diagnosis.
    • Looking back. ACEs, and many life events, are reported years later. Memory can fade or be shaped by current mood.

    Real-life examples

    • Moving to the UAE. Most people living in the UAE are expatriates. A teacher who moves to Dubai leaves behind family, friends and a professional identity built over years. Social identity theory predicts that rebuilding group memberships, such as joining a choir or a sports club, matters for well-being, not only finding people to talk to.
    • Community gatherings. Many communities in the UAE have a majlis, a traditional gathering place where relatives, neighbours and friends meet regularly to talk. A regular place to share news and problems works like a ready-made confiding network, although no study in this chapter tested this in the UAE.
    • Starting at a new school. A school in Sharjah pairs each new student with a current student for their first term. This gives new arrivals a group to belong to quickly, which fits both the buffering idea and the social cure.

    Key study 1: Brown and Harris (1978), the social origins of depression

    AimTo investigate the role of social factors, especially life events and ongoing difficulties, in the onset of depression in women living in the community.
    Participants458 women living in Camberwell, south London, sampled from the general community rather than from patients.
    ProcedureResearchers used a standard psychiatric interview to assess whether each woman had become depressed in the past year. A semi-structured interview (which became the LEDS) recorded life events and ongoing difficulties, with the background of each one. A separate team rated the severity of each, judging the threat it would carry for an average woman in the same circumstances.
    Findings37 women (8% of the sample) had become depressed in the past year. Of these, 33 (almost 90%) had experienced a severe life event or major difficulty before the onset. Only about 30% of the women who did not become depressed had experienced such an adversity. Four vulnerability factors increased the risk of depression when a severe event or difficulty occurred: lack of a close, confiding relationship; three or more children under 14 living at home; no paid employment outside the home; and loss of the mother before the age of 11. Working-class women with children were more likely to become depressed than middle-class women with children.
    ConclusionDepression in these women was often brought on by a severe event or difficulty (a provoking agent), and it was more likely when vulnerability factors were present, especially the lack of a confiding relationship. Depression has social origins, not only biological or cognitive ones.
    What happened nextCostello (1982) repeated the procedure with 449 women aged 18 to 65 in Calgary, Canada. Lack of an intimate relationship and severe life events and difficulties were again linked to depression. But employment status, number of children at home, loss of mother before 11 and social class were not. Costello suggested that some social risk factors may be specific to a community.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section B (life events, chronic stress, social support); Paper 1 Section C on measurement or causality; Paper 2 Section B practice (interviews and community samples).
    Evaluate it withMeasurement: the contextual ratings judged an event by its circumstances, not by how the woman reported feeling, which reduces the risk that depression coloured the measure of stress. Causality: the events came before the onset, which supports a causal direction, but the design is correlational and women were asked to look back over the past year. Bias: women from one area of London, so the findings may not generalise to men or to other communities, as Costello's replication suggests.

    Key study 2: Rosenquist, Fowler and Christakis (2011), depression in social networks

    AimTo test whether depressive symptoms cluster in social networks, and whether a person's depression is linked to the depression of the people they are connected to, including friends of friends.
    Participants12,067 people in the Framingham Heart Study, a long-running health study in Framingham, Massachusetts, USA. The focal participants were 5,124 members of the study's Offspring Cohort. Their contacts came from across the Framingham cohorts.
    ProcedureFramingham records of each participant's relatives, spouse, close friends and neighbours let researchers map the network over time. Depressive symptoms were measured with the CES-D at three examinations between 1983 and 2001. A person was classed as depressed if they scored 16 or more. Statistical models tested whether a person's depression at one examination was linked to their contacts' depression, controlling for the person's age, gender, education and their own earlier depression score.
    FindingsIf a directly connected person was depressed, a participant was 93% more likely to be depressed. The figure was 43% at two degrees of separation (a friend of a friend) and 37% at three degrees. At four degrees there was no effect. Depressed female friends were linked to a much larger increase; male friends showed no significant effect. Mutual friends showed the largest effect, while friends who named the participant without being named back showed none. People with more friends had fewer symptoms later, but depressed people also lost about 6% of their friends over roughly four years.
    ConclusionDepressive symptoms cluster in social networks up to three degrees of separation. The authors suggested that depression may spread between people, and also that depression shapes networks, because depressed people tend to lose friends.
    What happened nextCritics questioned the "contagion" claim. Cohen-Cole and Fletcher (2008) used the same kind of model on data from American teenagers and found apparent "network effects" for acne, headaches and height, which cannot spread between friends. Once they adjusted for shared environment, the effects became small and non-significant. Shalizi and Thomas (2011) showed that in observational network data, homophily (choosing friends who are like you) and contagion cannot be separated without strong assumptions.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section B (social networks and social support); Paper 1 Section C on causality or measurement; Paper 2 Section B practice (longitudinal correlational research).
    Evaluate it withCausality: the longitudinal data and the friendship-direction test make a social influence explanation more plausible, but shared environment and homophily cannot be ruled out. Measurement: "depressed" means a CES-D score of 16 or more, a screening cut-off, not a clinical diagnosis, and friendships were limited to the few close contacts each person listed. Bias: the sample came from one American town and included few people from minority groups.

    How useful are environmental explanations of depression?

    Strengths. Environmental research explains what biology alone cannot: why depression often begins after a loss, and why it is more common in some communities. The links with severe events and lack of a confidant appeared again in Costello's replication. Longitudinal studies measure the environment before later depression, which helps with direction. The research is also useful: if support and group membership protect, schools and communities can build them.

    Limitations. Most evidence is correlational, so genes, poverty or shared neighbourhoods may explain the links. The arrow can point both ways: depressed people lose friends. Checklists are crude, interviews are slow, and childhood experiences are recalled years later. Many key studies come from the UK and USA, so specific risk factors may not transfer. Environmental explanations work best alongside biological and cognitive ones, as the diathesis-stress model suggests.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to environmental factors in one or more mental health disorders
    MeasurementStress can be measured with a quick checklist (SRRS) or a long contextual interview (LEDS); depression with a screening questionnaire (CES-D) or a clinical interview. What counts as "stress" and "depressed" shapes the findings.
    CausalityResearchers cannot randomly assign people to adversity, so they rely on timing (events before onset), longitudinal data and graded relationships. None of these fully rules out third variables.
    BiasSamples from one town or one area of London, and adults recalling childhood, may distort findings. Depressed people may also recall more negative events.
    ChangeDepression can change a person's network (losing friends), and changing the environment (joining groups) may support recovery.
    PerspectiveA sociocultural perspective looks for causes in relationships and groups; the diathesis-stress model combines this with biological and cognitive perspectives.
    ResponsibilityIf the environment contributes to depression, schools, workplaces and communities share some responsibility for building support, and depression is not simply a personal failing.
