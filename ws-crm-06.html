    Surveys and questionnaires

    A survey collects self-reported information from people using a fixed set of written questions, the questionnaire. The IB treats the two words as interchangeable. Because every participant gets the same items, surveys are standardised, and they can reach large samples quickly, on paper or online. A survey can be cross-sectional (one snapshot of different people at one time) or longitudinal (the same people surveyed again over months or years).

    Item typeWhat it looks likeDataStrength and limitation
    Closed questionFixed options: "How many hours do you sleep on a school night? Under 6 / 6 to 7 / 7 to 8 / over 8"QuantitativeEasy to count and compare; participants cannot explain or add anything
    Rating scale (Likert) itemA statement rated on a scale: "I feel anxious before tests" from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (5)Quantitative (ordinal)Measures strength of attitude; people may avoid the extremes or always choose the middle
    Open questionNo options: "What do you do when schoolwork feels overwhelming?"QualitativeDepth and unexpected answers; harder and slower to analyse and compare

    Good questionnaires use clear, neutral wording; one idea per question (no "double-barrelled" items such as "Do you find homework useful and enjoyable?"); simple, general questions first and more personal ones later; and a pilot with a few people to catch confusing items before the real survey.

    Biases in self-report

    Surveys and interviews both depend on what people say, which brings particular risks:

    • Social desirability bias: answering in the way that looks good rather than truthfully, for example under-reporting screen time.
    • Acquiescence: a tendency to agree with statements whatever they say. Mixing positively and negatively worded items helps to detect it.
    • Leading questions: wording that suggests the "right" answer, such as "Don't you agree that phones distract students?"
    • Question order effects: earlier questions shape answers to later ones.
    • Memory limits: people are often poor at recalling how often they did something, especially over long periods.
    • Who replies: if only some people return the survey, the sample may be biased; people who care strongly about a topic are more likely to respond.

    Anonymity, neutral wording, careful ordering and piloting reduce these problems. None removes them completely, which is why self-report is often combined with other measures.

    Interviews and focus groups

    In an interview the researcher asks questions in person, and the interviewer becomes part of the measuring instrument: their wording, tone and follow-up questions shape the data.

    TypeWhat it meansStrengthLimitation
    StructuredThe same questions in the same order for everyone, with no follow-upComparable answers; several interviewers can be standardisedLittle depth; no chance to explore unexpected answers
    Semi-structuredA core interview schedule, but the interviewer can probe, follow up and change the orderBalances comparability and depth; the most widely used typeHarder to compare across participants; more room for interviewer effects
    Focus groupA small group discussion led by a moderator, where participants respond to each other as well as to the moderator. In IB class practicals, groups have three to eight participants.Shows shared views, group norms and how people build ideas togetherOne person may dominate; people may hold back or follow the group in front of peers

    Unstructured interviews, with no set schedule at all, are used in some research, but the IB lists only structured, semi-structured and focus group interviews for class practicals. A good interview schedule moves from engagement questions (easy, general openers) to exploration questions (the main topic, with probes) to exit questions (anything else the participant wants to add).

    Interviews are usually audio-recorded with consent and transcribed, then analysed with thematic analysis or content analysis: coding the transcripts and grouping the codes into themes (CRM-09). Biases specific to interviews include interviewer effects (the interviewer's reactions influence answers), and in focus groups a dominant participant or pressure to agree. Interviewer training, a piloted schedule and reflexivity help to reduce them.

    AspectSurvey / questionnaireInterview / focus group
    Similarity: self-reportDepends on what participants say about themselvesAlso depends on what participants say
    Similarity: ethicsNeeds informed consent, anonymity and the right to withdrawNeeds the same, plus consent to record
    Similarity: biasSocial desirability and leading questionsSocial desirability and leading questions, plus interviewer effects
    DataMainly quantitative; open questions add some qualitative dataMainly qualitative; analysed by coding into themes

    Key study: Hofstede's survey of IBM employees

    AimTo compare work-related values across many countries.
    ProcedureBetween 1967 and 1973, more than 100,000 questionnaires were collected from employees of the multinational company IBM in many countries. Items asked about values at work, such as how people relate to managers and how far they prefer clear rules.
    FindingsAnswers were averaged by country and analysed for patterns, producing four original dimensions on which national cultures differed, including power distance and individualism. Later work added two more.
    Why a surveyOnly a standardised questionnaire could collect comparable answers from so many people in so many countries. The same items, translated, made the comparison possible.
    Use it forThis chapter (large-scale surveys); cultural dimensions (HR-04); the etic approach.
    Evaluate it withBias: all participants worked for one company, mostly in similar jobs, so they may not represent their countries. Measurement: do translated questions mean the same thing in every language? Perspective: applying the same measure everywhere is an etic approach, which may miss values that matter within a particular culture.

    Real life: asking people questions

    Real life
    • Customer feedback. A UAE government service asks you to rate your visit from one to five stars after you finish. Who tends to answer, and what does that do to the average rating?
    • School surveys. A student well-being survey is more honest when it is anonymous and completed on a device rather than handed to a teacher.
    • Focus groups in design. Companies ask small groups to discuss a new product before launch, because the discussion reveals reasons that a rating scale cannot.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to surveys and interviews
    BiasSocial desirability, acquiescence, leading questions, interviewer effects and who chooses to respond can all push answers in one direction.
    MeasurementSelf-report measures what people say, which may differ from what they do. Item wording, scale design and coding decisions all affect validity.
    PerspectiveInterviews capture people's own meanings and experiences; surveys impose the researcher's categories. Each gives a different view of the same topic.
    CausalitySurveys usually show relationships between variables, not causes. A link between phone use and focus does not show which causes which.

    Practice study

    This is a short practice study to learn the method. It is not one of your class practicals for Paper 2. Your class practical for the survey is in the Paper 2 page.

    Aim: to investigate students' self-reported social media habits and how they think these relate to their focus when studying. The questionnaire is anonymous and completed in class.

    1. Closed: How many hours a day do you spend on social media on a typical school day? Under 1 / 1 to 3 / 3 to 5 / more than 5
    2. Rating scale: When you sit down to study, how often do you check your phone in the first 15 minutes? Never / Rarely / Sometimes / Often / Almost always
    3. Rating scale: "Using social media in study breaks helps me refocus." Strongly disagree to strongly agree
    4. Rating scale: "I find it hard to concentrate when my phone is nearby." Strongly disagree to strongly agree
    5. Open: Describe one strategy you use to manage distractions while studying.

    Complete it anonymously on paper. Your teacher will tally the class results.

    • Most common answer to item 2

    Paper 2, Section B warm-up [6 marks]

    Study (invented for practice): 247 students aged 14 to 18 in three schools completed an anonymous questionnaire in class, with a teacher in the room. One item asked about daily social media use in four categories (under 1 hour, 1 to 3, 3 to 5, more than 5). A validated 10-item scale measured academic self-efficacy, with statements such as "I am confident I can complete my schoolwork successfully." There was a moderate negative correlation (r = -0.52): students reporting more social media use tended to report lower self-efficacy. The researchers concluded that reducing social media use may improve teenagers' academic self-efficacy.

    Using AI to learn

    Make AI question you, not write for you

    Try one of these after you have attempted the chapter yourself:

    • "Here are five questionnaire items I wrote. For each, tell me whether it is leading, double-barrelled or unclear, and ask me how I would fix it. Do not fix it for me."
    • "Act as a participant in a semi-structured interview about how students prepare for exams. Give short answers so that I have to practise asking probes."
    • "Ask me to compare a survey and an interview on five aspects, one at a time, and tell me if I have given a similarity or a difference."

    Remember: check every study an AI tool names against this chapter or your textbook, because these tools sometimes invent studies and figures. Never paste in your class data or anyone's name, and do not use AI to draft or mark your IA or EE.
