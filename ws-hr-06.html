    Social learning in a nutshell

    Social learning theory

    The basic idea of social learning theory is observational learning: people can learn new behaviours by watching others, without being rewarded or punished for those behaviours themselves. This is how people learn practical skills, such as cooking a family recipe, and social behaviours, such as cooperating, arguing and helping.

    Social learning theory is covered in full in Cognition and Learning, Chapter 02, so this chapter does not repeat it. Here we look at how the theory has been applied to group behaviour in three areas: aggression, prosocial behaviour and behaviour on social networks.

    Bandura's Bobo doll experiments marked the start of the theory. Children who watched an adult model act aggressively towards an inflatable doll went on to imitate that aggression. Two details matter for this chapter.

    • Identification with the model. Children were more likely to imitate a model they identified with. Boys, for example, imitated a male model's physical aggression more than a female model's.
    • Generalisation. Children did not only copy the exact acts they had seen. Some also showed aggressive acts that the model had never performed.

    The theory describes mediating variables: things that stand between watching and doing, and decide whether an observed behaviour is copied. Two of them are identification with the model and self-efficacy, a person's belief that they can successfully perform the behaviour.

    In an exam you may be asked to explain the theory with one example (Paper 1 Section A) or to apply it to a new situation (Section B).

    Aggression and violent video games

    After Bandura's studies, researchers asked many follow-up questions. Can children learn aggression from television characters as well as live models? From video games? Do the effects last? For a long time, many researchers concluded that violent games lead young people to learn aggressive behaviour. Yet the scientific picture is inconsistent, and three limitations of the research help explain why.

    1. Correlational designs. Many studies were correlational, so the direction of the link is unclear. Do violent games make people aggressive, or do people who are already aggressive choose violent games? This is called bidirectional ambiguity.
    2. Short exposure. In laboratory experiments, participants usually played for only a short time: from four minutes to two hours, with a median of 15 minutes.
    3. Immediate measurement. Effects were usually measured straight after playing, so the studies say little about whether any effect lasts.

    Key study 1: Kühn et al. (2019), violent video games over two months

    AimTo test whether playing a violent video game every day for two months causes changes in aggression and related behaviour in adults, and whether any changes are still present two months later.
    Participants90 adults (48 women), mean age 28, who had played little or no video games in the previous six months.
    ProcedureA randomised controlled trial. Participants were randomly allocated to one of three groups: a violent game group, who played a violent action game; an active control group, who played a non-violent life-simulation game; and a passive control group, who played no games during the study. The two gaming groups were given a games console and asked to play for about 30 minutes a day for two months. Everyone was tested three times: before the study began, straight after the two months, and two months after that, to check for delayed effects. At each session the researchers measured 52 outcome variables, including aggression, hostility, impulsivity, empathy, mood and anxiety, using questionnaires and computer-based behavioural tasks.
    FindingsPlaying the violent game had no effect on any of the measures. Only 3 of the 208 statistical comparisons gave a significant result in the predicted direction. With that many comparisons, about 10 significant results would be expected by chance alone.
    ConclusionTwo months of daily violent gameplay had no detrimental effects on adult non-gamers. The authors suggested that effects found in earlier studies may have been real but very short-lived, since those studies used brief play and measured behaviour immediately.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section C on causality, change, measurement or responsibility; Paper 2 Section B practice (a randomised controlled trial with two control groups).
    Evaluate it withCausality: random allocation and an active control group mean that any difference could be put down to the violence of the game, not to gaming in general. Measurement: 52 variables at three sessions is very thorough, but making so many comparisons increases the chance of false positive results, which the authors took into account. Bias: the participants were adults who did not normally play games, and effects may be stronger in adolescence. Change: the study lasted far longer than earlier experiments and included a follow-up, but the game was a different style from those used in earlier studies, so the results are not directly comparable.

    Quick lens: causality. Studies on violent games disagree about cause and effect. Keep these questions open. You will come back to them in the Section C plan.

    • In a correlational study, could the arrow run the other way? What would that mean for the claim that games cause aggression?
    • Why did Kühn et al. add a group that played a non-violent game, as well as a group that played nothing?
    • If the effect of watching depends on mediating variables, such as identification with the model, can one study of one kind of participant tell us what games do to everyone?

    Prosocial behaviour: can television teach kindness?

    If watching can teach aggression, can it also teach kindness? Prosocial behaviour is behaviour that benefits other people, such as sharing, helping and cooperating. Three studies tested the idea.

    StudyWhat was doneWhat was found
    Bryan and Walbek (1970)Children watched a filmed model play a game and win tokens. The model then either gave some of the tokens to charity or kept them all. The children then played the game themselves and had the chance to donate.Children who had watched the generous model donated more than children who had watched the selfish model.
    Stein and Friedrich (1972)Nursery-age children were observed, then assigned to watch aggressive cartoons, neutral films or a prosocial programme (Mister Rogers' Neighborhood) regularly over several weeks. Their behaviour in nursery was then observed again.Aggressive cartoons increased aggression, but only in children who were already more aggressive than average. The prosocial programme increased prosocial behaviour towards others mainly in children from low-income families.
    Friedrich-Cofer et al. (1979)Children from low-income urban families in preschool programmes watched episodes of the same prosocial programme over eight weeks. Some only watched; others also took part in classroom activities, such as role-play and talking about the programme's themes.Watching alone produced few changes in behaviour. Watching combined with role-play and discussion led to significant increases in positive social behaviour.

    Two ideas follow. First, the effects of television depend on many mediating variables, such as who is watching, so asking whether television has "positive" or "negative" effects in general may be the wrong question. Second, watching alone does not seem to be enough. Encouraging children to apply what they see to their own behaviour makes the difference. That is what the tutor of 7C was doing in the warm-up.

    Key study 2: Brady et al. (2021), learning outrage on a social network

    Moral outrage is a strong emotion felt when someone believes another person has broken a moral norm. When it is expressed in public, it can put pressure on the person who broke the norm and strengthen the group's norms. It can also spread hostility and make people less willing to speak freely. Brady and colleagues asked how people learn to express outrage online.

    AimTo separate two kinds of learning in the expression of moral outrage online: learning the norm by observing what others post (social learning), and learning from feedback such as likes (reinforcement learning).
    Participants120 adults recruited online for one experiment in the paper (a second, similar experiment also used 120 participants).
    ProcedureParticipants used a simulated social media platform. They were randomly allocated to an outrage norm condition, in which 75% of the 12 posts in their newsfeed expressed moral outrage, or a neutral norm condition, in which none did. After scrolling through the feed, they completed 30 trials. In each trial they saw two posts on the same topic, one with outrage and one without, and chose one to share. After each choice they received feedback in the form of likes. In both conditions, outrage posts received more likes on average.
    FindingsOn the first trial, participants in the outrage norm condition were much more likely to choose the outrage post, and participants in the neutral norm condition were more likely to choose the neutral post. This shows social learning of the norm from the feed alone. Over the trials, both groups chose outrage posts more often as the likes came in, which shows reinforcement learning. However, the reinforcement effect was smaller in the outrage norm group.
    ConclusionPeople learn how to express outrage both by observing the norm in their network and from social feedback. Once people have learned a norm by watching, they are less affected by feedback.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section B (social learning of norms in an online group); Paper 1 Section C on change, measurement or responsibility.
    Evaluate it withCausality: random allocation to the two feeds means the difference on the first trial can be put down to the norm participants observed. Measurement: social learning and reinforcement learning were measured at different points (the first trial and the change across trials), which is a clever way to separate them. Bias: a simulated platform with chosen posts is not the same as a real network, where people choose whom to follow. Responsibility: if platforms reward outrage with visibility, the people who design them share responsibility for the norms that spread.

    There is a loop here. On social networks, people tend to end up among others who share their views. The norms they learn affect whom they choose to follow, which then strengthens those norms through further observation.

    What the evidence means for changing behaviour

    Social learning is a powerful tool for change. It has been used to build programmes that teach behaviour by showing others behaving that way, and it helps explain how norms spread online. But any programme should rest on solid experimental evidence of a clear link between cause and effect.

    What the evidence showedWhat it means for a programme
    Television alone changed little; television with class activities changed prosocial behaviour (Friedrich-Cofer et al., 1979).Showing a model is not enough. Add practice.
    Effects depended on who was watching: how aggressive the children already were, and their family background (Stein and Friedrich, 1972).Test who the programme works for before assuming it works for everyone.
    Effects in many studies were measured within minutes and may be short-lived.Measure over weeks and months, not only straight afterwards.
    Many studies on games and aggression were correlational, and a long experiment found no effect (Kühn et al., 2019).Do not ban or promote anything until you know it produces the outcome you fear or hope for.

    The last row is a question of responsibility. Before banning a video game, we need good reason to believe it causes harm in our own context. Researchers who study aggression or outrage need the approval of an ethics committee, and technology that makes public outrage easy should be weighed for the norms it may create and the misinformation it may spread.

    Real life: the pile-on

    A student posts an angry comment about a classmate in the class group chat. A few people reply with angry emojis. The first comment collects a lot of likes. Next time, the comments are a little harsher. Nobody sat down and decided that this is how the chat would talk.

    In the UAE this is not only a matter of manners. Under Federal Decree-Law No. 34 of 2021 on Combatting Rumours and Cybercrimes, insulting people online and spreading rumours can be criminal offences.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to social learning in groups
    ChangeModels can change group behaviour, but the prosocial studies suggest that watching alone changes little; practice, and the right audience, make change more likely and more lasting.
    CausalityCorrelational studies of games cannot show which way the arrow runs; randomised experiments such as Kühn et al. (2019) and Brady et al. (2021) can support causal claims, but only for the people and settings they studied.
    MeasurementAggression and prosocial behaviour are measured through questionnaires, tasks and observation; many measures mean many comparisons, so some significant results will appear by chance.
    BiasShort laboratory exposure, immediate measurement and narrow samples (young children, adult non-gamers, online volunteers) may have shaped the conclusions.
    PerspectiveSocial learning explains behaviour through models and norms; temperament, family background and the design of online platforms offer other perspectives on the same behaviour.
    ResponsibilityPolicymakers should act on solid evidence before banning or promoting media; platform designers and group members share responsibility for the norms they model and reward.

    HL extension

    Data analysis: too many tests

    Kühn et al. (2019) made 208 statistical comparisons. Researchers usually call a result significant when p < 0.05. This means that, if there were no real effect, a result like it would turn up by chance less than 5% of the time. Finding a significant result that is not real is called a Type I error. With 208 tests, about 10.4 would be expected to come out significant by chance alone.

    Here is a small example, invented for practice. A researcher tests a school's kindness programme on 40 different outcome measures and finds that 2 of them are significant at p < 0.05.

    How many significant results would you expect by chance alone? What does that mean for the researcher's claim that the programme works? [3]
