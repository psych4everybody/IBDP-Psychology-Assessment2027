    Two pictures of development: a staircase or a ramp?

    When a child gets better at thinking, what kind of change is happening? There are two broad answers.

    A stage theory says development happens in a series of distinct steps, like a staircase. Each stage is a different kind of thinking, not just more of the same. This is called qualitative change. Stage theories usually make three claims: the stages come in a fixed order (an invariant sequence), no stage can be skipped, and every child in every culture goes through the same stages (they are universal). The ages attached to each stage are averages, not deadlines.

    A continuous model says development is gradual, like walking up a ramp. A child's thinking gets faster, holds more information and uses better strategies, but it does not switch into a new kind of thinking. This is called quantitative change: a change in amount rather than in kind.

    A caterpillar does not become a butterfly by getting bigger; it changes its whole body, which is like a stage. A pine tree keeps the same roots, stem and needles as it grows taller, which is like a continuous model. In the warm-up, if you said Saeed "thinks differently" from Noor, you were taking a stage view. If you said he "knows more and pays attention to more", you were taking a continuous view.

    Piaget's building blocks: schemas, assimilation and accommodation

    Schema theory

    Jean Piaget (1896 to 1980) was a Swiss psychologist who spent decades watching and questioning children, including his own three. He saw the child as a "little scientist" who builds knowledge by acting on the world and fixing ideas when they fail.

    The basic unit of knowledge in Piaget's theory is the schema: a mental framework that organises what we know about an object, an action or an idea. A baby has a schema for grasping; a toddler has one for "dog". Schemas change in two ways.

    • Assimilation means fitting new information into a schema you already have. A child who has only seen horses sees a camel at the desert farm and calls it a "lumpy horse". The camel has been assimilated into the horse schema.
    • Accommodation means changing a schema, or making a new one, because the new information does not fit. When the child learns that camels store fat in their humps, can go a long time without drinking and are not ridden in the same way, a separate "camel" schema forms.

    Piaget argued that children are driven to keep their schemas in balance with their experience. He called this equilibration. When something does not fit (disequilibrium), the child feels puzzled and has to accommodate. Assimilation, accommodation and equilibration happen at every age, so they are the continuous part of Piaget's theory. The stages describe the bigger shifts in thinking that these small changes add up to.

    Piaget's four stages of cognitive development

    Piaget proposed that all children pass through four stages in the same order. Each stage is a different way of understanding the world.

    StageApproximate ageWhat thinking is likeKey milestone or limitation
    1. Sensorimotor0 to 2 yearsThe infant understands the world through senses and actions: looking, sucking, grasping, shaking.Object permanence: knowing that a toy still exists when it is hidden under a blanket. Piaget thought this develops through the first two years.
    2. Pre-operational2 to 7 yearsThe child uses words and images to stand for things (symbolic thought) and enjoys pretend play, but does not yet reason logically.Egocentrism: difficulty seeing a situation from another person's point of view. Centration: focusing on one feature, such as height, and ignoring others, so the child fails conservation tasks.
    3. Concrete operational7 to 11 yearsThe child can reason logically about real, physical objects and events.Conservation: understanding that an amount stays the same when its appearance changes. Reversibility: mentally "pouring the juice back". Abstract and hypothetical ideas are still hard.
    4. Formal operational11 years and overThe young person can reason about abstract ideas and hypothetical situations ("What would happen if ...?").Hypothetical-deductive reasoning: forming a hypothesis and testing it step by step, as in algebra or designing an experiment.

    Go back to the warm-up. Noor, at four, is pre-operational. She centres on the height of the juice and says the tall glass has more. Saeed, at nine, is concrete operational. He can conserve: he knows the amount is the same, because nothing was added or taken away and the juice could be poured back. For Piaget, Saeed does not just know one more fact. He has a different set of mental operations.

    Key study 1: Piaget and Inhelder (1956), the three mountains task

    AimTo find out at what age children can work out how a scene looks from a viewpoint other than their own, and so to measure egocentrism.
    ParticipantsSwiss children aged 4 to 12 (usually reported as 100 children). The study is described in Piaget and Inhelder's book The Child's Conception of Space (English edition 1956), not in a journal article.
    ProcedureEach child sat at a table in front of a model of three mountains of different sizes. One had snow on top, one had a house and one had a red cross. The child could walk around the model first. A small doll was then placed at different positions around it. The child was asked to show what the doll could see in three ways: by arranging cardboard shapes of the mountains to match the doll's view, by choosing the doll's view from a set of ten pictures, and by placing the doll where it would see the view shown in a given picture.
    FindingsFour-year-olds usually chose the picture of their own view, as if the doll saw what they saw. Six-year-olds showed some awareness that the doll's view was different, but often chose the wrong picture. Only from about seven to eight years did children choose the doll's view consistently.
    ConclusionYoung children in the pre-operational stage are egocentric: they find it hard to separate their own viewpoint from someone else's. The ability to decentre appears with the move into the concrete operational stage.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example for schema theory or cognitive models); Paper 1 Section B; Paper 1 Section C on measurement or perspective.
    Evaluate it withMeasurement: the task asks children to match a 3D scene to flat pictures and to remember left and right from another position, so a wrong answer may show difficulty with the task rather than egocentrism. Bias: all the children were from one Swiss setting, so the ages may not generalise to other cultures. Change: the study compared different children at different ages (a cross-sectional design), so it did not follow any child as their thinking changed.

    Continuous models: development as gradual growth

    Continuous models agree that children's thinking improves a great deal, but they explain it as steady growth rather than a series of jumps.

    Information processing models compare the mind to a system that takes in, stores and uses information. On this view, children have the same basic processes as adults (attention, memory, reasoning) from early on. What changes is the amount: working memory holds more, processing gets faster, and children learn better strategies. Noor may fail the juice task because she cannot yet hold height and width in mind at the same time, not because she has a different kind of mind. Robert Siegler (1996) added that children often use several strategies at the same time, and the better ones slowly become more common. He described this as "overlapping waves" rather than a staircase.

    Lev Vygotsky (1896 to 1934) was a Russian psychologist who lived at the same time as Piaget. His sociocultural theory says that thinking develops through social interaction and the cultural tools a child grows up with, especially language. Three ideas matter for this chapter. Chapter 3 goes deeper into sociocultural factors.

    • The zone of proximal development (ZPD) is the gap between what a child can do alone and what the same child can do with guidance from an adult or a more capable peer. Learning that happens inside this zone moves development forward.
    • The more knowledgeable other (MKO) is anyone who can do the task better than the child: a parent, a teacher, an older cousin or a classmate. The term was added by later writers to sum up Vygotsky's idea.
    • Scaffolding is the support the MKO gives, which is reduced step by step as the child becomes able to do the task alone. The word comes from Wood, Bruner and Ross (1976), who built on Vygotsky's ideas.

    Vygotsky is often placed on the "continuous" side because, for him, development depends on the support and culture around the child rather than a fixed timetable inside the child. Be careful with this label in an essay. Vygotsky did write about periods of childhood and the "crises" between them, so his theory is not purely continuous. The safest claim is that he saw the mechanism of development as gradual and social.

    FeaturePiaget (stage theory)Vygotsky (sociocultural)Information processing (continuous)
    Type of changeQualitative: new kinds of thinking at each stageMostly gradual, driven by social learningQuantitative: more capacity, speed and better strategies
    Main driverThe child acting on the world, plus maturationInteraction with others and cultural tools such as languageGrowth in memory, attention and practice
    Role of other peopleMainly to create puzzles that trigger accommodationCentral: learning happens with a more knowledgeable otherTeaching and practice build strategies
    Universal?Yes, the same stages in the same order everywhereNo, development depends on cultureBasic processes are universal; strategies vary

    Real-life examples

    • Juice, dates and fair shares. At a family gathering in Al Ain, a four-year-old insists that her brother got more juice because his glass is taller, or that her cousin has more dates because his are spread out on the plate. This is centration. Older children at the same table laugh, because they can already conserve.
    • Homework with an older sister. Hamdan, aged six, is learning to read Arabic. His older sister covers part of each word, sounds out the first letter with him and asks what comes next. Each week she helps a little less. She is acting as a more knowledgeable other and scaffolding inside his zone of proximal development.
    • From counters to algebra. In many primary classrooms in Dubai and Abu Dhabi, young pupils learn maths with counters, blocks and drawings before they move to written sums, and algebra with letters comes later in secondary school. This order matches Piaget's move from concrete to formal operations. A continuous model would explain it as gradually building the memory and strategies that abstract maths needs.

    Key study 2: Hughes (1975), the policeman doll study

    AimTo test whether young children can take another person's viewpoint when the task makes sense to them, unlike the three mountains task.
    ParticipantsChildren aged three and a half to five years. The study was part of Martin Hughes's unpublished PhD thesis at the University of Edinburgh. It is best known from Margaret Donaldson's book Children's Minds (1978), which reported it, so some details, such as the exact sample, are not widely available.
    ProcedureTwo low walls crossed to make four sections, like a plus sign. First, one policeman doll was placed at the end of a wall, and the child was asked to hide a boy doll where the policeman could not see him. Hughes checked that each child understood the task and explained any mistakes before testing. Then a second policeman was added, and the child had to hide the boy from both policemen. Only one section was hidden from both of them, so the child had to think about two other viewpoints at once.
    FindingsIn the two-policeman task, about 90% of the children's answers were correct. In a harder version with more walls and a third policeman, about 90% of four-year-olds still succeeded.
    ConclusionChildren much younger than seven can take another person's viewpoint when the task makes "human sense" to them. Donaldson argued that hiding from a policeman is a situation children understand, while matching a doll's view of mountains to a picture is not.
    What happened nextOther studies found the same pattern. Borke (1975) used a familiar farm scene and a well-known children's TV character, and found that even three-year-olds could often work out the character's view. Later researchers, such as John Flavell, pointed out that the two tasks test different things. Knowing whether someone can see an object (Hughes) is easier than knowing how a scene looks from their position (Piaget). So the studies may both be right about different skills.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section C on measurement or perspective; Paper 1 Section B (questioning the ages in Piaget's stages); Paper 2 Section B practice (how task design affects results).
    Evaluate it withMeasurement: the practice and corrections before testing may have helped children succeed, which is closer to scaffolding than to measuring what they could already do alone. Bias: the study was never published in a peer-reviewed journal, so it was not checked in the usual way. Perspective: the researcher decides what "makes sense" to a child, and that depends on the child's culture and experience.

    How effective are stage theories and continuous models?

    The learning objective asks how effective these theories are. A theory is effective if it describes development accurately, explains why change happens, makes predictions we can test, and is useful in real life, for example in schools. Use these four tests when you evaluate.

    Strengths of stage theories. Piaget's theory describes a clear, testable sequence, and when his tasks, such as conservation, are repeated in many countries, children usually pass them in a similar order, even if the ages vary. It changed how teachers see children, as active learners rather than empty containers, and supports hands-on learning in the early years.

    Limitations of stage theories. Piaget seems to have underestimated young children. Hughes (1975) showed perspective-taking well before seven, and McGarrigle and Donaldson (1974) found that more four- to six-year-olds could conserve when a "naughty teddy" messed up the counters by accident rather than when an adult changed them on purpose. Baillargeon, Spelke and Wasserman (1985) found that five-month-old infants looked longer at a physically impossible event involving a hidden object, which suggests some object knowledge far earlier than Piaget expected. Cross-cultural research found that many adults do not show formal operational reasoning on Piaget's science tasks, and Piaget (1972) himself later suggested that people may reach formal operations mainly in areas they know well. If stages depend on culture and familiarity, they are less universal than the theory claims.

    Strengths of continuous models. They explain the uneven, gradual progress that teachers see every day: a child can conserve number before volume, or succeed on one day and fail the next. Vygotsky's ZPD gives teachers a clear plan of action: find what a child can do with help, and scaffold it.

    Limitations of continuous models. They can describe how much a child improves without explaining why some changes, such as the start of pretend play or abstract reasoning, seem to appear fairly suddenly. The ZPD is hard to measure precisely, and Vygotsky says less about what the child brings to learning, such as biological maturation.

    A useful conclusion. Whether development looks like a staircase or a ramp often depends on how closely we measure it. Tested once a year with a hard task, children seem to jump from failing to passing. Tested every week with easier tasks, the same change looks gradual. Many psychologists now argue that both views capture something real, and that the best theory depends on the skill being studied.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to stage theories and continuous models
    MeasurementA child's "stage" is inferred from performance on a task, so a confusing task can make a child look less able than they are (three mountains compared with the policeman doll study).
    CausalityAge is linked to success on Piaget's tasks, but age itself does not cause the change. Maturation, schooling, language and practice all grow with age, so it is hard to tell which one is responsible.
    BiasPiaget's early observations included his own children, and his samples came from one European culture, so the ages may not apply to children everywhere.
    ChangeThis is the heart of the chapter: is change qualitative (new kinds of thinking) or quantitative (more of the same)? Cross-sectional studies compare age groups but do not track change within one child.
    PerspectivePiaget takes a cognitive perspective focused on the individual child; Vygotsky takes a sociocultural perspective focused on interaction and culture. The perspective a researcher takes shapes the tasks they design and what they notice.
    ResponsibilityLabelling a child as "not ready" for a stage could lower what teachers expect of them. Researchers and teachers have a responsibility to use stage ages as rough guides, not limits.
