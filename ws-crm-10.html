    How the decisions in a study connect

    Every study is a chain of decisions, and each one limits what the next can achieve. The Foundations chapters each covered one link.

    DecisionThe question to askChapter
    Research question and aimWhat exactly do I want to find out, about whom?CRM-03, CRM-10
    MethodDo I need to test a cause (experiment), describe behaviour (observation), measure how common something is (survey), or understand experiences (interview)?CRM-03 to CRM-06
    SampleWho is my population, and which sampling technique gives me a sample I can justify?CRM-07
    Procedure and measurementHow will I operationalise and record the key variables, and how will I keep things consistent?CRM-03, CRM-05, CRM-08
    EthicsHow will I protect participants, and how might that affect the data?CRM-04
    AnalysisWhich statistics, graphs or themes fit my data?CRM-09
    ConclusionWhat can my design actually support, and for whom?CRM-01, CRM-02, CRM-08

    When you evaluate any study, you are checking these links one by one: is each decision justified, and does the conclusion stay within what the chain can support?

    The IA in brief

    The internal assessment is a research proposal: you design a study to investigate a real-life problem for a population of interest, such as students in your school or a group in your community. You do not collect data. The task is about justifying your decisions.

    FeatureWhat the guide sets
    MethodOne of the four class practical methods: experiment (true or quasi), interview (structured, semi-structured or focus group), observation, or survey/questionnaire
    Length and timeA maximum of 2,200 words, and about 20 hours of class time
    Marks and weighting24 marks across four criteria of 6 marks each; 30% of the final grade at SL and 20% at HL
    Criterion A: IntroductionA focused aim, the problem and its impact on the population, and two relevant studies with clear links to the proposal
    Criterion B: Research methodologyThe method and why it suits the aim; the procedure, including sampling, sample, design, setting and process; ethical considerations specific to the study
    Criterion C: Data collectionOne data collection tool that you create yourself, the decisions behind it, and the challenges you expect. A survey/questionnaire, structured interview or controlled observation needs at least five items. In an experiment, the tool is the procedure and materials used to measure the DV. An existing published survey cannot be your tool, although you may reuse a few published items if you reference them. A copy of the tool goes in the appendix, but everything essential must be in the main text
    Criterion D: DiscussionPotential findings and their implications for policy or practice, your own possible bias, and one other method that would add understanding

    The proposal must be one that an ethics committee would approve. Some topics are not allowed at all, such as abuse or self-harm, and your teacher will advise on topics that are sensitive here in the UAE. Safe, popular directions include sleep and memory in exam season, screen time and attention, learning in a second language, belonging for newly arrived students, and peer influence on study habits.

    Choosing a method

    There is rarely one right method, but there is always a justified one. Match the method to what the question needs:

    If the question asks...A good fitWhat you give up
    Does X cause a change in Y?Experiment (true if you can randomly allocate; quasi if groups already exist)Often realism; sometimes ethics limits what you can manipulate
    What do people actually do in a situation?ObservationThe reasons behind the behaviour; cause and effect
    How common is a behaviour or attitude, and how does it relate to other variables?Survey/questionnaireDepth; accuracy of self-report; cause and effect
    How do people experience something, and what does it mean to them?Interview or focus groupSample size and generalisability; comparability across participants

    Criterion D asks for one other method that would add understanding. The best answers say exactly what the second method would reveal that the first cannot, for example "interviews would explain why students check their phones at night, which the survey only counts". Combining methods to check findings from different angles is triangulation.

    Paper 2 Section B: the full task

    Section B gives you an unseen study and asks you to discuss it with regard to two or more concepts, chosen from at least four listed in the question. It is worth 15 marks; plan for about 5 minutes and write for about 40.

    1. Read the study twice. The second time, underline every detail that raises a concept: the sample, how participants were placed in groups, how variables were measured, who did the analysis, what the conclusion claims.
    2. Choose your concepts. Pick the two (or three) that the details support most richly. Two concepts discussed in depth and two or more discussed for breadth are both acceptable approaches.
    3. Plan each point with the rung 3 moves (CRM-02): the concept and the idea, the exact detail, the consequence for the conclusion, and a link to another concept.
    4. Show concepts interacting. For example, how participants were allocated (causality) and who was sampled (bias) together limit what the conclusion can claim.
    5. End with a conclusion that answers the question and follows from your points, ideally naming a condition (rung 5).

    The marks rise with how well you understand and engage with the concepts in relation to this study, how developed your links are, how accurate your terms are, and how specific your references to the study are.

    Paper 2, Section B warm-up: choose your concepts [6 marks]

    Study (invented for practice): Researchers used semi-structured interviews to explore how students aged 14 to 17 experience comparing themselves with others on social media, and how this affects their confidence. Twelve participants were recruited by snowball sampling, starting with two volunteers from a psychology class. Each was interviewed one-to-one for about 40 minutes. The lead researcher had previously published two studies arguing that social media harms teenagers' self-esteem, and carried out the thematic analysis alone. Four themes were reported: comparing with carefully edited posts, seeking approval through likes, awareness that online lives are not real, and limiting use as a way of coping. The researchers concluded that social media is a major cause of low confidence in teenagers and recommended compulsory media-literacy lessons in all secondary schools. Participants gave written consent and were debriefed. Two participants said during their interviews that they felt the questions were steering them towards particular answers.

    Evaluate this study with reference to two concepts of your choice from: bias, causality, change, measurement, perspective and responsibility. [6]

    Spend a minute deciding which two concepts the details support most richly. The choice is part of the task.

    Paper 2, Section B [15 marks]

    Study (invented for practice): screen time, sleep and memory

    Researchers wanted to know whether reducing evening screen time improves memory in teenagers. They invited all Grade 10 students at two secondary schools in Dubai to take part; 64 volunteered, with written consent from their parents. Students at School A (n = 33) joined a four-week programme in which they agreed to stop using phones and tablets one hour before bed. Students at School B (n = 31) carried on as normal. Students were allocated by school, not as individuals.

    Each morning, students recorded their screen use and hours of sleep in an online diary. At the start and end of the four weeks, all students took a 20-word free recall test in their classroom, supervised by their own teachers, who knew which school was in the programme. Six students in the programme group stopped completing their diaries and were removed from the analysis, leaving 27.

    The programme group's mean recall rose from 11.2 to 13.1 words. The comparison group's mean rose from 11.4 to 11.9 words. The researchers concluded that limiting screen use before bed improves memory, and recommended that all UAE schools introduce the programme.

    Discuss this study with regard to two or more of the following concepts: bias, causality, measurement, responsibility. [15]

    Plan for about 5 minutes: for each concept, the detail you will use, the point you will make and a link to another concept. Then write for about 40 minutes, ending with a conclusion that follows from your points.

    • Concept 1: detail, point, link
    • Concept 2: detail, point, link
    • Concept 3 (optional)
    • Conclusion in one sentence

    Using AI to learn

    Make AI question you, not write for you

    Try one of these after you have attempted the chapter yourself:

    • "I am designing an IA proposal on sleep and memory in Grade 12 students. Ask me one question at a time about my aim, method, sample, tool and ethics, the way a supervisor would. Do not suggest answers."
    • "Here is my Paper 2 Section B plan. Tell me which concept is best supported by specific details and which is weakest. Do not rewrite it."
    • "Give me a short invented study and ask me which two concepts I would choose to evaluate it, and why."

    Remember: check every study an AI tool names against this chapter or your textbook, because these tools sometimes invent studies and figures. Never paste in your class data or anyone's name, and do not use AI to draft or mark your IA or EE.
