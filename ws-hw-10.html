    What exactly are we preventing or treating?

    "Social media addiction" is not an official diagnosis. It is not listed as a disorder in the DSM-5. The closest condition in the DSM-5 is internet gaming disorder, and even that is placed in a section for conditions that need more research. Psychologists who study the problem usually call it problematic social media use and treat it as a possible behavioural addiction: a pattern of behaviour that looks like addiction even though no substance is involved.

    Heavy use is not the same as problematic use. Use becomes problematic when it shows the features of addiction. Researchers often describe six:

    • Salience: social media dominates the person's thoughts.
    • Mood modification: it is used to escape stress or low mood.
    • Tolerance: more and more time is needed to get the same effect.
    • Withdrawal: the person feels restless or irritable when they cannot use it.
    • Conflict: use causes problems with sleep, school work or relationships.
    • Relapse: attempts to cut down fail, and use returns to the old level.

    Questionnaires such as the Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale ask one question about each feature. If researchers cannot agree on what the problem is, they will find it hard to agree on whether a strategy has fixed it.

    Prevention and treatment

    Prevention aims to stop a health problem from developing in the first place. It is often universal, meaning it is given to everyone in a group (for example, a whole year group at school), whether or not they have a problem. Treatment addresses a problem that has already developed, and is given to individuals who are struggling. In practice the line is blurry: a time limit can prevent problems in a 12-year-old with a first phone, or help treat a 17-year-old who already cannot stop.

    Many strategies build on earlier chapters. Platforms reward use on an unpredictable schedule (a like, a reply, a new video), which is operant conditioning. People copy the phone habits around them, which is social learning. Programmes that break "use my phone less" into small weekly targets build self-efficacy, the belief that you can carry out the behaviour.

    Prevention strategies

    Digital literacy. Digital literacy programmes teach young people how platforms are designed to hold attention. Features such as infinite scroll, autoplay, streaks and notifications are not accidents. Once students understand that a notification is a variable reward, they may notice the pull and choose whether to follow it. The aim is to help them use social media on their own terms, not to frighten them away from it.

    School and family approaches. Schools can set phone-free times and teach skills for managing use. Families can agree shared rules, such as no phones at dinner or phones charged outside bedrooms at night. Because children learn by observation, these rules work better when parents follow them too.

    Design changes. Some strategies target the platforms rather than the users. Phones now include built-in screen time reports and app limits. Some platforms let users hide like counts, turn off autoplay or set reminders to take a break. These changes put some of the responsibility on the companies, but companies earn money from attention, so default settings rarely encourage less use.

    Limiting use. The simplest strategy is a daily time limit, used as prevention or as part of treatment. For most young people limiting is more realistic than quitting, because social media is also how they talk to friends, family and classmates. Key study 1 tests whether it works.

    Treatment strategies

    CBT-based approaches. Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) has been adapted for problematic internet and social media use. The cognitive part targets the thoughts that keep the habit going, such as "If I do not check now, I will miss something important" or "Scrolling is the only way I can relax." The person learns to test these thoughts against evidence. The behavioural part changes the environment and routine. Examples include turning off notifications, moving apps off the home screen, keeping the phone out of the bedroom, planning other activities for high-use times, and logging when and why the urge appears. Treatment usually ends with relapse prevention: planning what to do in situations, such as exam stress, where old habits may return.

    Abstinence or reduction? One treatment goal is abstinence: stopping use completely, for example by deactivating an account. Another is reduction: keeping social media but using it less and more deliberately. Abstinence is clear and easy to check, but it can cut people off from friends and information. Reduction fits real life better, because the goal is healthy use rather than no use, in the same way that the goal for a person who overeats is healthy eating, not giving up food. Key study 2 tests what happens when people stop completely for four weeks.

    Why is it so hard to cut down? Cognitive dissonance

    Cognitive dissonance

    Cognitive dissonance is the uncomfortable feeling a person has when they hold two cognitions (beliefs, attitudes or knowledge about their own behaviour) that do not fit together. The theory was proposed by Leon Festinger in 1957. It says that people are motivated to reduce this discomfort, and they can do it in two main ways: change the behaviour, or change the cognitions so that the behaviour seems acceptable.

    Think about Hamdan in the warm-up. He holds two cognitions that clash: "I want to spend less time on my phone because it is hurting my revision" and "I spent four hours on my phone last night." Changing the behaviour is hard, because scrolling is reinforced every time. So he changes the cognitions instead. "Everyone in my class does it" makes the behaviour seem normal, and "It helps me relax after football" makes it seem useful. The dissonance goes down, and so does the pressure to change.

    This helps explain why people keep using apps they say they want to cut down on. It also suggests how strategies might work. Self-monitoring, such as checking a weekly screen time report, makes it harder to tell yourself "I only use it a bit" when the phone says four hours a day. A good strategy makes the excuses harder to believe and the change easier to make.

    How do we know if a strategy works?

    To judge effectiveness, researchers need to know what changed, by how much, and for how long.

    What is measuredHowWhat it tells usA problem with it
    Time spentScreen time reports or phone screenshotsObjective data on how much people used each appLess time does not always mean less problematic use; people can move to other apps.
    Addiction featuresQuestionnaires such as the Bergen Social Media Addiction ScaleWhether features such as withdrawal and conflict have reducedSelf-report; people may give the answer they think the researcher wants.

    Length of effectFollow-up weeks or months laterWhether the change lastsMost studies stop after a few weeks.

    The strongest design for testing effectiveness is a randomised controlled trial, where participants are randomly allocated to the strategy or to a control group. Random allocation means that differences at the end are likely to be caused by the strategy, not by the type of person who chose it. A systematic review by King, Delfabbro, Griffiths and Gradisar (2011) checked the quality of eight published treatment trials for internet addiction. They found four main weaknesses: researchers defined and diagnosed internet addiction in different ways, randomisation and blinding were rarely used, control or comparison groups were often inadequate, and important details such as sample characteristics and effect sizes were often missing.

    A moving target

    In this area of study the thing being treated keeps changing. Platforms redesign their apps every year, and new apps appear while old ones fade. Hunt et al. (2018) studied Facebook, Instagram and Snapchat; apps that later became popular, such as TikTok, were not included. Allcott et al. (2020) studied Facebook in the USA in 2018. A study can be well designed and still tell us little about the problem students face now. When you evaluate a study in this chapter, always ask: which platforms, which year, and which features?

    Real-life examples

    • Phone-free family time. A family in Abu Dhabi agrees that all phones, including the parents' phones, go in a basket during dinner and for an hour before bed. This uses social learning: the children see their parents keep the rule.
    • Screen time reports. A student in Al Ain turns on her phone's weekly screen time report and is shocked by the total. The number makes "I hardly use it" harder to believe.
    • Exam season limits. A student in Dubai sets a 30-minute daily limit on two apps during mock exams but keeps messaging apps for family and teammates. This is reduction, not abstinence.

    Key study 1: Hunt, Marx, Lipson and Young (2018), limiting social media use

    AimTo test whether limiting social media use causes a change in well-being, including loneliness and depression, using an experiment with objective tracking of use rather than only self-report.
    Participants143 undergraduates at the University of Pennsylvania, USA, aged 18 to 22.
    ProcedureAfter one baseline week of monitoring and well-being questionnaires, participants were randomly allocated to one of two conditions for three weeks. The experimental group limited Facebook, Instagram and Snapchat to 10 minutes per platform per day, which is 30 minutes a day in total. The control group used social media as usual. Use was tracked objectively: participants sent screenshots of their iPhone battery screens, which show how long each app was actively used. Well-being was measured with seven outcome measures, including fear of missing out (FOMO), anxiety, depression and loneliness.
    FindingsThe limited group showed significant reductions in loneliness and depression over the three weeks, compared with the control group. The benefits were strongest for participants who were more depressed at the start. Both groups showed significant decreases in anxiety and FOMO compared with baseline.
    ConclusionLimiting social media use to about 30 minutes a day may lead to better well-being. Because the control group also improved on anxiety and FOMO, simply monitoring your own use may have some benefit.
    What happened nextFaulhaber et al. (2023), at Iowa State University, tested a similar strategy with 230 college students. The limited group was asked to keep social media to 30 minutes a day for two weeks and received daily reminders. Compared with the control group, they had lower anxiety, depression, loneliness and FOMO, and more positive feelings. Benefits were found even for participants who sometimes went over the limit.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section B (limiting use as a strategy); Paper 1 Section C on causality, measurement or change; Paper 2 Section B (a true experiment with objective measurement of the IV).
    Evaluate it withCausality: random allocation to a limited group and a control group supports the claim that limiting use caused the fall in loneliness and depression. Measurement: battery screenshots gave objective data on use, but well-being was still self-reported. Change: the study lasted three weeks and covered three platforms, so it cannot show whether the change lasts or applies to newer apps.

    Key study 2: Allcott, Braghieri, Eichmeyer and Gentzkow (2020), deactivating Facebook

    AimTo measure the effects of stopping social media use completely for four weeks on time use, knowledge of news, well-being and later use of Facebook.
    ParticipantsUS adult Facebook users recruited through adverts on Facebook in September and October 2018. The main sample was 1,661 people: 580 in the deactivation group and 1,081 in the control group. Participants were younger, more educated and heavier Facebook users than the average Facebook user.
    ProcedureA field experiment. Participants were randomly allocated to a deactivation group, who were paid $102 to deactivate their Facebook account for four weeks (October to November 2018), or a control group, who kept using Facebook. The researchers checked whether accounts were really deactivated by regularly checking each participant's public profile. Participants answered daily text message questions about their mood, and surveys on happiness, life satisfaction, loneliness, depression, anxiety, time use and news knowledge.
    FindingsAccounts in the deactivation group were deactivated on about 90% of checks. Deactivation freed up about 60 minutes a day. People spent more time offline, including with friends and family and watching TV alone. They knew less about current news. Overall well-being improved, but the effect was small (0.09 standard deviations), and the daily text message mood measures did not show a significant change. After the four weeks ended, the deactivation group used Facebook less than the control group, and the amount they said they would need to be paid to stop again fell.
    ConclusionStopping completely led to small improvements in well-being and a lasting reduction in use, but it also had costs, such as knowing less about the news.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section B (abstinence as a strategy); Paper 1 Section C on change, causality or measurement.
    Evaluate it withChange: use stayed lower after the study ended, which suggests that a break can change habits, not just pause them. Causality: random allocation and checks on deactivation support a causal claim. Bias: participants were adults who agreed to quit Facebook for money, so they may differ from teenagers who are struggling to stop. Measurement: well-being was self-reported, and different measures gave different results.

    How effective are prevention and treatment strategies?

    Strengths. Experiments such as Hunt et al. (2018) and Allcott et al. (2020) used random allocation, so they can show that limiting or stopping use caused changes in well-being. Both checked behaviour objectively rather than relying only on what participants said. The findings point the same way: using social media less is followed by small improvements in how people feel, and Faulhaber et al. (2023) found a similar result with a different sample. Limiting use and self-monitoring are cheap, and reduction does not ask young people to cut themselves off from friends.

    Limitations. Most studies last two to four weeks, so we do not know whether the effects last for months. Most participants were US university students or adults, not teenagers or people in the Gulf region. They were also mostly ordinary users, not addicted, so the studies test prevention more than treatment. Treatment research has the weaknesses identified by King et al. (2011), including different definitions of the problem and weak control groups. Effects on well-being are often small, and evidence about one app in one year may not apply to the apps students use now.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to preventing and treating social media addiction
    MeasurementEffectiveness can be measured as time spent, addiction features or well-being, and each gives a different picture. Hunt et al. measured use objectively but well-being by self-report.
    CausalityRandom allocation in Hunt et al. and Allcott et al. supports causal claims, but most treatment trials lack proper control groups (King et al., 2011).
    BiasSamples are mostly US university students or adults who volunteered, and self-report measures are open to social desirability.
    ChangeStrategies aim to change behaviour, but the platforms themselves change every year, and few studies follow people long enough to see whether the change lasts.
    PerspectiveA cognitive perspective focuses on thoughts and dissonance; a learning perspective on reinforcement and modelling; a sociocultural perspective on family and school rules.
    ResponsibilityWho should act: the individual, parents, schools or the companies that design apps to hold attention?
