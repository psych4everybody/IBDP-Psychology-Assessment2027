    Why compare cultures at all?

    There are two ways to study a culture. In the emic approach, the researcher immerses themselves in one culture and tries to understand it on its own terms, without importing ideas from outside. This gives deep insight, but it cannot compare one culture with another. To compare, researchers need a set of measures that apply to every culture. That is the etic approach, and cultural dimensions are exactly such a set.

    Geert Hofstede described culture as a kind of shared mental programming: the patterns of thinking, feeling and acting that members of one group learn and that set them apart from members of other groups. His idea was that cultural differences are not random. They seem to follow a small number of common patterns, and each pattern can be described as a dimension.

    Exam tip. You do not have to list all six dimensions. In an answer, it is equally acceptable to choose one and explain it well.

    Key study 1: Hofstede (1980), values at IBM

    AimTo find out whether the work-related values of people in different countries differ in a small number of systematic ways.
    ParticipantsEmployees of IBM, a multinational company with staff in many countries. Between 1967 and 1973, more than 100,000 questionnaires were collected, eventually covering more than 50 countries and regions. Because staff did similar jobs in every country, nationality was the main thing that differed. Most participants were men.
    ProcedureA questionnaire survey of values, such as how people felt about their manager, about cooperation and about rules. Average answers were calculated for each country, and the country averages were analysed with factor analysis, a statistical technique that looks for hidden patterns behind many answers.
    FindingsFour dimensions emerged: individualism versus collectivism, power distance, masculinity versus femininity, and uncertainty avoidance. Each country received a score on each dimension. For example, Guatemala scores very high on power distance (95) while Austria scores very low (11), and individualism is high in the USA (91) and low in Malaysia (26).
    ConclusionNational cultures differ in a few broad, measurable ways, which allows cultures to be compared with one set of measures.
    What happened nextLong-term versus short-term orientation was added after a survey written by Chinese researchers found a dimension that Hofstede's questions had missed. Indulgence versus restraint was added later, from analysis of a different worldwide survey. Later studies with other groups, such as airline pilots, students and civil service managers, tested whether the same patterns appeared outside IBM.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (cultural dimensions, the etic approach); Paper 1 Section B (applying a dimension); Paper 1 Section C on measurement or bias.
    Evaluate it withMeasurement: dimensions were found in country averages, so they describe nations, not individuals. Bias: one company, mostly men, in the late 1960s and early 1970s, with questions written by Western researchers. Change: the data are now more than fifty years old, and cultures may have changed since. Perspective: the dimensions began as a tool for understanding businesses.

    The six dimensions

    DimensionOne endThe other end
    Individualism versus collectivismIndividualism: identity comes from personal characteristics and success. Autonomy, competitiveness and self-sufficiency are valued.Collectivism: identity is linked to the social group. Belonging to the group takes priority over personal goals.
    Power distanceHigh: hierarchy between the less powerful and the more powerful is firmly established and rarely questioned.Low: people question authority and do not see authority figures as particularly superior to themselves.
    Masculinity versus femininityMasculine values: achievement, assertiveness, competitiveness.Feminine values: caring, cooperation, modesty, compassion.
    Uncertainty avoidanceHigh: ambiguity is avoided, and unexpected events are not tolerated well.Low: ambiguity is tolerated and new things are embraced more easily.
    Long-term versus short-term orientationShort-term: relies on the past for guidance and values tradition.Long-term: pragmatic, oriented to the future and its challenges.
    Indulgence versus restraintIndulgence: relatively free enjoyment of life and having fun.Restraint: enjoyment is regulated by strict social norms.

    A national score describes an average pattern. It is not a statement about every person in that country, and the next sections explain why that difference matters so much.

    How a dimension is measured

    Culture cannot be seen directly, so researchers record something that might stand for it. Measures of cultural dimensions are questionnaires of statements that people rate themselves on, usually on a Likert scale from "strongly disagree" to "strongly agree". The statements are written on the basis of a theory about what the dimensions are. Three steps turn those answers into dimensions.

    1. Pairwise correlations. For every pair of statements, the researcher asks: do people who agree with one tend to agree with the other (a positive correlation), tend to disagree (a negative one), or show no tendency at all?
    2. A correlation matrix. All the pairwise correlations go into one table. If dimensions are real, statements that belong to the same dimension should correlate strongly with each other. Statements that belong to different dimensions should not.
    3. Factor analysis. A more complex statistical procedure that finds the groups of linked statements more objectively, instead of by eye.

    From a measurement point of view, then, a cultural dimension is a group of statements that are strongly linked to each other and fairly independent of all the other statements. National scores come from the average answers of a sample in each country.

    Two ideas help us judge whether any survey is a good measure.

    • Reliability: does the survey give consistent results? One kind, internal consistency, asks whether the items in one scale hang together.
    • Construct validity: do the survey's items really measure the idea they are supposed to measure, such as power distance?

    Key study 2: Gerlach and Eriksson (2021), testing Hofstede's survey

    Hofstede's questionnaire is called the Values Survey Module (VSM). It has been revised several times; the latest version is the VSM 2013.

    AimTo test the reliability (internal consistency) and validity of three scales of the VSM 2013: individualism, power distance and indulgence.
    Participants22,863 people in 57 countries, surveyed in 2019 and 2020. About four in five were students.
    ProcedureParticipants answered the VSM 2013 items as part of a larger international study. The researchers checked whether the items in each scale hung together, and compared each country's scores with the published Hofstede scores and with averages from other studies.
    FindingsInternal consistency was very low for all three scales: the items within a scale did not hang together. Country scores correlated only weakly with the published national scores.
    ConclusionThe authors concluded that the validity of the VSM 2013 is in doubt. They noted that newer studies have matched Hofstede's original national scores less and less closely over the years.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section C on measurement (reliability and construct validity) or change.
    Evaluate it withBias: most participants were students, unlike the IBM employees, so a weak match might reflect a different kind of sample. Change: the data were collected about fifty years after the IBM survey. Measurement: a very large sample in many countries makes the finding hard to dismiss.

    This leaves a puzzle with two possible explanations for why newer data no longer match the old national scores.

    • The survey works, but cultural values themselves have changed over time.
    • Cultural values are fairly stable, but the survey is not a reliable measure, so the results move around.

    The evidence so far cannot settle which explanation is right. That is a real problem for measurement, because the two explanations lead to opposite conclusions about the same numbers.

    Who was measured, and what does a score describe?

    • The sample. Hofstede's original research was very large, but it was done in a business setting, at one of the most elite companies of its time. Because of hiring patterns in those years, most participants were men.
    • Individual and national level. Hofstede's measures were designed to produce national scores, not to assess individuals. If Japan scores high on collectivism, that does not mean every Japanese person shares collectivist values. A person picked at random may value independence, tolerate ambiguity and treat authority figures as equals.
    • Differences within a nation. The model assumes that one nation has one culture. Its great appeal is that it explains many cultural differences with only a few dimensions. The price is that differences inside a nation get lost.

    Quick lens: bias. Every limitation above is a way the picture could tilt in one direction. Keep these questions open. You will come back to them in the Section C plan.

    • Hofstede's participants were mostly men working for one company. Whose culture does that describe best, and whose might it miss?
    • A single score treats a nation as one culture. Think of your own class. How many different family backgrounds are in the room, and what would one number for your class hide?
    • The dimensions began as a tool for business. What might be lost when the same dimensions are used to explain the behaviour of a teenager at school?

    Culture and trust in the in-group

    Social identity theory (Chapter 5) predicts that we trust members of our in-group more than strangers. Does culture change that? Research on individualism and collectivism suggests it might.

    Yuki et al. (2005) compared American and Japanese participants, using a questionnaire in one study and an online game about sharing money in another. Americans trusted in-group members more than out-group members: belonging to the same category was enough. For Japanese participants, a possible personal link, such as knowing someone in common, raised trust in an out-group member more than it did for Americans.

    Brewer and Yuki (2007) read findings like these as two kinds of trust. For the American participants, trust was group-based: sharing a category was enough. For the Japanese participants, trust was more relationship-based: it depended on who you are connected to. If so, social identity theory may fit individualistic societies better than collectivistic ones, at least when it comes to trust within the in-group.

    Real life: the team briefing.

    Offices in the UAE often bring together teams from many countries. Some companies run workshops that use country scores to prepare managers. A new manager might be told: "Your colleague comes from a high power distance country, so do not expect him to disagree with you in meetings."

    That briefing might help the manager start a conversation. It might also make the manager stop listening. The score came from averages of thousands of answers, collected in a different era, and it says nothing certain about this colleague.

    During the reading: where could a briefing like this help, and where could it mislead? What would you add so that a manager treats the score as a question to ask and not an answer to act on?

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to cultural dimensions
    MeasurementA dimension is a cluster of self-rated statements found by correlation and factor analysis; Gerlach and Eriksson (2021) found low reliability and weak validity for three VSM 2013 scales.
    BiasThe IBM sample was one company, mostly men, and the questions were written by Western researchers; a national score hides differences within a nation.
    ChangeNewer data match the old national scores less closely, which could mean cultures have changed or that the survey is unreliable.
    CausalityA national score cannot show that a dimension causes one person's behaviour; it describes an average pattern.
    PerspectiveAn etic tool designed in one culture may miss what matters in another, as the later addition of long-term orientation shows.
    ResponsibilityUsing a country score to predict what one colleague or student will do can turn a research average into a stereotype.

    I do. Imagine a survey item meant to measure power distance: "A good student never argues with a teacher." Is it a good match for the construct? Power distance is about whether hierarchy is accepted and rarely questioned. A student could agree with this item because they accept that teachers hold more power. But they could just as easily agree because they think arguing is impolite, whoever the other person is. The item may be picking up manners, not hierarchy. That is a construct validity question: does the item measure the idea it claims to measure?
