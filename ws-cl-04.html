    Two ways of thinking

    The dual processing model proposes that people think in two different ways. System 1 is fast, automatic and effortless. It works by association and gives us answers before we know we have asked a question. System 2 is slow, deliberate and effortful. It follows rules, can hold several steps in mind, and can check the answers that System 1 offers. The labels come from Stanovich and West (2000), and Kahneman (2011) made them widely known. Some researchers prefer to talk about Type 1 and Type 2 processes, to avoid suggesting that there are two separate parts of the brain.

    FeatureSystem 1System 2
    SpeedFastSlow
    EffortLittle or noneHigh; it uses up limited attention
    ControlAutomatic; hard to switch offDeliberate; we choose to engage it
    How it worksAssociations, patterns, past experienceRules, logic, step-by-step reasoning
    Everyday examplesReading a road sign, recognising a friend's voice, answering 2 + 2Filling in a visa form, comparing two phone contracts, solving 17 x 24

    System 2 can override System 1, but it is effortful, so people often accept System 1's answer without checking it. In the warm-up, AED 1 comes to mind first. It feels right, which is why so many people never check it. The correct answer is AED 0.50, because the notebook would then cost AED 10.50 and the total would be AED 11.

    Why the model matters: errors in decision-making

    The model explains why errors in thinking are systematic rather than random. When System 1 gives a quick answer and System 2 does not check it, the same kinds of mistakes appear again and again. These patterns are cognitive biases, which you will study in Chapter 5. Anchoring bias, for example, happens when a first number pulls a later judgement towards it.

    Real-life examples

    • Shopping festival signs. A sign in a Dubai mall during a shopping festival reads "Was AED 400, now AED 199". System 1 compares AED 199 with AED 400 and judges it a bargain. System 2 would ask whether the item was ever worth AED 400 at all.
    • Driving. A new driver uses System 2 for every gear change. An experienced driver on a familiar route through the city drives almost on autopilot, until something unusual happens and System 2 takes over.
    • Exams. A question that looks familiar invites a fast System 1 answer. Reading the command term slowly is a deliberate System 2 check.

    Key study 1: Frederick (2005), the Cognitive Reflection Test

    AimTo develop a short test of cognitive reflection (the tendency to stop and check an intuitive answer) and to see how it relates to decision-making.
    MethodA three-item test (the CRT). Each item has an intuitive answer that comes to mind quickly but is wrong. The best-known item is the bat-and-ball problem, which your Warm-up copied.
    Participants3,428 respondents in 35 separate studies over 26 months, most of them undergraduates at American universities.
    FindingsThe average score was 1.24 out of 3. A third of respondents scored zero and only 17% answered all three correctly. Students at MIT scored highest, with an average of 2.18. People who gave the intuitive wrong answer often thought the problem was easy. Higher scorers were more patient in choices about money over time.
    ConclusionMany people report the first answer that comes to mind without checking it, and people differ in how readily they engage System 2.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example of the model); Paper 1 Section B; Paper 1 Section C on measurement or bias.
    Evaluate it withMeasurement: three items is a very short test, and once people have seen the questions they may remember the answers, so the test may stop measuring reflection. Bias: most respondents were American university students. Causality: the links with patience are correlations, so they do not show that reflection causes patience.

    Key study 2: Alter et al. (2007), and the replication that followed

    AimTo test whether making a task feel difficult triggers System 2 thinking.
    Participants40 undergraduate volunteers at Princeton University.
    ProcedureParticipants were randomly allocated to complete the CRT printed either in a clear 12-point black font (fluent condition) or in a small, pale grey, italic font that was hard to read (disfluent condition).
    Findings90% of participants in the fluent condition answered at least one question wrongly, compared with 35% in the disfluent condition.
    ConclusionA feeling of difficulty may act as a signal that a task needs effort, which engages System 2.
    What happened nextMeyer et al. (2015) pooled the data from this experiment with 16 attempts to repeat it, more than 7,000 participants in all. They found no benefit of the hard-to-read font on solution rates. The font made people slightly slower, but not more accurate.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section C on causality or measurement; Paper 2 Section B practice (a true experiment with a small sample).
    Evaluate it withCausality: random allocation supports a causal claim, but with 20 people per group a large difference can appear by chance. Measurement: "using System 2" was never observed; it was inferred from correct answers. Bias: striking results from small studies are more likely to be published and repeated in textbooks, which is why replication matters.

    How useful is the model?

    Strengths. It explains why errors in thinking follow patterns, and it links many cognitive biases to one idea. It makes testable predictions, for example that people will make more errors when they are rushed or tired. It has practical uses, such as designing forms and signs that prompt people to slow down at important moments.

    Limitations. Two systems may be too simple: thinking may vary along a continuum rather than switching between two modes, and Keren and Schul (2009) argued that two-system theories are not clearly defined. Researchers cannot observe which system is working; they infer it from accuracy and response time. Some well-known supporting findings, such as the disfluent font effect, have not replicated.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to the dual processing model
    MeasurementWhich system is working is never observed directly; it is inferred from answers and response times.
    CausalityAlter et al. used random allocation to test a cause, but the effect disappeared in larger samples.
    BiasThe model explains cognitive biases, and research on it is open to publication bias.
    ChangeEffortful System 2 skills, such as driving or reading, can become automatic with practice.
    PerspectiveThe model is a cognitive explanation; it says little about emotion, culture or biology.
    ResponsibilityDesigners who use System 1 tendencies to steer choices (for example, on shopping websites) raise questions about manipulation.

    The finding: In Alter et al. (2007), 35% of participants made at least one error when the test was hard to read, compared with 90% when it was easy to read.

    A first reaction: "So making things harder to read makes people think more carefully." Before you accept that, ask one more question.
