    Childhood experiences and the self

    Your self is the picture you hold of who you are: what you are good at, how much you are worth, and how you relate to other people. Nobody is born with this picture. It is built from experience, and a great deal of that experience happens in childhood, long before we can remember it.

    In the attachment chapter you met Bowlby's idea of an internal working model. Through thousands of everyday interactions with a caregiver, a child builds expectations about two things: whether other people can be relied on, and whether they themselves are worth caring for. A child whose needs are met reliably tends to learn "people help me, and I matter". A child whose care is unpredictable, or who has no stable carer at all, may learn something quite different. These early expectations can then shape how the child approaches friends, teachers and challenges later on.

    This chapter asks how strong that early influence really is. Two ideas pull in different directions. The first is that early experience has a lasting effect, because the young brain is especially sensitive to it. The second is that development stays open to change, so that later relationships and opportunities can redirect it. As you will see, the research supports parts of both ideas.

    Privation, deprivation and adverse experiences

    Rutter (1981) argued that "maternal deprivation" was being used for very different experiences, and that they should be separated.

    • Deprivation is the loss of, or long separation from, an attachment figure after a bond has formed. An example is a young child separated from a parent during a long hospital stay.
    • Privation is never having the chance to form an attachment bond at all. This can happen when a child is raised in a large institution with many changing carers and no one adult who responds to them in particular.

    In practice, researchers do not always use these words consistently. The study you will read below talks about institutional deprivation, meaning care in large institutions with few adults, little individual attention and little to explore or play with. For many of those children, this was closer to privation, because they had no chance to form a bond with one carer.

    A broader term is adverse childhood experiences (ACEs). These are stressful or harmful experiences before the age of 18, such as abuse, neglect, or serious difficulties in the household, for example a parent with a severe mental illness. In the original ACE study, Felitti et al. (1998) sent a questionnaire to 13,494 adults who had had a medical check-up through a large health organisation in the USA, and 9,508 replied. More than half reported at least one of the seven categories of adverse experience they asked about. The more categories a person reported, the higher their risk of later health problems, including depression and heart disease. This is called a graded relationship: risk rose step by step with the number of adversities.

    Two points matter for evaluation. First, the ACE study is correlational, so it shows that adversity and poor adult health go together, not that one causes the other. Second, adults were reporting their childhoods from memory, which may be incomplete or coloured by their present situation. Still, the graded relationship supports the idea of cumulative risk: one difficulty may be manageable, but several difficulties together are much harder to cope with.

    Critical periods, sensitive periods and neuroplasticity

    Why would early experience matter more than later experience? One answer comes from the brain. Neuroplasticity is the brain's ability to change its structure and function in response to experience. In early childhood, the brain forms new connections between neurons very quickly, and connections that are used are strengthened while unused ones are pruned. Some of this growth seems to "expect" certain experiences, such as responsive care, language and play. If those experiences are missing, development may take a different path.

    This links to the idea of a timing window, which comes in a strong and a weak version.

    FeatureCritical periodSensitive period
    DefinitionA fixed window in which a specific experience must happen for normal developmentA time when the brain is especially responsive to a type of experience
    If the experience is missedThe ability cannot develop fully laterLater development is still possible, but harder or less complete
    How strong a claimStrong: the window closesWeaker: the window narrows
    Typical evidenceAnimal research, such as imprinting in birdsHuman studies comparing children who experienced adversity for different lengths of time

    Single case studies are often used to argue for critical periods in humans. The best known is the case of a girl called Genie, who was found in Los Angeles in 1970 at about 13 years old after many years of extreme isolation. Curtiss (1977) reported that her vocabulary grew quickly, but her grammar stayed very limited. This seems to support a critical period for language. However, one case cannot separate the effect of isolation from other possible factors, such as a developmental difficulty present from birth, and the research itself raised serious ethical concerns. For stronger evidence, psychologists turned to larger groups of children, such as those in Key study 1.

    Risk, resilience and protective factors

    A risk factor is anything that makes a poor outcome more likely, such as poverty, serious family conflict, or a caregiver's severe illness. A protective factor is anything that reduces the effect of risk. Resilience is doing well despite serious adversity. Psychologists now see resilience as a process, not a fixed trait that a child either has or lacks. It depends on what the child brings and on the support around them, and it can grow or shrink over time.

    LevelExamples of protective factors (from Werner, 1993, 2005)
    IndividualA temperament that draws positive attention from others; problem-solving skills; a special talent or hobby; a positive self-concept; believing that your own actions make a difference (an internal locus of control)
    FamilyA close bond with at least one stable, competent caregiver, who may be a grandparent or older sibling; structure, rules and shared responsibilities at home; religious faith
    CommunityA trusted teacher or mentor; supportive elders and friends; youth groups and faith communities that give a sense of belonging

    Why do children respond differently? The diathesis-stress model

    Two children can live through the same difficult event, and one struggles while the other copes. The diathesis-stress model offers an explanation. A diathesis is a vulnerability that a person carries, which may be genetic, biological or psychological (for example, a temperament that reacts strongly to change). Stress is the adversity in the environment. The model proposes that a disorder or difficulty develops when the vulnerability and the stress together pass a threshold. A child with a high vulnerability may struggle after a moderate stress; a child with a low vulnerability may need much more stress before they struggle.

    Protective factors fit into this model as the other side of the balance: they can raise the threshold. The model is useful because it rejects the idea that either genes or experience alone decide the outcome. It is harder to test than it sounds, however. Caspi et al. (2003) reported that a gene variant involved in serotonin made depression more likely only in people who had experienced many stressful life events. A later pooled analysis of 38,802 people found no clear evidence for this interaction (Culverhouse et al., 2018). The general idea of vulnerability plus stress remains widely used, but specific gene and environment combinations have been hard to replicate.

    Real-life examples

    • Grandparents and extended family. In many Emirati and expatriate families in the UAE, grandparents, aunts or older siblings are part of daily care. Werner found that substitute caregivers like these were often the stable adult who protected high-risk children.
    • Moving country. Many students at international schools in Dubai and Abu Dhabi have moved country at least once. For most, one move is manageable. The idea of cumulative risk suggests it becomes harder when it comes on top of other stresses, such as a family illness, and that a welcoming peer group or a teacher who checks in can act as a protective factor.
    • Hospital stays. A young child who needs a long stay in hospital usually copes better when a parent or familiar carer can stay close and keep routines going. This reflects research on the effects of separation from an attachment figure.

    Key study 1: Rutter et al. (1998) and Sonuga-Barke et al. (2017), the English and Romanian Adoptees (ERA) study

    AimTo find out how far good family care after adoption could make up for severe early institutional deprivation, and whether the length of deprivation affected later development.
    Participants165 children who had spent time in Romanian institutions and were adopted into UK families between 2 weeks and 43 months of age, arriving between 1990 and 1992. A comparison group of 52 children adopted within the UK before 6 months of age, who had not experienced institutional deprivation.
    ProcedureA longitudinal natural experiment. The researchers did not decide which children were adopted or when; the timing was largely set by the fall of the Romanian government in 1989. Conditions in the institutions included poor hygiene, not enough food, little personal care and very little stimulation. Children were assessed at age 4 (the 111 children who had arrived before age 2), then at 6, 11, 15 and 22 to 25, using IQ tests, questionnaires and interviews with parents and adoptees. For analysis, the Romanian adoptees were grouped by time in an institution: less than 6 months or more than 6 months.
    FindingsOn arrival, about half the children were below the 3rd centile (the bottom 3%) for height, weight, head size and development. By age 4, children adopted before 6 months had almost completely caught up. Children adopted later had caught up a great deal, but not fully: their average cognitive score was 92, compared with 109 for the UK adoptees (Rutter et al., 1998). In young adulthood, the under-6-months group were similar to the UK adoptees on most outcomes. The over-6-months group still had higher rates of autism-like social and communication difficulties, disinhibited social engagement (being overly friendly with strangers) and inattention and overactivity. Their higher rate of cognitive impairment in childhood had fallen to normal levels by young adulthood, but emotional difficulties rose sharply in young adulthood. About one in five of this group (15 people) had no difficulties at any assessment (Sonuga-Barke et al., 2017).
    ConclusionSevere deprivation lasting more than about six months had long-lasting effects on some areas of development, even after many years of loving family care. Recovery was still large, it varied a great deal between children, and some effects changed over time. This fits a sensitive period better than a strict critical period.
    What happened nextMackes et al. (2020) scanned the brains of 67 Romanian adoptees and 21 UK adoptees in their twenties. The Romanian adoptees' total brain volume was about 8.6% smaller, and each extra month of deprivation was linked with a smaller brain volume. Smaller volume was linked with lower IQ and more symptoms of inattention and overactivity. This is evidence that early experience can shape brain structure, a point about neuroplasticity.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example for neuroplasticity); Paper 1 Section B; Paper 1 Section C on change or causality; Paper 2 Section B practice (a longitudinal natural experiment).
    Evaluate it withCausality: children were not randomly allocated to deprivation, so other factors (such as health before birth) could play a part; but because the timing of adoption depended on political events rather than on the children, it is a strong natural experiment. Change: the length and the timing of deprivation went together (a child deprived for longer was also older at adoption), so the study cannot show exactly when a sensitive window closes. Measurement: different questionnaires had to be used at different ages, and about a quarter of the sample did not take part in the adult follow-up.

    Key study 2: Werner (1993, 2005), the Kauai Longitudinal Study

    AimTo follow a whole birth cohort to see how early risk factors, stressful life events and protective factors affect development, and to find out why some high-risk children develop well.
    ParticipantsAll 698 children born in 1955 on the island of Kauai, Hawaii. The cohort was ethnically mixed, mostly of Japanese, Filipino and Hawaiian descent, and more than half grew up in poverty. About 30% (around 200 children) were classed as high risk: they had four or more risk factors by age 2, such as poverty, complications around birth, chronic family conflict or a parent's mental illness, and a mother with little schooling.
    ProcedureA prospective longitudinal study: data were collected from before birth and the children were followed up at ages 1, 2, 10, 18, 32 and 40. The team used interviews, developmental and personality tests, and records from health, education, social and court services.
    FindingsTwo in three of the high-risk children had serious learning or behaviour problems by age 10, or delinquency or mental health problems by age 18. One in three (72 children) grew into what Werner called "competent, confident and caring" young adults, with no serious problems in childhood or adolescence. They tended to have the protective factors in the table above, including at least one close bond with a stable caregiver and a positive self-concept. Most of the high-risk teenagers who did have problems had recovered by their thirties or forties. Turning points included further education at a community college, military service, and active involvement in a faith community. At 40, none of the resilient group were unemployed, in trouble with the law or dependent on social services.
    ConclusionRisk is not destiny. Protective factors in the child, the family and the community can reduce the effects of a difficult start, and development stays open to change well into adulthood.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example for the diathesis-stress model); Paper 1 Section B on protective factors; Paper 1 Section C on change or causality.
    Evaluate it withChange: because it is prospective and lasted 40 years, it shows how people changed, rather than relying on adults' memories of childhood. Causality: protective factors were observed, not manipulated, and they cluster together (an easy-going baby may attract more support from adults), so it is hard to say which factor made the difference. Measurement: resilience was largely defined as the absence of problems in records and interviews, which may miss private difficulties. The sample comes from one island and one year, so the findings may not generalise to other cultures and times.

    How useful is research on childhood experiences?

    Strengths. Long longitudinal studies such as ERA and Kauai follow the same people for decades, so they can show how development changes rather than taking a single snapshot. Natural experiments like ERA get close to causal evidence where a true experiment would be unethical. Where random allocation has been possible, it has supported the same pattern: in the Bucharest Early Intervention Project, 136 children in Romanian institutions were randomly assigned to stay in institutional care or move to foster families, and those placed in foster care made significant cognitive gains (Nelson et al., 2007). The research has had practical effects too, supporting family-based care instead of large institutions, early intervention, and school mentoring.

    Limitations. Causality remains difficult. Adversity rarely comes alone, so it is hard to know which part of a child's experience had the effect. Many studies, including the ACE study, rely on adults remembering their childhoods. Key ideas such as "resilience" and "the self" are measured in different ways in different studies, which makes them hard to compare. Much of the research comes from the USA and Europe; Werner (1993) herself noted that resilience research had focused on urban, industrialised settings, and protective factors may work differently in other cultures. Finally, research on children who have experienced adversity raises ethical questions about consent, privacy and the risk of causing distress.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to childhood experiences
    MeasurementResilience, attachment difficulties and the self cannot be observed directly; they are inferred from tests, questionnaires, interviews and records, which change as children grow.
    CausalityChildren cannot ethically be assigned to adversity, so researchers rely on natural experiments and correlations, and adversities tend to come together.
    BiasRetrospective reports of childhood can be distorted by memory, and samples from one culture may not represent others.
    ChangeSome early effects fade (cognitive impairment in ERA) while others last or appear later (emotional difficulties), and many troubled teenagers in Kauai recovered by midlife.
    PerspectiveA biological perspective stresses sensitive periods and brain development; a sociocultural perspective stresses caregivers, mentors and community.
    ResponsibilityResearch on early adversity places responsibility on governments, schools and communities to provide stable care, and on researchers to protect vulnerable participants.
