    What is a health problem?

    In Unit 4.1 you studied a mental health disorder, major depressive disorder. This part of the unit shifts the lens slightly to health problems: patterns of behaviour or physical states that harm a person's health and well-being, such as obesity or social media addiction. Mental health disorders and health problems are not separate boxes. They share risk factors and research methods, and one person can have both at once.

    The IB guide groups health problems under three areas of study: factors affecting their prevalence (this chapter), the role of social learning, and the role of stress. We will use social media addiction as our shared case for all three, the way major depressive disorder was the shared case in Unit 4.1.

    Meet Hamdan (fictional character)

    Hamdan is a 17-year-old Grade 12 student at a private school in Dubai. He is a strong student, plays football for his school team and has a wide circle of friends. In the lead-up to his mock exams, his bedtime drifted later and later because he was scrolling through short videos at night, often without quite deciding to. He told a friend he just needed five minutes to switch off before going back to revising, and the five minutes regularly became an hour or more. His grades did not collapse, but his sense of control over his own time did.

    Hamdan's story will appear across this part of the workbook, the way Layla's did in Unit 4.1. Each chapter looks at a different angle on the same pattern.

    What does "social media addiction" mean?

    Social media addiction is not a diagnosis in the DSM-5. The only behavioural addiction the DSM-5 formally lists is gambling disorder. Internet gaming disorder is listed only as a condition for further study. Social media addiction is therefore studied by analogy with behavioural addictions such as gambling disorder. Many researchers prefer the term "problematic social media use", because it describes a pattern of behaviour without claiming it is a disorder.

    Andreassen et al. (2012) built the most widely used measure on six components that appear in many models of addiction:

    ComponentWhat it meansHow it might look for Hamdan
    SalienceThe activity dominates thinking and behaviour.He thinks about what he might be missing while revising.
    Mood modificationThe activity is used to change mood or escape problems.He scrolls to switch off from exam stress.
    ToleranceMore and more use is needed for the same effect.Five minutes no longer feels like enough.
    WithdrawalRestlessness or irritability when use is stopped.He feels uneasy when his phone is in another room.
    ConflictUse causes problems with sleep, study, work or relationships.His bedtime drifts later before mock exams.
    RelapseReturning to earlier patterns after trying to cut down.He promises himself "no phone after 10 pm" and breaks it within days.

    One item on Hamdan's list does not make an addiction. Many people use social media to relax. Researchers are interested in a pattern in which several components appear together and cause real problems.

    How is it measured?

    The Bergen Facebook Addiction Scale (BFAS) was developed by Andreassen et al. (2012) with 423 university students in Norway. They started with 18 items (three per component) and kept the best item for each component, so the final scale has six items. Each item is rated on a five-point scale from 1 (very rarely) to 5 (very often). Andreassen et al. (2016) later replaced the word "Facebook" with "social media", which gave the Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale (BSMAS). It asks about the past year, so scores range from 6 to 30.

    A questionnaire gives a score, but prevalence needs a yes or no: is this person "addicted" or not? That decision depends on a cut-off. Andreassen et al. (2012) suggested two, and noted that neither had yet been tested:

    • Polythetic (more lenient): a score of 3 or more on at least four of the six items.
    • Monothetic (stricter): a score of 3 or more on all six items.

    Other researchers use a total score. In a nationally representative sample of 5,961 Hungarian adolescents, Bányai et al. (2017) used statistics to sort students into groups and found that 4.5% were in an "at risk" group. They suggested a cut-off of 19 on the BSMAS. The lesson is simple: the same questionnaire can produce very different prevalence figures depending on where the line is drawn. Always ask how a prevalence figure was measured before you compare it with another one.

    Three different causality questions

    Three questions sit underneath the word "prevalence", and the exam will not always tell you which one it is asking. They look like one question. They are not. Mixing them up is one of the most common ways to lose marks in this area of study.

    1. Aetiology: how and why does one person develop this pattern? (Why Hamdan?)
    2. Change within a population: why has the reported rate in one population gone up or down over time?
    3. Difference between populations: why is the reported rate different in one country, age group or community compared with another?

    This chapter is about questions 2 and 3. Each needs different evidence, and a strong answer says which question it is answering.

    Factors that may explain changes over time

    The Health Behaviour in School-aged Children (HBSC) study surveys young people aged 11, 13 and 15 every four years. In its 2024 report with the WHO Regional Office for Europe, covering almost 280,000 young people in 44 countries and regions, problematic social media use rose from 7% in 2018 to 11% in 2022. In 2022 the rate was 13% for girls and 9% for boys. Several factors may explain a rise like this.

    • Platform design. Many apps are designed to keep people using them: infinite scroll and autoplay remove natural stopping points, and likes and notifications arrive unpredictably. In learning terms (Unit 2), rewards that arrive on an unpredictable schedule produce behaviour that is hard to stop. As platforms add more of these features, more people may show the components of addiction.
    • Smartphones. A phone means social media is available everywhere, including in bed at night. Easier access gives more chances for use to become a habit and to conflict with sleep or study.
    • The COVID-19 pandemic. In 2020 and 2021, school, friendships and entertainment moved online for many young people, including through distance learning in the UAE. The HBSC rise from 2018 to 2022 spans the pandemic years.
    • Measurement and awareness. A rise in reported rates can also come from changes in how a problem is defined or how willing people are to report it. Stable methods across survey rounds, as in the HBSC study, reduce this problem but do not remove it.

    Notice the causality problem. All of these factors changed at the same time. The HBSC survey asks different young people at each round (a repeated cross-sectional design), so it can show that the rate changed, but it cannot separate the effect of the pandemic from the effect of new app features or more phones.

    Factors that may explain differences between populations

    • Age. In a survey of 23,533 Norwegian adults aged 16 to 88, Andreassen et al. (2016) found that younger age was linked with higher scores on the BSMAS.
    • Gender. The same study found that being female was linked with higher scores, and the HBSC data show the same pattern among adolescents. One possible explanation is that girls and boys tend to use different platforms for different purposes.
    • Measurement. Studies in different countries often use different questionnaires and cut-offs. As Key study 2 shows, this alone can multiply a prevalence figure several times.
    • Culture and cultural dimensions. Hofstede described cultures as differing along cultural dimensions, such as individualism versus collectivism. In more individualist cultures, people tend to see themselves as independent and put personal goals first. In more collectivist cultures, people tend to see themselves as part of close groups, such as the family, and put group goals and relationships first. If social media is a main way of keeping up with a large family or friendship group, frequent use may be expected rather than seen as a problem. Cultural dimensions describe national averages, not individuals, so they can never tell you about one person.
    • Quality of life. People may turn to the internet more when life offline is less satisfying, a factor tested in Key study 1.

    Real-life examples

    • Almost everyone is connected. DataReportal's Digital 2025 report estimated that 99% of people in the UAE use the internet, and counted social media user identities equal to 100% of the population (these are accounts, not unique people). When almost everyone has access, access alone cannot explain why some people develop problematic use and others do not.
    • A new law. The UAE's Federal Decree-Law No. 26 of 2025 on Child Digital Safety came into force on 1 January 2026. It covers children under 18 and requires platforms to use age verification and offer tools for parents, such as daily time limits. If reported rates in the UAE change over the next few years, this law will be one possible factor, but only one among many.
    • Design in your pocket. Streaks that reset if you miss a day, red notification badges and videos that start the next one automatically are all features you can find on your own phone. Each one removes a moment when you might decide to stop.

    Key study 1: Cheng and Li (2014), internet addiction across 31 nations

    AimTo estimate the prevalence of internet addiction around the world and test two explanations for differences between nations: the accessibility hypothesis (more internet access and wealth, more addiction) and the quality of (real) life hypothesis (a less satisfying life offline, more addiction).
    ParticipantsA meta-analysis of 80 reports (164 samples) published between 1996 and 2012, with 89,281 participants from 31 nations in seven world regions. The mean age was about 18. No studies from Africa were found.
    ProcedureThe researchers pooled studies that used one of two questionnaires: the Young Diagnostic Questionnaire or the Internet Addiction Test. They then compared national prevalence with national measures such as internet access, wealth (GDP per person), life satisfaction, time spent commuting and pollution.
    FindingsThe global prevalence was 6.0%. It was highest in the Middle East (10.9%, from only nine samples) and lowest in Northern and Western Europe (2.6%). Internet access did not predict national rates. Higher rates were linked with lower life satisfaction, longer commuting time, more pollution and lower GDP.
    ConclusionThe quality of life hypothesis was supported and the accessibility hypothesis was not. People may turn to the internet more when life offline is harder. The authors described the support as tentative.
    What happened nextThis study is about internet addiction in general, not social media. Cheng et al. (2021) used the same approach for social media addiction and added the two factors this study did not test: the cut-off used and cultural values (Key study 2).
    Use it forPaper 1 Section B (differences between populations); Paper 1 Section C on causality or measurement; Paper 2 Section B practice (a meta-analysis of correlational data).
    Evaluate it withCausality: the links are correlations between national averages, so they do not show that pollution or long commutes cause addiction; wealth or city life could explain both. Measurement: internet addiction was measured with self-report questionnaires that use different cut-offs. Bias: with no African data and only nine Middle East samples, some regional figures rest on little evidence.

    Key study 2: Cheng et al. (2021), social media addiction across 32 nations

    AimTo estimate the prevalence of social media addiction and find out why reported rates vary so much, by comparing the classification schemes (cut-offs) studies used and the cultural values of the nations.
    ParticipantsA meta-analysis of 63 independent samples with 34,798 respondents from 32 nations in seven world regions.
    ProcedureThe researchers grouped studies by how strictly they classified someone as addicted: monothetic schemes (all components needed), strict polythetic schemes or cut-offs for a severe level, and polythetic schemes or cut-offs for a moderate level. They also grouped nations as individualist or collectivist and compared pooled prevalence across groups.
    FindingsPooled prevalence depended on the classification scheme: about 5% with monothetic schemes, 13% with strict polythetic schemes or severe-level cut-offs, and 25% with polythetic schemes or moderate-level cut-offs. Prevalence in collectivist nations (31%) was about twice that in individualist nations (14%).
    ConclusionBoth the cut-off used and cultural values need to be considered when interpreting a prevalence figure for social media addiction.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example of cultural dimensions); Paper 1 Section B; Paper 1 Section C on measurement or causality.
    Evaluate it withMeasurement: the same behaviour can give 5% or 25% depending on the cut-off, so a prevalence figure means little without its method. Causality: culture was not manipulated; collectivist and individualist nations differ in many other ways, and in which cut-offs their studies used. Perspective: the questionnaire was developed in Norway, an individualist culture, so items about "conflict" or "salience" may not mean the same thing elsewhere (an etic approach).

    How useful is research on the prevalence of social media addiction?

    Strengths. Large surveys and meta-analyses combine data from tens of thousands of people, so they can show patterns no single school study could. Repeated surveys with the same method, like the HBSC study, can track change in one population over time. Meta-analyses such as Cheng et al. (2021) show why figures differ, which helps policy makers and schools avoid panicking over a single headline.

    Limitations. There is no agreed definition or cut-off, so studies are hard to compare. Almost all data are self-report and cross-sectional, so they show associations, not causes. National comparisons rely on averages and cannot tell us about individuals. A questionnaire built in one culture may not measure the same thing in another. Finally, calling everyday behaviour "addiction" risks treating normal social media use as a disorder.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to the prevalence of social media addiction
    MeasurementPrevalence depends on the questionnaire and the cut-off: 5% or 25% in Cheng et al. (2021), from similar behaviour.
    CausalityA change or difference in rates is correlational; many factors change or differ at once.
    BiasSome regions have few studies, and online volunteer samples may include more heavy users.
    ChangeRates can rise within one population, as in the HBSC rise from 7% to 11% between 2018 and 2022.
    PerspectiveAn etic tool from Norway may miss what "too much" use means in a collectivist culture; an emic approach would ask local people first.
    ResponsibilityWho is responsible for reducing problematic use: platforms that design for engagement, governments that pass laws, or users and families?

    Rung 4: One more question

    The finding: In Cheng et al. (2021), the pooled prevalence of social media addiction was 31% in collectivist nations and 14% in individualist nations.

    A first reaction: "So living in a collectivist culture causes social media addiction." Before you accept that, ask one more question.

    • Was culture manipulated by the researchers, or were nations sorted into groups after the data were collected?
    • The same meta-analysis found that the cut-off alone could change prevalence from 5% to 25%. Could the two groups of nations differ in the cut-offs their studies used?
    • The questionnaire was developed in Norway. Would an item about "conflict" with family mean the same thing to a teenager in a culture where staying in constant contact with family is expected?
