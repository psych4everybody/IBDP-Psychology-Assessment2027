On this page

    Aim

    Not everything you find belongs in your essay

    The IB guidance asks you to justify every study you choose by linking it clearly to your research question, and to evaluate each one on its own strengths and limitations rather than with generic points.

    By the end of this stage you can:

    • score any source with four questions: Match, Method, Merit and Role
    • say what kind of claim a study's design can and cannot support
    • give every study you keep a clear job in your argument
    • write evaluation that is about this study and your question
    Our example questionTo what extent does taking notes by hand rather than on a laptop improve students' learning from lectures?
    The check

    Match, Method, Merit, Role

    M

    Match

    Does it study the same thing as your question, measured in a way that fits, with people close enough to the ones you are asking about?

    M

    Method

    Can its design support the kind of claim you want to use it for: cause and effect, or only an association?

    M

    Merit

    How much can you trust it? Peer review, sample, replication, how recent it is, and who paid for it.

    R

    Role

    What job will it do in your argument: support, challenge, qualify, or give context?

    A prompt, not a formula

    The score starts a conversation; it does not end one. A weak study can stay if its weakness is the point you are making, for example to show how thin the early evidence was.

    Match

    Is it really about your question?

    The most common mistake is keeping a study that is about a nearby idea. Check three things.

    CheckOff-targetOn-target
    The constructScreen time, or reading on a tabletThe medium used to take notes
    The outcomeSelf-reported "memory problems"Scores on a test of the lecture content
    The people and settingOffice workers in meetingsStudents learning from lectures or lessons

    A partial match is not a reason to cut, but it is a limitation you must name. Most note-taking studies used university students and short recorded talks, so an essay about school students should say how far those findings can travel.

    Method

    What claim can the design support?

    DesignCan supportCannot supportRevise
    True experiment (random allocation)Cause and effect, in that settingThat the effect holds outside the lab or over longer periodsCRM-03
    Quasi- or natural experimentCause and effect, with cautionRuling out pre-existing differences between groupsCRM-04
    Correlational study or surveyA relationship and its strengthWhich variable causes whichCRM-07
    Interviews and other qualitative workHow people experience something, and possible reasons whyHow common it is, or what causes whatCRM-06
    Case studyDepth on one person or group, and new questionsGeneralising to othersCRM-07
    Meta-analysisThe overall pattern and size of an effectAnything better than the studies it includes

    Matching the strength of your wording to the design is part of using causality well.

    Merit

    How much can you trust it?

    • Peer review. Has it been checked by other researchers before publication?
    • Sample. How many people, and who? A small sample can produce a large result by chance. There is no magic number: what counts as big enough depends on the design and the size of the effect. How the sample was chosen often matters more than its size.
    • Replication. Has anyone repeated it? What did they find? Famous studies are not always the most reliable ones.
    • Recency. Is it recent enough, or has newer work replaced it?
    • Funding and interests. Who paid for it? Does anyone involved gain from a particular result?
    • Openness. Was it preregistered, and are the data available? A bonus, not a requirement.

    These are bias and measurement questions, the same ones you use in Papers 1 and 2.

    Role

    Give every study a job

    Criteria C and D reward a line of argument. A study with a clear job is easy to link back to your question; a study with no job is padding.

    JobSentence starter
    Support"This study supports the claim that ... because ..."
    Challenge"This study challenges the claim that ... because ..."
    Qualify"This study suggests the effect depends on ..., because ..."
    Context"This study shows why ... matters / how ... is defined, which I use to ..."

    Generic evaluation

    "This was a lab experiment, so it lacks ecological validity."

    Specific evaluation

    "Students watched a short recorded talk and took a quiz soon afterwards without reviewing their notes. My question is about learning across a unit, when students do review, so this study tells us about the first stage only."

    The specific version names a feature of this study and says what it means for your question. That is what the IB guidance means by avoiding a formulaic approach.
    Ethics

    The IB guidance notes that ethics is rarely worth evaluating in an EE unless it limits what the research can tell us. One exception is useful: ethics can explain why some evidence does not exist at all.

    Red flags

    Signs your source list needs work

    • An abstract is about a nearby idea, not your exact one.
    • The sample or setting is far from your question and your essay never says so.
    • One study carries the whole argument.
    • Every source agrees with you. You probably have not looked for the other side yet.
    • You are citing a study from a summary you read, not from the paper itself.
    For supervisors

    Notes for supervisors

    Suggested use
    • Exercise 1 works well in pairs: score separately, then argue about any study where the scores differ by two or more.
    • Ask students to bring their scored source list (from the tool) to the second supervision meeting.
    • The six abstracts in Exercise 1 are fictional and written for practice; the replication hunt uses real studies.
    Exercise 1 · 30 minutes

    Source triage

    Six summaries of studies for our example question. All six are fictional, written for practice. For each one:

    1. Score Match, Method, Merit and Role from 0 to 2, with a reason for each score.
    2. Decide: keep, park or cut.
    3. For every study you keep or park, write one sentence linking it to the question, using a starter from the Role table.

    Study A

    Fictional · peer-reviewed journal · 2022

    220 Grade 11 students in four schools were randomly assigned to take notes on laptops or by hand during a six-week psychology unit. At the end of the unit, the handwriting group scored slightly higher on conceptual questions. There was no difference on factual questions.

    Study B

    Fictional · peer-reviewed journal · 2020

    An online survey of 1,800 adults asked how many hours a day they spend on screens and how often they forget things. People who reported more screen time also reported more memory problems.

    Study C

    Fictional · company blog · 2024

    A company that sells digital notebooks reports that students who type their notes "improve their grades by 30%". The post gives no information about who took part or how grades were measured.

    Study D

    Fictional · peer-reviewed journal · 2023

    A meta-analysis of 20 experiments with university students. Overall, handwriting gave a small advantage on later tests, but mainly when students reviewed their notes before the test. With no review, the difference was close to zero.

    Study E

    Fictional · peer-reviewed journal · 2009

    24 university students typed or handwrote a list of 40 words read aloud, then recalled as many as they could. Handwriters recalled more words, a difference that was significant only with a one-tailed test. No replication has been published.

    Study F

    Fictional · peer-reviewed journal · 2021

    Semi-structured interviews with 18 secondary students about how they choose to take notes. Themes included distraction from messages and other apps on laptops, the speed of typing, and feeling that handwritten notes were "more mine".

    Model answer

    Try the exercise first, then compare.

    Model scores for the example question. Reasonable people may differ by a point. Your reasons matter more than the exact numbers.

    StudyMatchMethodMeritRoleTotalDecision and reason
    A22127Keep. Same constructs, a school setting close to yours, random allocation over a whole unit. Merit is 1, not 2, because it is one study in four schools and has not been replicated. Role: partial support (concepts only, not facts).
    B00101Cut. Screen time is not note-taking medium, and self-reported memory problems are not test performance. A correlational survey cannot tell you what typing notes does to learning.
    C10001Cut. Not peer reviewed, no method, and written by a company that sells the product it praises (a conflict of interest). At most, it shows why the question matters to schools.
    D22228Keep, as a core source. A meta-analysis gives the overall pattern. Its key point (the advantage mostly appears when notes are reviewed) gives you a moderating variable. Role: qualify the claim. Note it is about university students.
    E12014Park. A real experiment, but word lists are not lectures, the sample is 24, the result was only significant one-tailed, and it was never replicated. Useful only to show how weak the early evidence was.
    F11125Park, possibly keep as context. Interviews cannot show what improves learning, but they can explain why: students describe distraction on laptops. That gives a possible mechanism to test against Studies A and D.

    Sample Role sentences.

    • Study A: "This study supports the claim for conceptual learning, because students randomly given paper scored higher on conceptual questions after a six-week unit; it does not support it for factual recall."
    • Study D: "This meta-analysis qualifies the claim: the advantage of handwriting appears mainly when students review their notes, which suggests that what students do after the lecture matters as much as the pen."
    Exercise 2 · 30 minutes

    Replication hunt

    Famous studies are not always the most reliable ones. Choose one study you know from the course, then use forward chaining (Stage 3) to find out what later research found.

    • Mueller and Oppenheimer (2014): laptop versus handwritten notes
    • Sparrow, Liu and Wegner (2011): the "Google effect" on memory
    • Mueller and Dweck (1998): praise for intelligence versus effort
    1. Summarise the original claim in one sentence.
    2. Find at least two later studies: replications, extensions or meta-analyses. Note what each found.
    3. Write two or three sentences: how should an EE that uses this study describe it today?
    EE-04-2Keep your answer in your researcher's reflection space.

    Model answer

    Try the exercise first, then compare.

    Model answer for Mueller and Oppenheimer (2014).

    1. The original claim. Students who typed notes wrote more words and copied more of the lecture word for word, and they did worse on conceptual questions than students who wrote by hand.
    2. Morehead, Dunlosky and Rawson (2019). A replication and extension. The handwriting advantage was weaker, and the authors concluded it was too early to say which method is better.
    3. Urry et al. (2021). A direct replication of Study 1 with 142 students, plus a mini meta-analysis of eight similar studies. Typed notes again had more words and more verbatim copying, but quiz scores did not differ significantly.
    4. Flanigan et al. (2024). A meta-analysis of 24 studies. Students who handwrote and reviewed their notes tended to achieve more, even though typed notes held more words.

    What this does to an essay. An essay that cited only the 2014 study would overclaim. A stronger essay says the effect is smaller than first reported and may depend on reviewing. That is a measurement and bias point (a small original sample and a famous result that was hard to repeat).

    Other studies on this site with a replication story: Sparrow, Liu and Wegner (2011) on the "Google effect" (see what the large replication project of Camerer et al., 2018, found) and Mueller and Dweck (1998) on praise for intelligence.

    Exercise 3 · 15 minutes

    Make the evaluation specific

    Each sentence below could be written about almost any study. Rewrite each one so it is about a specific study for our example question and says what the point means for the question. Use Mueller and Oppenheimer (2014) or Urry et al. (2021).

    1. "This study was a lab experiment, so it lacks ecological validity."
    2. "The sample was small, so it cannot be generalised."
    3. "The study may be unethical because participants were not told the aim."
    EE-04-3Keep your answer in your researcher's reflection space.

    Model answer

    Try the exercise first, then compare.

    Generic

    "This study was a lab experiment, so it lacks ecological validity."

    Specific

    "Students watched a short recorded talk and took a quiz soon afterwards without reviewing their notes (Urry et al., 2021). My question is about learning across a unit, when students do review, so this study tells us about encoding only."

    Generic

    "The sample was small, so it cannot be generalised."

    Specific

    "Study 1 tested 67 students from one university (Mueller and Oppenheimer, 2014). In a sample this size a large difference can appear by chance, which may be why a direct replication with 142 students did not find it (Urry et al., 2021)."

    Generic

    "The study may be unethical because participants were not told the aim."

    Specific

    "Ethics does not limit what this study can tell us. A more useful question is measurement: a short quiz soon after a talk measures immediate recall, not the longer-term understanding my question is about."

    Notice the pattern: name the specific feature of the study, then say what it means for your research question.

    Check yourself

    Five quick questions

    A survey finds that students who use laptops more get lower grades. Can you use it to argue that laptops cause lower grades?

    No. A survey shows a relationship, not a cause. Students who use laptops more might differ in other ways, such as how much they study. Use it as an association and say so.

    Is a small sample always a reason to cut a study?

    No. It is a reason to trust a single result less, especially a large one. If the finding has been replicated, or if the study's weakness helps your argument, it can stay. Say how the sample limits it.

    What does the Role question add that Merit does not?

    Merit asks whether the study is good. Role asks whether it is useful to your argument. A strong study with no job is padding; it does not earn marks in Criteria C and D.

    Why is "every source agrees with me" a warning sign?

    Real research questions have evidence on both sides. If all your sources agree, you have probably not searched for the challenge evidence yet, and your discussion and evaluation (Criterion D) will be one-sided.

    When is ethics worth evaluating in a psychology EE?

    When it limits what the research can tell us, for example when ethical rules mean a key experiment could never be done. Otherwise the IB guidance says it is rarely relevant.
