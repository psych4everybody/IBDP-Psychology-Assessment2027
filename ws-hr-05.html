    Why do groups come into conflict?

    Before social identity theory, two explanations of conflict between groups were common.

    • Aggressive personality explanations said that conflict happens because some people are aggressive or hostile by nature. They looked inside the person and paid little attention to the social situation.
    • Realistic group conflict theory, developed from the work of Muzafer Sherif in the 1950s, said that conflict is caused by real competition between groups for limited resources, such as land, food, money or a prize.

    Henri Tajfel, a social psychologist born in Poland who worked in Britain, asked a sharper question. Is competition really necessary? Or can the simple feeling of belonging to a group be enough to produce favouritism and discrimination? The Falcons and the Oryx in the warm-up show why this is hard to answer in everyday life: there is a trophy and there are colours, so we cannot tell which one is doing the work.

    What social identity theory says

    Social identity theory

    Social identity theory (Tajfel and Turner, 1979) proposes that part of how we see ourselves, our social identity, comes from the groups we belong to. The theory can be summed up in six claims.

    ClaimIn plain words
    1Competition over resources is not always needed for conflict between groups to develop.
    2Simply seeing yourself as a member of one of two groups, called social categorisation, is enough to trigger in-group favouritism and out-group discrimination.
    3Categorising gives people a way to build a social identity. They define themselves as similar to or different from other groups, and as "better" or "worse" than them.
    4People try to achieve a positive social identity, because it raises their self-esteem.
    5A positive social identity depends on distinctiveness: the in-group has to look positively different from certain out-groups. The process of judging your group against others is social comparison.
    6When a social identity is not positive, people either try to leave the group or try to make it more positively distinct.

    Not to be confused with realistic group conflict theory. Social identity theory does not say that competition never matters. Claim 1 says it is not always necessary. The theory asks what is left of the conflict once the competition is taken away.

    The minimal group paradigm

    The evidence behind these claims came first. Years before social identity theory was published, Tajfel's team asked what the smallest possible condition for group favouritism was, so they needed groups that were real in the participants' minds and in no other way. The theory was later built partly to explain what they found. They used the minimal group paradigm. Participants are divided into two groups on a trivial basis, such as how they estimated dots on a screen, which painting they preferred, or even a coin toss. The members never meet as a group, they have no shared history, and there is nothing for the groups to compete over. If favouritism still appears, it cannot be explained by competition, friendship or a shared past. It has to come from the categorisation itself.

    The paradigm used matrices: rows of paired numbers that represent points, later turned into small amounts of real money. In each column, one number is the reward for one person and the other number is the reward for a second person. By choosing a column, a participant decides how to share out the points. Participants never gave points to themselves, so self-interest could not explain their choices.

    Key study 1: Tajfel et al. (1971), Experiment 1, minimal groups

    AimTo test whether being placed in a group on a trivial basis is enough to produce in-group favouritism, when there is no competition and no personal gain. (The findings later became evidence for claim 2.)
    Participants64 boys aged 14 to 15 from one comprehensive school in Bristol, England. They took part in eight groups of eight, and the boys in each group knew each other well.
    ProcedureA laboratory experiment in two stages. Stage 1, categorisation: the boys saw 40 slides showing clusters of dots and estimated how many dots each cluster contained. They were then told that they would be grouped as "overestimators" or "underestimators". In reality, the grouping had nothing to do with their answers. Stage 2, sharing rewards: each boy sat alone in a cubicle with a booklet of matrices and shared out points, worth real money, between two other boys, who were identified only by a code number and their group. In some matrices both boys were from the same group; in others, one was from the boy's own group and one was from the other group.
    FindingsWhen the choice was between a member of their own group and a member of the other group, most boys gave more points to the in-group member. Their choices were not usually the most extreme ones, but on average they leaned away from the fairest option and towards their own group. When both boys were from the same group, choices stayed close to fairness.
    ConclusionSocial categorisation alone, with no competition, no history and no personal gain, was enough to produce in-group favouritism. This supports claim 2.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example of social identity theory); Paper 1 Section B (social categorisation and in-group favouritism); Paper 1 Section C on causality, bias or measurement; Paper 2 Section B (a laboratory experiment).
    Evaluate it withCausality: the groups differed only in their label, so the categorisation is the most likely cause of the favouritism. Measurement: the matrices measure favouritism precisely and in a way that is easy to repeat, but sharing points in a booklet is not the same as discrimination in daily life. Bias: all the participants were teenage boys from one school in one city, so the findings may not generalise to girls, adults or other cultures. Perspective: the forced choice between two groups may have suggested that the boys were expected to compete (a demand characteristic).

    Key study 2: Tajfel et al. (1971), Experiment 2, maximum difference

    The second experiment, published in the same paper, went further. It asked what the boys were trying to achieve when they favoured their group.

    AimTo test whether people care more about their group doing well, or about their group doing better than the other group. (The findings later became evidence for claim 5, distinctiveness.)
    Participants48 boys of the same age from the same school, tested in three groups of 16.
    ProcedureThe boys looked at pairs of abstract paintings, one by Paul Klee and one by Wassily Kandinsky, and said which they preferred. They were told they had been grouped by their preference; in fact, the grouping was random. Working alone, they then completed matrices built so that three different strategies could be told apart: maximum joint profit (the biggest total for both boys together), maximum in-group profit (the most for the in-group member, whatever the other boy gets) and maximum difference (the biggest gap in favour of the in-group member).
    FindingsMaximum joint profit had little influence on the boys' choices. Maximum difference had a strong influence. Many boys chose options that gave the in-group member fewer points than they could have had, as long as the in-group member came out ahead of the out-group member.
    ConclusionThe boys were willing to give up profit for their own group in order to make it positively distinct from the other group. This supports claim 5: a positive social identity depends on being better than, not just doing well.
    What happened nextThe minimal group paradigm was repeated many times with other ages, other countries and other ways of forming groups. Follow-up studies found that the effect of categorisation remained even when participants were told openly that the groups were random.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section B (social comparison and distinctiveness); Paper 1 Section C on causality or perspective.
    Evaluate it withCausality: random allocation means that painting preference could not explain the choices. Measurement: the matrices were designed to separate the three strategies, which makes the result about distinctiveness more convincing than a simple "more or less" measure. Bias: the same narrow sample of teenage boys from one school. Perspective: it shows what happens in an artificial setting with no history between the groups; real groups have histories, power differences and real resources at stake.

    How useful is social identity theory?

    Strengths. The theory has wide explanatory power.

    • It explains why favouritism and discrimination can appear even when nothing is being competed for.
    • It helps explain how members of lower-status groups respond to their position, for example by trying to leave the group or by finding new ways to see it positively (claim 6).
    • It helps explain stereotyping: if distinctiveness matters for self-esteem, stereotypes that make other groups look worse may matter too.
    • It has led to practical strategies for reducing conflict between groups (see the next section).
    • The experiments behind it were tightly controlled and have been replicated many times.

    Limitations. Researchers have raised three main problems.

    • The self-esteem hypothesis is unresolved. The theory predicts that self-esteem should rise when your group is judged better, and that members of low-status groups will look for ways to show their group in a better light. Both predictions have received little consistent support (Brown, 2000).
    • The positive and negative asymmetry. When studies replace rewards with something unpleasant, such as exposure to an unpleasant noise, in-group favouritism and out-group discrimination are much weaker. The effect appears more when people share out good things than when they share out bad things.
    • Explaining is easier than predicting. The theory is better at explaining a rivalry after it has happened than at predicting exactly how two groups will behave in a given situation.

    Quick lens: perspective. Keep these questions open. You will come back to them in the Section C plan.

    • The theory starts with a social fact (you belong to a group) and ends with a social outcome (how you treat others), but it explains the link through a mental process: it is perceived membership that matters. What does the theory gain by being both social and cognitive?
    • Aggressive personality explanations look at the person. Realistic group conflict theory looks at the competition. How would each describe the Falcons and the Oryx differently from social identity theory?
    • In Chapter 4 (cultural dimensions), social identity theory predicted trust in the in-group more clearly for individualist participants than for collectivist ones. Does a theory look the same from every culture?

    Using the theory to change behaviour

    If belonging alone can produce rivalry, the theory also suggests how to reduce it. Researchers have proposed four strategies.

    StrategyWhat it doesSchool example
    Make membership less noticeableWeakens the link between the group and the person's identity.Take away the team colours and names once the competition is over.
    Cut across the groupsIntroduces a new category that mixes members of the old groups.Two groups that clash are split into mixed teams for a new project.
    Redraw the boundaryWidens the in-group so that it includes the old out-group.Two rival classes become one team representing the whole grade.
    Face a shared third partyFormer rivals join together against someone else.Both rival teams combine to play a visiting school.

    Because new identities are so easy to create, any plan that uses them should be weighed carefully, with the costs set against the benefits. Look again at the last strategy. Does it solve the problem, or does it move it somewhere else?

    Real life: us and them, online

    Online fan groups, gaming communities and group chats form in minutes. Each one has a name, in-jokes, an emoji or a badge that shows who is in and who is out. Nobody has to compete for anything. Belonging is enough to start the language of "us" and "them".

    During the reading: think of an online group you belong to (describe the group, not the people in it). What marks the "us"? Is anything real at stake, or is it only belonging? What would social identity theory predict about how members talk about the "them"?

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to social identity theory
    CausalityThe minimal group paradigm removes competition and history, so categorisation is the most likely cause of the favouritism; whether the same cause operates in real groups is much harder to show.
    MeasurementFavouritism is measured as points shared out in matrices: precise and repeatable, but a long way from discrimination in everyday life. Self-esteem, the theory's key motive, is hard to measure and the evidence for it is weak.
    BiasThe classic samples were teenage boys from one British school, and the forced choice between two groups may have created demand characteristics.
    ChangeThe theory suggests ways to reduce rivalry, such as cutting across groups or redrawing the boundary, and claim 6 describes how people respond when their group's identity is not positive.
    PerspectiveThe theory is both social and cognitive; personality explanations and realistic group conflict theory offer different perspectives on the same rivalry, and the theory may look different from collectivist cultures.
    ResponsibilityBecause group identities are so easy to create, schools, teams and online platforms that create them are responsible for the rivalry they may produce. Researchers must use deception, such as false group labels, carefully and debrief participants.

    Rung 4: One more question

    The finding: In Tajfel et al. (1971), boys gave more points to a member of their own group than to a member of the other group, even though the groups had been formed on a trivial basis.

    A first reaction: "So belonging to a group makes people discriminate." Before you accept that, ask one more question.

    • On average, did the boys choose the most extreme option for their group, or something between fairness and favouritism? Does the finding show that people always favour their group, or that on average they lean towards it?
