On this page

    Aim

    Read for what you need, note it in your own words

    The IB guidance is clear: abstracts alone cannot support an EE. When you read a study, focus on the procedure and the discussion, and describe it accurately and concisely. Statistical details are rarely needed.

    By the end of this stage you can:

    • read a journal article in an order that saves time
    • record the details you need to describe and evaluate a study
    • paraphrase properly and avoid accidental plagiarism
    • build a literature matrix that shows how your studies compare
    Our example questionTo what extent does taking notes by hand rather than on a laptop improve students' learning from lectures?
    Read smart

    How to read a journal article

    1. Title and abstract. Is it relevant? Decide whether to keep reading. Never cite from the abstract alone.
    2. End of the introduction. Find the aims and hypotheses: what exactly were they testing?
    3. Discussion. What did they conclude, how cautious were they, and what limitations do they admit?
    4. Method. Who took part, how were they allocated, and how were the key variables measured? This is where most evaluation comes from.
    5. Results. Skim for the main finding in words. You rarely need to report the statistics.
    Two passes

    First pass: steps 1 to 3, about ten minutes, to decide whether it is worth your time. Second pass: the method, slowly, with your notes template open.

    Your own words

    Paraphrase, do not patchwrite

    Patchwriting means keeping the original sentence and swapping a few words. Even with a citation, it is too close to the source. A real paraphrase changes the structure and the words, keeps the meaning, and still cites the source.

    1. Read the passage until you understand it.
    2. Close it, and write the idea from memory in your own words.
    3. Check it against the original: is the meaning the same and the wording different?
    4. Add the citation.
    Mark quotations in your notes

    Most accidental plagiarism starts in notes, when copied words lose their quotation marks. Put every copied phrase in quotation marks with a page number the moment you write it down.

    See the pattern

    Build a literature matrix

    A literature matrix is a table with one row per study and one column per field. Read across a row to recall a study; read down a column to see patterns: which studies used the same measure, which samples dominate, where the findings disagree. Those patterns become your discussion.

    Exercise 1 · 20 minutes

    Abstract versus full text

    Below is a fictional study, written for practice. Read the abstract first and write down what you would conclude. Then read the method and discussion extracts and list everything the abstract did not tell you.

    Hadley and Okafor (2021). Typing notes harms learning: An experiment.

    Fictional study · Abstract

    We compared students who took lecture notes on laptops with students who wrote notes by hand. Laptop users scored significantly lower on a test of the lecture content. The findings suggest that typing notes harms learning and that schools should consider limiting laptop use in lessons.

    Method (extract)

    Fictional study

    Forty students from one introductory psychology course took part for course credit. Students on the left side of the lecture room used laptops; students on the right side wrote by hand. Laptop users kept Wi-Fi and messaging switched on; the handwriting group had no devices. All students watched a 12-minute video and took a test 10 minutes later. Conceptual answers were scored by the first author, who knew each student's condition.

    Discussion (extract)

    Fictional study

    The difference appeared on conceptual questions only; factual scores were similar. The laptop group had lower average grades in the course before the study, and the difference became smaller when prior grades were taken into account. We cannot rule out that access to messaging, rather than typing itself, explains the lower scores.

    Model answer

    Try the exercise first, then compare.

    What the abstract leaves out.

    • Groups were not randomly allocated. Students chose by where they sat, so the two groups may have differed before the study began. The discussion admits the laptop group had lower average prior grades.
    • A confound. The laptop group had Wi-Fi and messaging switched on; the handwriting group had no devices. Any difference could be distraction, not typing.
    • Measurement. Conceptual answers were scored by one researcher who knew each student's condition: a risk of researcher bias, with no second rater.
    • Generalisation. 40 students from one course, taking part for course credit, tested 10 minutes after a 12-minute video. This is not how students revise from notes.
    • The size of the claim. The abstract says typing "harms learning", but the difference appeared only on conceptual questions and shrank once prior grades were taken into account.

    The lesson. An abstract is an advertisement for a paper. The method and discussion are where you find out what the study can really support.

    Paraphrase practice

    Paraphrase the last sentence of the discussion extract above in your own words, with an APA citation. Then compare: is yours a paraphrase or patchwriting?

    Model answer

    Try the exercise first, then compare.

    Patchwriting (too close)

    The authors argue that the difference may have been caused by distraction rather than the act of typing, because the laptop group had access to messaging during the lecture (Hadley & Okafor, 2021).

    A real paraphrase

    Hadley and Okafor (2021) accept that their laptop users could message during the talk, so distraction, not typing itself, may explain the lower scores.

    The strong version changes the sentence structure and the words, keeps the meaning, and still cites the source. A paraphrase always needs a citation, even when every word is your own.

    Check yourself

    Five quick questions

    Which two parts of an article does the IB guidance say to focus on?

    The procedure (method) and the discussion.

    Do you need to report t-values and p-values in your essay?

    Rarely. Report what was found in words and describe the method concisely and accurately.

    What is patchwriting?

    Keeping the original sentence structure and swapping a few words. It is too close to the source, even with a citation.

    Does a paraphrase need a citation?

    Yes. The idea belongs to the source, even when every word is yours.

    What can a literature matrix show that separate notes cannot?

    Patterns across studies: shared measures, dominant samples, and where findings disagree. These feed your discussion.
