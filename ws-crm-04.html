    When a true experiment is not possible

    A true experiment has two features: the researcher manipulates the IV, and participants are randomly allocated to conditions. Together these let the researcher claim that the IV caused any difference in the DV. But random allocation is not always possible, and it is not always ethical.

    • It is impossible when the IV is something participants already have: age, gender, first language, whether they are bilingual, which school they attend. Nobody can be randomly allocated to being 15 or being a Grade 12 student.
    • It is unethical when the IV would harm people: you cannot randomly allocate children to grow up in poverty, or teenagers to lose sleep for a term, or anyone to a stressful life event.
    • It is impractical when the IV operates on whole communities or over years, such as a new national curriculum or a change in school start times.

    In each case, researchers can still compare groups, but they must accept that the comparison is weaker evidence of cause and effect.

    Quasi-experiments and natural experiments

    Experiments

    A quasi-experiment looks like an experiment, with conditions and a measured DV, but participants are not randomly allocated to the conditions. Often the groups already exist: Grade 9 versus Grade 12 students, bilingual versus monolingual students, students who chose to revise with music versus those who chose silence. The researcher may still control the setting and the task, but cannot control who is in which group.

    A natural experiment is a kind of quasi-experiment in which the IV changes in the real world without the researcher's involvement. A city introduces a new reading programme and a neighbouring city does not; a community gets broadband internet for the first time; a school changes its start time. The researcher measures the effect but decided nothing about who was exposed. In IB terms, natural experiments come under the quasi-experiment umbrella.

    True experimentQuasi-experimentNatural experiment
    Random allocation?YesNo: groups exist already or are formed another wayNo
    Who controls the IV?The researcherOften the researcher controls the task, but not who is in which groupNobody in the study: it changes naturally
    Evidence of cause and effectStrongest, if well controlledWeaker: the groups may differ in other waysWeaker, for the same reason
    Ecological validityOften lowerVariesOften high: real people in real settings
    Why use it?To test cause and effect with high internal validityWhen random allocation is impossible or unethicalWhen the change happens anyway and would be unethical or impossible to create

    The central evaluation point for quasi and natural experiments is always the same: without random allocation, the groups may differ in many ways at once, so a confounding variable could explain the result. Grade 12 students differ from Grade 9 students in age, but also in years of schooling, exam experience and motivation. Strong answers name a likely confound and explain how it could produce the result on its own.

    Key study: Charlton et al. (2000), television arrives on St Helena

    AimTo find out whether the arrival of broadcast television changed children's behaviour, in particular whether it increased anti-social behaviour.
    SettingSt Helena, a remote island in the South Atlantic that received broadcast television for the first time in 1995. This made a natural experiment possible: the IV (television) arrived whether or not researchers were there.
    ProcedureBefore television arrived, the free play of children aged 3 to 8 was video-recorded in the playgrounds of two schools. Five years after television's arrival, children of the same age were recorded in the same playgrounds. The recordings were coded for pro-social acts (such as sharing and turn-taking) and anti-social acts (such as hitting and kicking), and the before and after measures were compared.
    FindingsMost comparisons showed no significant change. Of those that did, five showed a decrease in pro-social behaviour, two showed an increase in pro-social behaviour (boys only) and two showed a decrease in anti-social behaviour (boys only). There was no increase in anti-social behaviour.
    ConclusionTelevision did not make these children more aggressive. Parents and teachers suggested that strong community expectations about behaviour on the island may have protected children from any effect.
    Use it forThis chapter (natural experiments); observation and coding (CRM-05); social learning theory (CL-02).
    Evaluate it withCausality: the IV could not be manipulated or randomly allocated, and many other things on the island changed over five years. Bias: the after group was not the same children, so differences could be between cohorts. Perspective: the result may say more about this small, close-knit community than about television in general.

    Ethics in psychological research

    Ethics

    Ethics is not a checklist; it is a judgement. Researchers weigh the likely benefits of the knowledge against the likely costs to participants. This is a cost-benefit analysis. A small, temporary discomfort may be justified if the study could help many people; serious or lasting harm is not justified by any benefit. The main guidelines are:

    GuidelineWhat it means
    Informed consentParticipants are told enough about the study, before it starts, to decide freely whether to take part. Children need a parent or guardian's consent as well as their own agreement.
    Right to withdrawParticipants can stop at any time, without giving a reason or losing anything. Agreeing to take part does not remove this right.
    Protection from harmParticipants should not be exposed to physical or psychological harm greater than in everyday life. If distress appears, the researcher responds, and stops the study if necessary.
    Confidentiality and anonymityPersonal data are kept secure. Where possible, results are anonymised so individuals cannot be identified.
    DeceptionAvoided unless it is essential, causes no harm, and is fully explained afterwards.
    DebriefingAt the end, participants are told the true aim, any deception and how their data will be used, and are given the chance to withdraw their data.

    Ethics also shapes the results. Giving full information may change how participants behave; the right to withdraw may leave a group that differs from the one you started with; anonymity can make people answer more honestly.

    Ethics for class practicals and the IA

    Ethics

    The IB sets extra rules for the studies you run and design yourself. For class practicals:

    • A teacher supervises every practical, and a student-led practical needs the teacher's approval first.
    • Consent must be explicit and in writing; for anyone under 16, a parent or guardian must also give written consent.
    • Nothing may cause anxiety, stress, pain or discomfort.
    • Partial deception is allowed only when knowing the aim would spoil the study, when it does no harm, and when participants are fully debriefed and can withdraw their data.
    • Conformity and obedience studies are never allowed, and animals are not used.
    • Researchers must be sensitive to local, national and international cultures.

    The IA is a proposal, so you do not collect data, but it must still be a study that an ethics committee would approve. Some topics are off-limits altogether (for example abuse or self-harm), and your teacher will advise on topics that are sensitive here in the UAE.

    Ethics and quasi-experiments belong in the same chapter for a reason: ethics is often why researchers use a quasi or natural design. You cannot randomly allocate teenagers to lose sleep, so you compare schools that already start at different times, and accept that your causal claim will be weaker. Ethics limits the method, and the method limits the conclusion.

    Real life: questions that need natural experiments

    Real life
    • School start times. Some school systems have moved to later start times. Comparing students before and after the change, or with schools that did not change, tests whether later starts improve sleep and attendance without asking anyone to lose sleep.
    • New technology. When a region gets fast internet for the first time, researchers can compare how young people spend their time before and after.
    • Bilingual education. Comparing students in bilingual and English-only programmes is a quasi-experiment: families chose the programme, so the groups may differ in other ways.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies
    CausalityWithout random allocation, groups may differ in several ways at once, so a quasi or natural experiment gives weaker evidence of cause and effect than a true experiment.
    ResponsibilityEthical guidelines protect participants, and they are often the reason a true experiment cannot be run. A cost-benefit analysis decides what is acceptable.
    BiasPre-existing groups can differ systematically. The right to withdraw can leave a biased sample if certain kinds of people drop out.
    ChangeNatural experiments often study change over time, such as before and after a new technology or policy, which raises the question of what else changed at the same time.

    Classify the design

    1. Researchers want to know whether a mindfulness programme reduces exam anxiety. They randomly allocate 60 Grade 11 students to a six-week after-school mindfulness programme or a study-skills workshop of the same length, then give both groups the same anxiety questionnaire.
    2. A national youth sport programme is introduced in some cities but not others, because of funding decisions made before the researchers were involved. A year later, researchers compare teenagers' well-being scores in cities with and without the programme.
    3. Researchers ask 80 university students to choose whether to revise with music or in silence for two weeks, then give everyone the same test.

    Using AI to learn

    Make AI question you, not write for you

    Try one of these after you have attempted the chapter yourself:

    • "Describe six short studies, one at a time. For each, ask me whether it is a true experiment, a quasi-experiment or a natural experiment, and why. Wait for my answer."
    • "Give me a study design that has an ethical problem. Ask me to find the problem and suggest a safer design, then tell me what my design would lose."
    • "Here is my evaluation of a quasi-experiment. Point out any sentence where I claim cause and effect too strongly. Do not rewrite it."

    Remember: check every study an AI tool names against this chapter or your textbook, because these tools sometimes invent studies and figures. Never paste in your class data or anyone's name, and do not use AI to draft or mark your IA or EE.
