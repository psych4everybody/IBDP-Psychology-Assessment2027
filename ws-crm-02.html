    Why general sentences do not earn marks

    Two exam tasks are built around concepts: the Paper 1 Section C essay and the Paper 2 Section B evaluation of an unseen study. In both, the marks rise as your links between the concept and the content become more relevant, more developed and more specific to the question. At the top of the range, examiners want analysis that is clearly about this study or this area of study, and a conclusion that follows from your argument.

    The trap is the general sentence. "Experiments can show cause and effect." "Lab studies lack ecological validity." "More research is needed." Each is true, and each could appear in almost any answer, which is exactly the problem: it shows you know the vocabulary, not that you can think with it. General sentences describe. Specific sentences analyse.

    Remember from CRM-01: exam questions use only the concept word, any idea can be linked to any concept, and it is the explanation of the link that earns the marks. This chapter gives you a routine for building those explanations.

    The swap test

    Before you keep a sentence about a concept, try to swap the study or topic in it for a different one. If the sentence still works, it is too general. A sentence passes when it could only belong to this answer, because it uses a detail of this study, this topic or this question.

    Fails the swap testPasses the swap test
    The sample was biased, so the results cannot be generalised.All 39 participants were students in Forer's own class, rating a profile handed out by their teacher, so they may have rated it more generously than strangers reading it online would.
    This was an experiment, so it shows cause and effect.Because the groups in Loftus and Palmer saw the same films and differed only in the verb, the difference in speed estimates can be put down to the wording of the question.
    The study used self-report, which may not be valid.Students rated the profile's accuracy on a 0 to 5 scale, which measures how much they liked the description as much as how well it fitted them.

    Notice what the passing sentences add: a detail from the study, and a consequence for what the study can or cannot show.

    The five rungs

    The five rungs are a ladder from understanding the question to committing to an answer. Do them in order. In every Concept Workout on this site, each rung has guiding questions that point the way without giving you the answer.

    RungWhat you doWhat it trains
    1. DecodeBreak the question into its context, area of study, concept and command term.Answering the question that was asked
    2. DefineDefine the topic and the concept in one sentence each, as they apply to this question.Accurate knowledge and terminology
    3. UpgradeRewrite a sentence that fails the swap test so that it passes.Relevant, specific links between the concept and the content
    4. One more questionTake a finding and ask one more question before you accept it.Critical analysis
    5. CommitWrite a conclusion sentence that names a condition.A reasoned, clearly stated conclusion

    For Paper 1 Section C, the workouts ask you to write plans, not full essays. A plan you can explain is worth more in revision than an essay you have memorised.

    Rung 1: Decode

    A Section C question is built from four parts. Find all four before you write anything.

    PartWhat to look forExample
    ContextOne of the four contexts, usually at the start: "In the context of..."learning and cognition
    Area of studyA topic from that context's table in the guide. Only the main areas are used, never the subheadings that group them.schema theory
    ConceptOne of the six concepts, named exactly.causality
    Command termEvaluate (weigh strengths and limitations), discuss (offer a balanced review with a range of arguments) or to what extent (consider the merits of a claim and reach a judgement).to what extent

    Then ask one more thing: which idea from another chapter sits close to this question but is not what it asks? Writing about the wrong neighbour, for example describing a memory model when the question asks about schema theory, is one of the most common ways to lose marks.

    Rung 2: Define

    Define the topic and the concept in one sentence each, as they apply to this question. A dictionary definition of causality ("one thing causing another") is accurate but does no work. A working definition sets up your argument: "Causality here means whether research can show that activating a schema changes what people remember, rather than only showing that the two go together."

    A good test: could your definition only appear in an essay on this question? If yes, it is doing its job.

    Rung 3: Upgrade

    This is the swap test in action. Take a sentence that fails and rebuild it in four moves:

    1. Name the concept and the idea you are using, for example causality and the role of a control group.
    2. Point to the exact feature of the study that raises it, for example "the groups saw identical films and heard different verbs".
    3. Explain the consequence for the conclusion, for example "so the wording, not the film, explains the difference in estimates".
    4. Connect to a second concept if you can, for example "but this only shows a change in the estimate participants gave, which is a measurement question".
    Weak: Experiments control variables, so this study shows causality.
    Upgraded: Because the only difference between the groups in Loftus and Palmer was the verb in one question, the higher speed estimates after "smashed" can be attributed to the wording; whether the wording changed the memory itself, or only the answer, is a separate question about what was measured.

    Rung 4: One more question

    Strong analysis does not accept a finding at face value. Before you accept a conclusion, ask one more question. Each concept suggests its own kind of question:

    ConceptOne more question to ask
    BiasWho took part, who was left out, and who interpreted the results?
    CausalityWhat else changed at the same time? Which way does the arrow point?
    ChangeWould this change have happened anyway? Does it last?
    MeasurementWhat exactly was counted or rated, and is that the same as the thing we care about?
    PerspectiveHow would a different approach explain the same finding?
    ResponsibilityWho could be harmed by this research, or by the way its findings are used?

    Then link it: most good questions about one concept turn out to raise another. That link is often the most impressive sentence in an answer.

    Rung 5: Commit

    A conclusion must take a position, and the strongest positions name a condition: when, for whom or in what way the claim holds.

    Research can establish causality in the study of schema theory when [condition], because [reason].

    Some conclusions are ruled out because they do not commit to anything:

    • "Experiments show cause and effect." (Which experiments? Shown how strongly?)
    • "It depends." (Unless you say on what.)
    • "More research is needed." (What research, and what would it show?)

    The study used in the workout: Loftus and Palmer (1974)

    AimTo find out whether the wording of a question asked after an event changes what people report about it.
    Experiment 145 university students in the USA watched seven short films of traffic accidents. Afterwards they answered questions, including the critical one: "About how fast were the cars going when they ___ each other?" Groups of nine heard different verbs: smashed, collided, bumped, hit or contacted. The mean speed estimate was 40.8 mph for "smashed" and 31.8 mph for "contacted", with the other verbs in between.
    Experiment 2150 students watched one film of a crash. One group was asked the speed question with "smashed", one with "hit", and a control group was not asked about speed. A week later all were asked whether they had seen any broken glass. There was none in the film. 16 of the 50 in the "smashed" group said yes, compared with 7 in the "hit" group and 6 in the control group.
    ConclusionInformation given after an event can change what people report, and possibly what they remember. This is often explained with schema theory: the word "smashed" may activate a schema of a violent crash, which then fills in details, such as broken glass, that were never seen.
    Use it forPractising the rungs (this chapter); the experiment as a research method (CRM-03); schema theory (CL-03).

    Common traps

    TrapWhat it looks likeFix
    Concept-droppingThe concept word appears once, in the first line, and never again.Keep returning to the concept in every paragraph, with a detail and a consequence each time.
    Listing related terms"This links to bias: sampling bias, researcher bias, confirmation bias..."Choose one idea and explain it properly. One developed link beats five names.
    Perspective called bias"The biological approach is biased because it ignores culture."Say that relying on one perspective can give a reductionist explanation, and explain what it leaves out.
    The universal limitation"The sample was small, so the findings cannot be generalised."Say who the sample was, who the conclusion is about, and why the difference matters for this finding.
    The empty conclusion"Overall, there are strengths and limitations."Name a condition: when, for whom, or in what way the claim holds.

    Rung 4: One more question

    The finding: In Loftus and Palmer (1974), participants who heard "smashed" gave higher speed estimates than participants who heard "contacted".

    A first reaction: "So leading questions change what people remember, because they activate a schema." Before you accept that, ask one more question.

    • Did the verb change the memory, or only the answer participants gave when they were unsure of the speed?
    • In the second experiment, participants were asked a week later about broken glass. What does that add to the causal claim?
    • The study measured what people said. Did it measure a schema at all, or is the schema an explanation added afterwards?

    Using AI to learn

    Make AI question you, not write for you

    Try one of these after you have attempted the chapter yourself:

    • "Here are five sentences I wrote about a psychology study. For each one, tell me whether it passes the swap test (could it be pasted into an answer about a different study?). Do not rewrite them."
    • "Give me a Paper 1 Section C style question for IB Psychology. Ask me to decode it into context, area of study, concept and command term, then tell me if I missed anything."
    • "I will give you a finding from a study. Ask me one more question about it, linked to one of the six concepts, and wait for my answer."

    Remember: check every study an AI tool names against this chapter or your textbook, because these tools sometimes invent studies and figures. Do not use AI to draft or mark your IA or EE.
