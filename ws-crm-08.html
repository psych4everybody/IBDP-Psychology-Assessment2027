    Reliability: consistency

    Reliability

    A measure is reliable if it gives consistent results. Reliability is checked in different ways depending on the method:

    • Test-retest reliability: the same people take the same test on two occasions; their scores should be similar if the thing measured has not changed. The quiz in the warm-up fails this test.
    • Inter-rater (inter-observer) reliability: two or more researchers score or code the same behaviour independently and agree (CRM-05).
    • Replication: other researchers repeat the whole study, ideally with new participants, and find the same result. A finding that replicates in many samples is far more trustworthy than one striking result.

    Reliability is improved by standardisation (the same instructions, materials and procedure every time), clear operational definitions and training. But reliability is not enough on its own: a bathroom scale that always reads 2 kg heavy is perfectly reliable and always wrong.

    Validity: measuring what you claim to measure

    Validity

    Validity is about whether a study measures what it claims to measure, and whether its conclusions are justified. The guide lists five kinds:

    KindThe question it asksExample
    Internal validityDid the IV, and nothing else, cause the change in the DV?A confounding variable, such as testing one group in the morning and the other after lunch, lowers internal validity.
    External validityDo the findings apply beyond this study, to other people, settings and times?A memory effect found with university students learning word lists may not hold for older adults remembering real events. Ecological validity (real-life settings) is one part of it.
    Content validityDoes the measure cover the whole of what it should, and only that?An anxiety questionnaire that asks only about physical symptoms misses worrying thoughts.
    Face validityDoes the measure look, on the surface, as if it measures what it claims to?Asking "How stressed do you feel?" has face validity for stress. Face validity is the weakest kind: looking right is not the same as being right.
    Construct validityDoes the measure really capture the underlying construct, as theory describes it?Does a speed estimate in Loftus and Palmer measure memory, or uncertainty and the wish to give a sensible answer?

    A useful way to remember the difference: reliability is hitting the same spot every time; validity is hitting the right spot. A measure can be reliable without being valid, but it cannot be valid without being reasonably reliable.

    Generalisability

    Generalisability

    Generalisability is the extent to which findings can be applied beyond the sample and setting of the study. It depends on:

    • The sample: how well it represents the population the conclusion is about (CRM-07). Much published psychology has relied on university students in Western, educated, industrialised, rich and democratic countries, sometimes shortened to "WEIRD" samples, who are a small and unusual slice of humanity.
    • The setting: whether behaviour in a laboratory or online task reflects behaviour in daily life.
    • The time: whether a finding from the 1970s still applies now, for example before and after smartphones.
    • The measures: whether the same measure means the same thing in a different culture or language.

    A strong evaluation point names exactly who was studied, who the conclusion is about, and why the difference matters for this particular finding, rather than simply saying "the sample was small".

    Researcher and participant biases

    Researcher and participant biases
    BiasWhat happensControls
    Researcher bias (researcher effects, observer bias)The researcher's expectations influence how the study is run or how data are interpreted, often without the researcher noticing.Standardised procedures and instructions; double-blind designs; precise coding schemes; more than one coder; reflexivity.
    Confirmation biasNoticing and reporting evidence that supports the hypothesis while overlooking evidence against it.Stating hypotheses and analysis plans in advance; reporting all results, not just the striking ones.
    Demand characteristicsParticipants guess the aim and change their behaviour to fit (or spoil) it.Keeping the aim general until the debrief; independent measures designs; single-blind procedures.
    Social desirabilityParticipants present themselves in a good light, especially in self-report.Anonymity; neutral wording; checking self-report against other measures.
    Sampling biasThe sample differs systematically from the population.Random or stratified sampling; describing the sample honestly and limiting conclusions to match.

    Quality in qualitative research

    Credibility, transferability and reflexivity

    Interviews, focus groups and unstructured observations produce words and meanings, not numbers. Their quality is judged with related but different terms.

    • Credibility: do the findings genuinely reflect what participants experienced and meant? Strategies: triangulation (checking findings across different methods, sources or researchers); member checking (asking participants whether the summary of their interview is accurate); spending enough time with participants; and a clear record of how codes and themes were reached.
    • Transferability: could the findings apply to other people or settings? Qualitative studies do not claim to generalise in a statistical sense. Instead, researchers give a thick description of the participants and context, so that readers can judge for themselves whether the findings fit another setting.
    • Reflexivity: researchers think about, and report, how their own background, beliefs, expectations and relationship to participants may have shaped the questions they asked and how they interpreted the answers. It does not remove bias, but it makes it visible. The IA asks you to do exactly this about your own proposal.
    • Bias in qualitative research is reduced by open, non-leading questions, reflexivity, triangulation, transparent reporting and, in some approaches, having a second researcher code part of the data.

    There is a useful parallel: credibility is close to internal validity, transferability to external validity, and checking codes with a second researcher is close to inter-rater reliability.

    Key study: Open Science Collaboration (2015), can psychology's findings be repeated?

    AimTo estimate how many published findings in psychology can be reproduced when the studies are run again.
    ProcedureAround 270 researchers worked together to repeat 100 experimental and correlational studies published in 2008 in three leading psychology journals, following the original methods as closely as possible and often consulting the original authors.
    Findings97% of the original studies had reported statistically significant results. Only 36% of the replications did. The effects found in the replications were, on average, considerably smaller than the original effects.
    ConclusionMany published findings are less reliable than they appear. Possible reasons include small samples, publication bias (journals favouring significant results) and flexible analysis choices.
    What happened nextThe project helped spark reforms: researchers increasingly register their hypotheses and analysis plans before collecting data, share their data openly, and publish replication studies.
    Use it forThis chapter (reliability and replication; publication bias); the concept of bias (CRM-01); evaluating any single study.
    Evaluate it withBias: publication bias means the published record overstates effects. Measurement: a replication can fail because the original effect was not real, or because the new sample or setting differed. Responsibility: researchers have a duty to report findings, including null results, honestly.

    Real life: judging claims

    Real life
    • A single study in the news. One striking result is weaker evidence than a finding that has been replicated many times by different teams.
    • Personality quizzes. A quiz that gives you a different "type" each time you take it has low test-retest reliability.
    • Job interviews. Structured interviews, with the same questions and scoring rules for every candidate, are generally more reliable than informal chats, which leave more room for interviewer bias.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies
    MeasurementReliability and the five kinds of validity are the core of measurement: is the tool consistent, and does it measure the right thing?
    BiasResearcher and participant biases, sampling bias and publication bias all push findings away from the truth. Reflexivity makes the researcher's own bias visible.
    CausalityInternal validity is the extent to which a study can support a causal claim; external validity is how far that claim reaches.
    PerspectiveQuantitative and qualitative research judge quality in different terms, which reflects different views of what good evidence is.

    Make AI question you, not write for you

    Try one of these after you have attempted the chapter yourself:

    • "Describe a study in three sentences. Ask me which kind of validity is most at risk and why. Wait for my answer, then tell me if another kind also applies."
