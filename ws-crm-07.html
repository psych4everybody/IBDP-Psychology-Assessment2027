    Populations, samples and sampling bias

    The target population is the whole group a researcher wants to draw conclusions about, for example Grade 11 students in the UAE. The sample is the smaller group that actually takes part. A sample is representative when it reflects the population on the characteristics that matter for the research question, such as age, gender, nationality, language or school type. The more representative the sample, the more confidently findings can be generalised.

    Sampling bias occurs when the sample differs from the population in a systematic way, so the findings are distorted in a predictable direction. In the warm-up, the people who chose to join the app study were already users who had kept the app and who were willing to spend four weeks on a study: they were probably more motivated and more positive about the app than users in general.

    The five sampling techniques

    Sampling techniques
    TechniqueHow participants are chosenStrengthLimitation
    RandomEvery member of the target population has an equal chance of being chosen, for example by a random number generator from a full listLeast sampling bias; most likely to be representativeNeeds a complete list of the population; people chosen may decline, which reintroduces bias
    StratifiedThe population is divided into subgroups (strata) that matter, such as year group or gender, and people are chosen randomly from each in the same proportions as the populationEvery important subgroup is represented in the right proportionNeeds detailed knowledge of the population; time-consuming
    OpportunityWhoever is available and willing at the time, such as one class. Also called convenience sampling.Quick, easy and cheap; the most common technique in practiceThose available may differ systematically from the population
    Self-selectedPeople volunteer, for example by answering an advertisementParticipants are willing and likely to complete the studyVolunteers may be more motivated, curious or affected by the topic than others
    SnowballExisting participants recruit further participants from people they knowReaches hard-to-find groups, such as people with a rare experience; builds trustThe sample comes from linked social networks, so it is unlikely to be representative

    Researchers in qualitative studies often describe purposive sampling: deliberately choosing people who have the experience being studied. It is not one of the five techniques in the guide, so if you use the term, explain it.

    Remember from CRM-03: random sampling (who is in the study) is different from random allocation (which condition they are placed in). A class can be an opportunity sample that is then randomly allocated to two conditions.

    Correlational studies

    Correlational studies

    A correlational study measures two or more variables, called co-variables, as they already are, without manipulating either, and tests whether they are related. The strength and direction of the relationship are given by a correlation coefficient (r), which runs from -1 to +1.

    • Positive correlation (r above 0): as one variable increases, so does the other, for example hours of revision and test scores.
    • Negative correlation (r below 0): as one increases, the other decreases, for example hours of sleep lost and memory test performance.
    • No correlation (r close to 0): no consistent straight-line relationship.

    The closer r is to +1 or -1, the stronger the relationship. As a rough guide, about 0.1 is weak, 0.3 moderate and 0.5 or above strong, though what counts as strong depends on the field. Correlations are shown on a scatterplot, which can also reveal curved relationships that r would miss (CRM-09).

    A correlation does not show cause, for two reasons:

    • Bidirectional ambiguity: if exercise and good mood go together, exercise may improve mood, or people in a good mood may exercise more, or both.
    • The third variable problem: ice cream sales and sunburn are positively correlated, but hot, sunny weather causes both.

    Correlational studies are still valuable. They can study variables that cannot be manipulated ethically, such as stress or screen time over years, and they show which relationships are worth testing with experiments.

    Case studies

    Case studies

    A case study is an in-depth investigation of a single person, group, organisation or event, often over a long period. Case studies usually combine several methods: interviews, observations, psychological tests, medical records and documents. The case is often unusual, which is exactly why it is studied.

    • Strengths: rich, detailed data; the only way to study rare conditions or events; can challenge an accepted theory with a single clear counter-example; generates hypotheses for later research.
    • Limitations: findings from one case may not generalise; much information may be retrospective and depend on memory; the researcher's interpretation plays a large role; anonymity is hard to protect when the case is unusual and well known.

    Correlational studies and case studies are both research methods in the guide, and they often appear as unseen studies in Paper 2 Section B. They are not class practical methods and cannot be used as the method for your IA, which must be an experiment, observation, survey/questionnaire or interview. They can, however, be the additional method you suggest in IA Criterion D, or when a Paper 2 Section A question asks you to suggest another method: the IB credits them there. A different type of the same method does not count as another method, so a semi-structured interview is not an alternative to a structured one.

    Key study: H.M., a case study of memory

    BackgroundIn 1953, a young man known in research as H.M. (Henry Molaison) had surgery to treat severe epilepsy. Parts of the medial temporal lobes on both sides of his brain, including most of the hippocampus, were removed.
    What was foundHis epilepsy improved, but he was left with severe difficulty forming new long-term memories of facts and events, while his short-term memory, his intelligence and many older memories were largely intact. Later testing by Brenda Milner showed that he could improve at a new motor skill, mirror drawing, over several days without remembering having practised it.
    Methods usedResearchers studied H.M. for decades, using memory tests, observation, interviews and, later, brain scans.
    ConclusionDifferent kinds of memory depend on different brain systems: the hippocampus is needed to form new memories of facts and events, but not to learn skills.
    Use it forThis chapter (case studies); biological factors in memory (CL-07); localization of function.
    Evaluate it withCausality: because the surgery is documented, the cause of his memory loss is unusually clear for a case study. Bias: one person, with a history of epilepsy and its treatment, may not represent how memory works in everyone. Responsibility: H.M. could not remember giving consent to each session, so consent and his privacy had to be protected by others.

    Real life: samples and correlations in the news

    Real life
    • Online polls. A website poll with 10,000 replies can be less representative than a random sample of 1,000, because only people who visit the site and care about the topic answer.
    • Health headlines. "People who drink coffee live longer" comes from correlational data. Coffee drinkers may differ in income, health and lifestyle.
    • App statistics. "Students who use our revision app score higher" says nothing about whether the app causes it if students chose whether to use it.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies
    BiasSampling bias limits how far findings can be generalised. Self-selected and opportunity samples are especially at risk.
    CausalityCorrelations show relationships, not causes, because of bidirectional ambiguity and third variables. Case studies may suggest causes but cannot test them.
    MeasurementCo-variables must be measured validly; a correlation is only as good as the measures behind it.
    PerspectiveCase studies give an in-depth, individual view; large correlational samples give a broad, general view. Each sees something the other misses.

    Using AI to learn

    Make AI question you, not write for you

    Try one of these after you have attempted the chapter yourself:

    • "Give me five research scenarios, one at a time, and ask me which sampling technique is being used and one limitation of it. Wait for my answer."
    • "Give me three correlations from everyday life. For each, ask me to suggest a third variable and explain the possible direction of cause."
    • "Here is my design for a study on music and studying. Ask me three questions an examiner might ask about my sampling and procedure. Do not rewrite it."

    Remember: check every study an AI tool names against this chapter or your textbook, because these tools sometimes invent studies and figures. Never paste in your class data or anyone's name, and do not use AI to draft or mark your IA or EE.
