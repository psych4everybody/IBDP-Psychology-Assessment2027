    What is a cognitive model?

    Cognitive models

    Nobody can open a skull and see a memory. What psychologists can see is behaviour: how many words a person recalls, which words they recall, and how quickly they answer. A cognitive model is a simplified description of a mental process that explains this behaviour. It is usually drawn as boxes (parts of the system, such as a store) and arrows (how information moves between them).

    A cognitive model is not a map of the brain. A box called "short-term memory" does not point to one place in your head. It is a claim about how the system behaves. Think of a map of the Dubai Metro. It leaves out every street and bend in the track, but it shows how the stations connect, which is what you need to plan a journey.

    Psychologists build cognitive models for three main reasons:

    • To explain. A model organises many findings into one picture.
    • To predict. A good model makes predictions that can be tested. If short-term memory is a separate store, then something that disrupts it should affect some memories but not others.
    • To be tested and changed. When a prediction fails, the model has to be changed or replaced.

    This chapter looks at two cognitive models of the same process, memory: the multi-store model and the working memory model.

    The multi-store model (Atkinson and Shiffrin, 1968)

    The multi-store model (MSM) proposes that memory is made of three separate stores. Information flows through them in a fixed order.

    1. Sensory memory. Everything you see, hear and touch enters sensory memory first. It holds a lot of information, but only for a very short time (less than a second for visual information). Only information you pay attention to moves on.
    2. Short-term memory (STM). STM holds the information you are aware of right now. It has a small capacity, often given as about seven items, plus or minus two (Miller, 1956). Without rehearsal, information in STM is lost within seconds. When Peterson and Peterson (1959) stopped people from rehearsing, most information was lost within about 18 seconds.
    3. Long-term memory (LTM). LTM holds information for long periods, possibly for a lifetime, and its capacity seems to be unlimited.

    The key process in the MSM is rehearsal. Repeating information keeps it in STM (maintenance rehearsal), and the more it is rehearsed, the more likely it is to be transferred to LTM.

    The MSM makes a clear prediction: STM and LTM are separate, so it should be possible to damage or disrupt one without affecting the other. Glanzer and Cunitz (1966), Key study 1 below, tested this with word lists. Brain-damaged patients offer another kind of test. The best known is patient HM, who could no longer form new long-term memories after brain surgery, although his short-term memory worked normally. You will study HM in detail in Chapter 7.

    Problems with the multi-store model

    • STM may not be one store. Shallice and Warrington (1970) described patient KF, who had brain damage after a motorcycle accident. His short-term memory for spoken numbers and letters was very poor, but his short-term memory for visual information was better, and his long-term memory still worked. If STM were a single store, damage to it should affect all kinds of information in the same way. This also raises a problem: if information must pass through STM to reach LTM, how could KF still learn new things?
    • Rehearsal is not the only route into LTM. We remember many things we never repeated to ourselves, such as what happened on our first day at a new school. Craik and Lockhart (1972) argued that thinking about meaning matters more than repetition.
    • STM does more than store. We use STM to work: doing mental arithmetic or following directions. The MSM describes STM as a waiting room, not a workspace.

    The working memory model (Baddeley and Hitch, 1974)

    Baddeley and Hitch kept the MSM's separate long-term store, but replaced the single STM box with a system of parts that work together. They called it working memory, because it is where we hold and work with information at the same time.

    • Central executive. The "boss" of the system. It directs attention, decides which tasks get resources, and coordinates the other parts. It has limited capacity and does not store information.
    • Phonological loop. Deals with sound and speech. It has two parts: the phonological store (the "inner ear"), which holds sounds for a short time, and the articulatory control process (the "inner voice"), which rehearses words by repeating them silently. Written words have to be turned into sound by the inner voice before they enter the store.
    • Visuospatial sketchpad. The "inner eye". It holds visual information (what things look like) and spatial information (where things are).
    • Episodic buffer. Added by Baddeley (2000). The original model could not explain how information from the loop, the sketchpad and LTM is combined into one experience. For example, people recall a meaningful sentence more easily than the same number of unrelated words, because they draw on LTM. The episodic buffer is a limited-capacity store that binds these kinds of information together and links working memory to LTM.

    The model's main prediction is about dual tasks. Two tasks that use the same part of the system will interfere with each other. Two tasks that use different parts can be done together with little interference. Saying the alphabet aloud while reading is hard, because both need the phonological loop. Saying it while picturing your bedroom is much easier. Baddeley and Hitch (1974) found that people could hold a short list of digits in mind while doing a reasoning task. The reasoning slowed down as the list grew longer, but people still made few errors, which suggested that STM is not one small store that fills up and stops.

    A special version of the dual task is articulatory suppression: repeating a meaningless word or number aloud, over and over. This keeps the inner voice busy, so it cannot rehearse. If a verbal task gets harder under suppression, the model says it depends on the phonological loop.

    Comparing the two models

    Extension. The guide no longer asks you to compare two cognitive models, but a comparison is a quick way to show what each model explains and what it misses, which is the value question the exam does ask.

    Similarities. Both are cognitive models: they describe memory as stores and processes, drawn as boxes and arrows, and they are based mainly on laboratory experiments with word lists and digits. Both agree that short-term (or working) memory has limited capacity and short duration, and that LTM is a separate store.

    Real-life examples

    • A one-time password. Your bank sends a six-digit code by text message, and you switch apps to type it in. Most people repeat the code under their breath. In the MSM, this is maintenance rehearsal keeping the code in STM. In the WMM, it is the inner voice refreshing the phonological store. If someone reads a phone number aloud at that moment, the code is often lost.
    • Revising with music. A student in Abu Dhabi revises history notes with a song with lyrics playing and keeps rereading the same paragraph. The words of the song and the words of the notes both need the phonological loop.
    • Finding your way on the Dubai Metro. A visitor holds a mental picture of the route and where to change lines (visuospatial sketchpad) while listening to station announcements (phonological loop). The WMM predicts these two tasks can run side by side.

    Key study 1: Glanzer and Cunitz (1966), the serial position effect

    AimTo test whether recall of the first and last words of a list depends on two different stores, as the multi-store model predicts.
    Participants46 Army enlisted men in the experiment described below.
    ProcedureParticipants were presented with lists of 15 words, one word at a time. After each list, they wrote down as many words as they could, in any order (free recall). There were three conditions, and each participant did all three (repeated measures): recall straight away, recall after a 10-second delay, and recall after a 30-second delay. During each delay, participants counted aloud from a number shown to them, which stopped them rehearsing the last words.
    FindingsWith immediate recall, participants remembered the first few words (the primacy effect) and the last few words (the recency effect) better than the middle words. This U-shaped curve is the serial position effect. Counting for 10 seconds reduced the recency effect, and after 30 seconds it disappeared. The primacy effect was not affected by the delay. In a separate experiment, more time between words improved recall of early and middle words but made little difference to the last words.
    ConclusionThe first words are rehearsed most and transferred to LTM, so they survive the delay. The last words are still in STM at the end of the list, so a short filled delay is enough to push them out. Two effects that can be changed separately suggest two separate stores, which supports the MSM.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example of a cognitive model making a testable prediction); Paper 1 Section B; Paper 1 Section C on causality or measurement.
    Evaluate it withCausality: the researchers controlled the delay, and the same men did every condition, so the loss of the recency effect can be linked to the filled delay. Measurement: the "stores" were never observed; they were inferred from which positions in a list were recalled. Word lists are also unlike everyday remembering. Bias: all participants were men from one military population, so the findings may not generalise to other groups.

    Key study 2: Baddeley, Thomson and Buchanan (1975), the word length effect

    AimTo test whether the capacity of short-term memory depends on how long words take to say, rather than on a fixed number of items.
    ParticipantsA series of eight small experiments, each with between 8 and 16 participants. Some were students at the University of Stirling in Scotland; others were paid volunteers from a research panel at the Applied Psychology Unit in Cambridge.
    ProcedureParticipants saw or heard lists of five words and recalled them straight away in the correct order (immediate serial recall). Lists contained either short words or long words. In one experiment, the words were names of countries, either short (for example, Chad, Burma, Malta) or long (for example, Somaliland, Czechoslovakia, Afghanistan). In some experiments, the researchers also measured how fast each participant could read words aloud. In the final experiments, some lists were recalled under articulatory suppression: participants counted from one to eight aloud, over and over, while the words were presented, either shown visually or read aloud.
    FindingsShort words were recalled better than long words. With country names, participants recalled on average 4.5 out of 8 five-word lists perfectly when the names were short, but fewer than 1 when they were long. Memory span matched the number of words a person could read aloud in about two seconds. Under articulatory suppression, the word length effect disappeared when the words were seen, but it remained when the words were heard, although recall was lower overall.
    ConclusionShort-term memory for words is limited by time (how much can be said in about two seconds), not just by a number of items. This supports a phonological loop with an inner voice that rehearses words. Articulatory suppression blocks the inner voice, so seen words cannot be turned into sound and rehearsed. Heard words seem to enter the phonological store directly.
    What happened nextThe word length effect became one of the main pieces of evidence for the phonological loop. Later researchers debated whether it is caused by how long words take to say or by other differences between long and short words, such as how complex they are.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example of a cognitive model); Paper 1 Section B; Paper 1 Section C on change, measurement or causality; Paper 2 Section B practice (a set of small, controlled experiments).
    Evaluate it withCausality: word length was controlled by the researchers and the same participants recalled both short and long lists, which supports a causal link between word length and recall. Measurement: the phonological loop was never observed; it was inferred from recall scores and reading speed. Bias: samples were small and mostly university students in the United Kingdom. Change: the study helped the WMM replace the single STM store of the MSM.

    How useful are cognitive models?

    Strengths. Cognitive models make testable predictions. The MSM predicted that STM and LTM could be separated, and Glanzer and Cunitz found that they could. The WMM predicted that two verbal tasks would interfere and that a verbal task and a visual task would not, and dual-task studies have supported this. They have practical uses: a teacher who knows working memory is limited can break instructions into short steps.

    Limitations. Models are simplified, so they can mislead. The MSM made STM and LTM look like single boxes, and later evidence showed that both are more complex. Some parts of models are vague: the central executive explains a lot, but it is hard to say what it is or to test it directly. The stores are never observed; they are inferred from recall scores, mostly in laboratory tasks with word or digit lists. Finally, box-and-arrow models describe what memory does, but they say little about the brain, emotion or culture. The statistician George Box put it simply: all models are wrong, but some are useful.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to cognitive models of memory
    MeasurementMemory stores cannot be seen, so they are inferred from what people recall, in what order, and how quickly.
    CausalityControlled experiments (changing the delay, the word length, or adding articulatory suppression) let researchers link one factor to a change in recall.
    BiasKey studies used small or narrow samples (Army men, university students), and word-list tasks favour a particular kind of memory.
    ChangeModels change as evidence accumulates: the single STM store of the MSM became the multi-part WMM, and the episodic buffer was added in 2000.
    PerspectiveBoth models take a cognitive perspective; biological research on patients such as HM and KF adds a different view of how memory is organised.
    ResponsibilityResearchers must protect the privacy of brain-damaged patients, which is why they are known only by initials. Teachers should also avoid labelling a student as having "poor working memory" on the basis of one test.

    Rung 4: One more question

    The finding: In Baddeley, Thomson and Buchanan (1975), when participants counted from one to eight aloud while they read lists of words, the advantage for short words over long words disappeared.

    A first reaction: "So this proves that the phonological loop exists, and the working memory model is correct." Before you accept that, ask one more question.

    • Did anyone see a phonological loop, or was it inferred from something else? What was actually recorded?
    • Counting aloud lowered recall of all the words. What else, apart from blocking an inner voice, might counting aloud do to a person's attention?
    • Who took part, and how many people were in each experiment? Would one set of small studies be enough to change a model on its own?
