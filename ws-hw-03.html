    True prevalence and recorded prevalence

    Prevalence is the proportion of a population that has a disorder. Lifetime prevalence is the proportion who have had the disorder at any point in their lives. 12-month prevalence is the proportion who had it in the past year. The DSM-5 criteria for MDD require at least five of nine symptoms for at least two weeks, and one of them must be depressed mood or a loss of interest or pleasure.

    The warm-up figures come from Bromet et al. (2011), who analysed the WHO World Mental Health Surveys: 89,037 adults in 18 countries, all interviewed face to face with the same structured diagnostic interview. Lifetime prevalence averaged 14.6% in the ten high-income countries and 11.1% in the eight low and middle-income countries. Yet the 12-month figures were almost the same (5.5% and 5.9%). The researchers suggested that people in some countries may find it harder to recall or report earlier episodes, which lowers lifetime figures most.

    This gives you the key idea of the chapter. A difference in prevalence between two cultures can mean two things:

    • A difference in true prevalence: more people in one culture really do have depression, for example because of differences in stress, inequality or social support.
    • A difference in recorded prevalence: the same amount of depression exists, but more of it is noticed, reported or counted in one culture than in the other.

    Most real differences are probably a mix of both. In an exam, say which factor pushes in which direction, and give the evidence.

    Why might prevalence rates differ between cultures?

    1. Diagnostic criteria. The DSM is published by the American Psychiatric Association, and its criteria were developed mostly with patients in Western clinical settings. The MDD criteria put depressed mood and loss of interest at the centre. If people experience depression mainly through the body, a checklist built around mood may miss them, leading to underdiagnosis.

    2. How symptoms are expressed. Somatisation is the expression of psychological distress through physical symptoms such as headaches, tiredness, dizziness, poor sleep or pain. People in every culture do this to some extent. Simon et al. (1999) studied 25,916 primary care patients in 15 centres across 14 countries. Of the patients with major depression, 69% reported only physical symptoms as the reason for their visit. The figure ranged from 45% to 95% across centres. So somatisation is common everywhere, but how common it is varies a lot.

    3. Stigma and reporting. Stigma is a set of negative beliefs about a group, here people with mental illness. Where stigma is strong, people may not tell an interviewer or a doctor about low mood, even when asked directly. This lowers recorded prevalence. Stigma about mental illness exists in every country, including Western ones. What varies is how strong it is.

    4. Help-seeking. Many prevalence figures come from clinical records: who was diagnosed by a doctor. These only count people who seek professional help. If people in a culture tend to turn first to family, friends or faith for support, or if services are hard to reach, many cases never reach a clinic. Community surveys interview people at home to solve this, but they still depend on what people are willing to say.

    5. Cultural dimensions. Cultural dimensions are ways of comparing cultures on shared values. Hofstede's best-known dimension is individualism versus collectivism. Individualistic cultures emphasise personal goals and independence. Collectivistic cultures emphasise the group, family duties and interdependence. These values might change true prevalence (close social networks could buffer stress) and recorded prevalence (people may describe distress through relationships or the body rather than inner feelings). These are averages for a whole country. They do not tell you what any one person or family is like.

    6. Social and economic factors. Bromet et al. (2011) suggested that greater income inequality in many high-income countries could raise true prevalence.

    The emic and etic approaches

    Emic approachEtic approach

    The linguist Kenneth Pike introduced the terms in 1954, and cross-cultural psychologists adopted them.

    • The etic approach studies behaviour from outside a culture, using one framework and one set of tools across many cultures so that the results can be compared. Using the same DSM-based interview in 18 countries, as the World Mental Health Surveys did, is an etic approach.
    • The emic approach studies behaviour from inside a culture, using the ideas, categories and words that make sense to its members. Asking patients what their illness means to them, in their own terms, is an emic approach.

    An etic study gives comparable numbers, but it may only find the version of depression its tools were designed to find. Berry warned about an imposed etic: taking a concept or test from one culture and assuming it works the same way everywhere. An emic study captures meaning, but its results are hard to compare across cultures. Berry suggested combining them: study each culture on its own terms first, then compare only what turns out to be shared (a derived etic).

    The DSM-5 has moved some way towards this. It includes a Cultural Formulation Interview: 16 questions that ask patients how they understand their problem, what they think caused it, and what help they would want. It also includes a glossary of cultural concepts of distress, such as shenjing shuairuo ("weakness of the nervous system") in China and kufungisisa ("thinking too much") in Zimbabwe.

    Key study 1: Kleinman (1982), neurasthenia and depression in China

    AimTo investigate the relationship between neurasthenia (shenjing shuairuo), a common diagnosis in China at the time, and depression, and to understand somatisation as a cultural way of experiencing and expressing distress.
    Participants100 patients who had been diagnosed with neurasthenia at the psychiatry outpatient clinic of Hunan Medical College, in Changsha, China.
    ProcedureKleinman, a psychiatrist and anthropologist, carried out a combined clinical and anthropological field study in Hunan in 1980. He assessed each patient against the DSM-III criteria (the version of the DSM used at the time). He also interviewed patients about their illness and what it meant in their lives, work and families. Patients who met the criteria for MDD were treated with antidepressant medication by the clinic's doctors, and Kleinman recorded what changed. (Decisions about medication are always made with a doctor.)
    Findings87 of the 100 patients met the DSM-III criteria for MDD. Their main complaints were physical: headaches (90%), insomnia (78%), dizziness (73%) and pain (48%). Only 9% reported depressed mood as a complaint. For about three-quarters of the patients, the social meaning of the illness was linked mainly to work. After antidepressant treatment, 70% showed substantial improvement in symptoms and 87% some improvement. Fewer patients showed a drop in help-seeking, and only a small number felt their social problems had improved.
    ConclusionNeurasthenia was best understood as a culturally shaped illness experience: a form of somatisation linked to depression and to culturally accepted ways of expressing distress. The symptoms improved with treatment, but the social problems behind them often did not.
    What happened nextRyder et al. (2008) returned to the same hospital in Changsha a quarter of a century later and compared 175 Chinese outpatients with 107 Euro-Canadian outpatients in Toronto, all with depression. In interviews, the Chinese patients reported more physical symptoms, but not on a self-report questionnaire. The Euro-Canadian patients reported more psychological symptoms (such as sadness) on all three measures. Part of the difference was explained by externally oriented thinking: focusing less on one's own inner emotional states. The researchers argued that the West may "psychologise" depression as much as China "somatises" it.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example of the emic approach, or of an imposed etic); Paper 1 Section B (somatisation and underdiagnosis); Paper 1 Section C on perspective, measurement or bias.
    Evaluate it withPerspective: the same patients were "neurasthenic" from a Chinese clinical perspective and "depressed" from a DSM-III perspective. Kleinman showed that both labels describe something real. Measurement: the DSM-III is itself an etic tool, so the 87% figure depends on accepting that tool. Ryder et al. show that the method of measurement (interview or questionnaire) changes the result. Bias: one clinic, one researcher and patients who had already sought help. China has changed a great deal since 1980, so the findings may not describe Chinese patients today.

    Key study 2: Chiao and Blizinsky (2010), collectivism, genes and mood disorders

    AimTo investigate whether cultural values of individualism and collectivism are linked to how common a particular gene variant is in a population, and whether both are linked to the national prevalence of anxiety and mood disorders.
    ParticipantsNo new participants. This was a correlational study of nations, using published data. Gene data came from 124 published studies covering 50,135 people in 29 countries. Prevalence data for anxiety and mood disorders were available for 12 of those countries.
    ProcedureThe researchers used Hofstede's individualism scores for each country. They calculated how common the short (S) version of the serotonin transporter gene (5-HTTLPR) was in each country. Earlier research had linked the S version to a higher risk of depression after stress. Prevalence figures came from the WHO World Mental Health Surveys, which use DSM-IV criteria. They then correlated the three variables across countries.
    FindingsMore collectivistic countries had a higher proportion of people carrying the S version (r = 0.70 across 29 countries). Yet more collectivistic countries had lower prevalence of mood disorders (r = −0.80 across 12 countries) and anxiety (r = −0.73). When collectivism and gene frequency were analysed together, collectivism still predicted lower prevalence, and the link between the gene and prevalence became much weaker.
    ConclusionThe researchers proposed that collectivistic values may have developed alongside the gene, and may protect people who are genetically at risk, for example through stronger social support. Collectivism appeared to explain (mediate) the link between the gene and lower prevalence.
    What happened nextThe authors noted that the prevalence figures could be affected by response bias, for example if stigma were stronger in more collectivistic countries, but argued this was unlikely because the pattern did not appear for other disorders. Later, Border et al. (2019) tested the 18 most studied "depression genes", including the serotonin transporter gene, in samples of up to 443,264 people. They found no clear evidence that any of them was linked to depression. This weakens the gene part of Chiao and Blizinsky's argument, though not the cultural correlation itself.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example of cultural dimensions); Paper 1 Section C on causality, measurement or bias; Paper 2 Section B practice (a correlational study that compares countries).
    Evaluate it withCausality: it is a correlation between countries, so it cannot show that collectivism causes lower prevalence, and it tells us nothing about any individual. Measurement: the prevalence figures came from an etic, DSM-based survey, so lower figures in collectivistic countries may partly reflect somatisation or under-reporting. Bias: only 12 countries had prevalence data, which is a small number for a correlation.

    Cultural approaches to mental health

    The second learning objective asks for examples of cultural differences in approaches to mental health: how people understand distress, where they look for help, and how treatment is delivered.

    1. Different first sources of help. In many cultures, the first source of support is the family, friends or faith rather than a doctor. Al-Darmaki, Thomas and Yaaqeib (2016) surveyed 70 female Emirati college students about mental health. The participants saw social and environmental factors as the most important causes of mental health problems. Social stigma was the barrier to help-seeking they named most often. Religious practices were often reported as a way of coping and of staying well. Most participants also said they would be willing to see a health professional. Family, faith and professional help were not either-or choices. This was one small sample of young women, so it cannot be generalised to everyone in the UAE.

    2. Adapting talking therapy to the culture. Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) was developed in Western settings. Therapists can adapt it by using the client's language and local examples, involving the family, or including the client's beliefs when the client wants this. Vally and Maggott (2015) combined 11 controlled studies (1,602 participants) of culturally adapted CBT for adult depression in low and middle-income countries. They found a large effect, and therapy delivered by trained lay workers worked about as well as therapy delivered by specialists.

    3. Changing who delivers help. The Friendship Bench in Zimbabwe trains older lay health workers, known as "grandmothers", to deliver problem-solving therapy at primary care clinics. Chibanda et al. (2016) ran a randomised controlled trial with 573 adults at 24 clinics in Harare. Symptoms were measured with the Shona Symptom Questionnaire, a tool developed in the local language. After six months, people in the Friendship Bench group had far fewer symptoms than those who received standard care plus information about common mental disorders. 14% of the Friendship Bench group still had symptoms of depression, compared with 50% of the standard care group. This is an emic idea (local language, trusted community members) tested with an etic method (a randomised trial).

    Mental health in the UAE

    The UAE is home to people from many nationalities, languages and faiths. There is no single "UAE culture" of mental health, and you should never assume what a person believes from their nationality or religion. What we can say, from official sources, is that mental health is a national policy priority. The UAE has a National Policy for the Promotion of Mental Health with five strategic objectives. In 2023 the UAE issued a federal law on mental health. It aims to protect the rights and dignity of people with mental disorders, requires informed consent and privacy, and guarantees access to psychotherapy and psychiatric medication. Laws like this are also a cultural approach to mental health.

    Real-life examples

    • A visit to the GP. A man in Abu Dhabi visits his doctor three times in two months for headaches and poor sleep. Tests find nothing physical. A doctor who asks only "Do you feel sad?" may get the answer "no". A doctor who asks about sleep, energy, work and family, in the patient's own words, is more likely to recognise depression. This is somatisation in everyday practice.
    • Choosing the words. A school counsellor in Dubai notices that students rarely use the word "depressed". They say they are "stressed", "tired" or "not myself". Using the students' own words in conversations and wellbeing surveys is a small emic move.
    • Services in many languages. In a country with residents from around the world, a clinic that offers therapy in Arabic, English, Hindi or Tagalog removes one barrier to help-seeking. A person who can describe distress in their first language is easier to understand and help.

    How useful is cross-cultural research on depression?

    Strengths. Large etic surveys use the same interview in many countries, so differences cannot be blamed on different questions. Emic research, like Kleinman's, shows how culture shapes the way depression is felt and described, and it has changed practice: the DSM-5 now includes the Cultural Formulation Interview. Research on adapted treatments, such as the Friendship Bench, shows how to reach people who would never visit a psychiatrist.

    Limitations. It is very hard to separate true prevalence from recorded prevalence, because the same cultural factors (stigma, somatisation, help-seeking) affect both the illness and the measurement. Country-level studies treat a whole nation as if everyone shared the same values, which is not true, especially in diverse countries like the UAE. Key studies date quickly: Kleinman's patients lived in China in 1980. Talk of "collectivistic" or "Western" cultures can also slide into stereotypes.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to cultural differences in depression
    MeasurementPrevalence depends on the tool: a DSM-based interview, a self-report questionnaire or clinical records can give different answers for the same people (Ryder et al., 2008).
    CausalityChiao and Blizinsky (2010) found a correlation between collectivism and lower prevalence across countries. It cannot show that collectivism causes lower prevalence.
    BiasUnder-reporting caused by stigma is a response bias. Applying a Western checklist everywhere is an imposed etic, which can cause cultural bias in diagnosis.
    ChangeDiagnoses change over time: neurasthenia was common in 1980s China, and depression is diagnosed more often there now. Treatments can also be changed to fit a culture.
    PerspectiveThe same patient can be "neurasthenic" from one perspective and "depressed" from another. Emic and etic approaches are two perspectives that see different things.
    ResponsibilityClinicians are responsible for not misdiagnosing people whose distress looks different from the checklist. Researchers are responsible for not turning cultural averages into stereotypes.
