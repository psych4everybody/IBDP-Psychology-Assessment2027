    Part A: Experiments, a recap

    What makes a study an experiment?

    An experiment is the only research method that can test cause and effect directly. The researcher changes one variable, keeps everything else the same, and measures what happens to a second variable.

    • The independent variable (IV) is the variable the researcher changes or compares.
    • The dependent variable (DV) is the variable the researcher measures. It is expected to depend on the IV.
    • Extraneous variables are any other variables that might affect the DV. When an extraneous variable changes along with the IV, it becomes a confounding variable, and the researcher can no longer tell which one caused the change in the DV.

    Back to the warm-up. The students who chose StudyStar may have been more motivated, had more time, or had more help at home. Any of these could explain their higher grades. A fair test would give the app to one group and not to another, with students placed in the groups at random, and then compare their scores. That is an experiment.

    Operationalising variables

    A variable is operationalised when you say exactly how it will be changed or measured. "Memory" is not operationalised. "The number of words correctly recalled from a list of 20, written down within two minutes" is. Operationalising matters because it lets another researcher repeat your study in exactly the same way, and it makes clear what your results can and cannot tell you.

    Hypotheses

    A research hypothesis predicts the effect of the IV on the DV. It must include both operationalised variables. A directional (one-tailed) hypothesis predicts which way the difference will go, for example "Group A will recall more words than Group B". A non-directional (two-tailed) hypothesis predicts only that there will be a difference. The null hypothesis states that the IV will have no effect on the DV, and that any difference found is due to chance.

    True experiments and quasi-experiments

    In a true experiment, the researcher manipulates the IV and places participants in conditions by random allocation. In a quasi-experiment, the researcher cannot do one of these. Usually the IV is a characteristic participants already have, such as age, gender or whether they are bilingual, so participants cannot be randomly allocated to it. A study comparing the recall of Grade 9 and Grade 12 students is a quasi-experiment, because nobody can be randomly allocated to an age.

    This matters for causality. In a quasi-experiment, the groups may differ in many ways at once. Grade 12 students are older, but they have also had more years of school and more exam practice. A difference in recall might be caused by any of these, so a quasi-experiment gives weaker evidence of cause and effect than a true experiment.

    FeatureTrue experimentQuasi-experiment
    The IVManipulated by the researcherUsually a characteristic participants already have
    Allocation to conditionsRandomNot random: participants are already in a group
    Evidence of cause and effectStrongerWeaker: the groups may differ in other ways
    ExampleStudents randomly allocated to revise with music or in silenceRecall compared between bilingual and monolingual students

    Experimental designs

    The design is how participants are used across the conditions of the IV.

    DesignWhat it meansStrengthLimitation
    Independent measuresDifferent participants in each conditionNo order effects; participants are less likely to guess the aimParticipant variables may differ between groups, so random allocation is needed
    Repeated measuresThe same participants do every conditionParticipant variables are the same in each conditionOrder effects (practice, boredom, fatigue); participants may guess the aim
    Matched pairsParticipants are matched on a key variable, then one of each pair goes into each conditionReduces participant variables without order effectsMatching takes time and is never perfect

    Order effects can be reduced in repeated measures by counterbalancing: half of the participants do condition A first, and half do condition B first. But some studies cannot use repeated measures at all, because once a participant has done one condition, they cannot "unsee" it. You will see this in Part B.

    Random allocation and controls

    Random allocation means every participant has an equal chance of being placed in each condition, for example by drawing folded slips from a box or using a random number generator. It does not make the groups identical. It spreads participant variables (such as general knowledge, maths ability or mood) across the groups by chance, so no condition is systematically different. Random allocation is not the same as random sampling. Random sampling is about who takes part in the study; random allocation is about which condition they are placed in.

    Controls keep extraneous variables the same across conditions:

    • Standardised instructions: every participant hears or reads exactly the same words.
    • Standardised procedure and materials: the same task, the same time limit, the same room, the same answer sheet layout.
    • Reducing demand characteristics: participants who guess the aim may change their behaviour. Researchers keep the aim general until the debrief and use an independent measures design where they can.

    Validity and reliability

    Internal validity is the extent to which the study shows that the IV, and nothing else, caused the change in the DV. Random allocation, standardisation and a clearly operationalised DV all raise internal validity. Confounding variables lower it.

    Ecological validity is the extent to which the findings apply to real-life settings. Experiments in a classroom or laboratory often use short, artificial tasks with no real consequences, so ecological validity is often low. There is usually a trade-off: the more control a researcher has, the less the situation looks like everyday life.

    Two related ideas: population validity is whether the findings apply to people beyond the sample, and reliability is whether the same result appears when the study is repeated. A finding that replicates in many samples is more trustworthy than one striking result.

    Sampling

    The target population is the group the researcher wants to learn about. The sample is the group that takes part. An opportunity (convenience) sample uses people who are available, such as a class. It is quick but may not represent the population. A random sample gives everyone in the population an equal chance of being chosen. A self-selected (volunteer) sample uses people who respond to an invitation; they may be more motivated than others.

    Real-life examples: experiments outside the classroom

    • Testing medicines. New medicines are tested in randomised controlled trials. Patients are randomly allocated to receive the medicine or a placebo, so that any difference in recovery can be linked to the medicine.
    • Testing apps and websites. Food delivery and shopping apps often show two versions of a screen to different users, chosen at random, and compare how many people complete an order. This is an independent measures experiment, sometimes called an A/B test.
    • Testing a school change. Imagine a school in Abu Dhabi tries a new homework timetable with one Grade 10 class and compares it with another class. Students cannot be randomly moved between classes, so this is closer to a quasi-experiment: the classes may differ in their teachers, timetables or abilities.

    Part B: Our class practical, a word recall experiment

    Read this part after you have taken part in the practical.

    Where the practical comes from

    The multi-store model (Chapter 6) says that information in short-term memory (STM) fades within seconds unless it is rehearsed, that is, repeated to yourself. Glanzer and Cunitz (1966), Key study 1 in Chapter 6, showed that a delay of 30 seconds before recall wiped out the recency effect, but only because participants counted aloud during the delay, which stopped them rehearsing. Our class practical asks a simpler question: does a 30-second wait before recall, on its own, reduce how many words people remember? The key study below shows why what happens during the wait matters so much.

    Key study: Peterson and Peterson (1959), how long STM lasts

    AimTo find out how long information stays in short-term memory when rehearsal is prevented.
    Participants24 psychology students at an American university.
    ProcedureEach participant heard a trigram, three consonants with no meaning (for example TGH). They then heard a three-digit number and immediately counted backwards from it in threes or fours, out loud, until a light signalled recall. Counting stopped them rehearsing the trigram. The delay was 3, 6, 9, 12, 15 or 18 seconds. Each participant did many trials at every delay, with a different trigram each time, so this was a repeated measures design.
    FindingsAfter 3 seconds, about 80% of trigrams were recalled correctly. After 6 seconds this fell to about 50%, and after 18 seconds fewer than 10% were recalled.
    ConclusionWithout rehearsal, information is lost from STM very quickly, within about 18 seconds.
    What happened nextKeppel and Underwood (1962) found that on the very first trials, people forgot almost nothing even after 18 seconds. Forgetting grew as earlier trigrams piled up and got in the way of new ones. So interference from earlier items, not only time passing, explains much of the forgetting.
    Use it forPaper 2 Section A, Question 1 (where our procedure came from); Paper 1 Section A (cognitive models: evidence for the multi-store model); Paper 2 Section B (evaluating an experiment).
    Evaluate it withCausality: the counting task controlled rehearsal, so the delay was the only thing that changed between trials. Measurement: meaningless trigrams are easy to score but are not like everyday memories, so ecological validity is low. Bias: a small sample of psychology students may have guessed the aim.

    Our practical: the write-up

    AimTo investigate whether a 30-second wait before recall affects how many words people remember, compared with recalling immediately.
    Research hypothesis (directional)Participants who recall a list of 20 words immediately will recall more words than participants who wait 30 seconds before recalling.
    Null hypothesisA 30-second wait before recall will have no effect on the number of words recalled; any difference is due to chance.
    IV (operationalised)When recall began: straight after the last word (immediate condition) or after a 30-second wait (wait condition).
    DV (operationalised)The number of words from the list correctly written down, out of 20.
    DesignIndependent measures (independent samples): each participant was in one condition only. Repeated measures would have needed two different word lists, because the same list cannot be learned twice, and the lists might not be equally easy.
    Type of experimentA true experiment if participants were randomly allocated to the two conditions; a quasi-experiment if the groups were formed another way, for example by where people sat. Check your notes:
    SamplingFrom your notes: the sampling technique and the number of participants in each condition .
    MaterialsOne list of 20 words; a sheet for writing down recalled words; a timer; the class results table.
    Procedure1. Participants gave informed consent and were told they could withdraw. 2. Both groups saw the same list of 20 words, in the same order. 3. The immediate group wrote down every word they could remember straight away. 4. The wait group waited 30 seconds and then wrote down every word they could remember. 5. Each sheet was scored out of 20 and the two conditions were compared. 6. Participants were debriefed and scores were kept anonymous.
    Watch outThere was no task during the wait, so participants could rehearse the words, which is exactly what Peterson and Peterson prevented. The wait and the word display were not timed exactly. Both weaken internal validity, and both are good material for Paper 2 answers.

    Ethics in our practical

    Ethical guidelineWhat we did
    Informed consentParticipants were told it was a short memory task, how long it would take and that taking part was their choice.
    Right to withdrawParticipants could stop at any time and could withdraw their score until the results were combined.
    AnonymityNo names were written on the recall sheets. Results were reported only as group averages.
    DeceptionParticipants knew it was a memory task. They were not told in advance that the timing of recall was the variable being tested, because knowing could change how hard they rehearsed. This was explained in the debrief.
    DebriefThe aim, both conditions and the multi-store model idea behind them were explained afterwards.
    Protection from harmThe task was short and neutral. No individual scores were shared, so no one could be embarrassed by how many words they remembered.

    Real-life examples

    Homework at the bell. A teacher reads out the homework as students pack up. Students who write it down at once usually get it right; students who wait until they reach the bus often forget a detail, especially if they chatted with friends on the way.

    A name at a family gathering. You are introduced to a relative's friend and then pulled straight into another conversation. A minute later the name has gone, because the new conversation stopped you rehearsing it.

    Directions in a car park. A security guard tells a driver "level 3, zone C, row 12". Drivers who repeat it to themselves while walking to the lift remember it far better than drivers who check their phone on the way.

    How good was our experiment?

    Strengths. Both groups saw the same 20 words in the same order, so the list cannot explain a difference between them. The DV was a number out of 20, so the two conditions were easy to score and compare. The procedure was short and simple, so another class could repeat it.

    Limitations. Nothing filled the 30-second wait, so participants could rehearse, and the wait group may have forgotten less than the multi-store model predicts. The wait and the word display were not timed exactly, so conditions were not fully standardised. With independent measures, one group may simply have had better memories (participant variables), especially if participants were not randomly allocated. The sample was small and from one school, and we ran no inferential test, so we cannot say whether a difference is statistically significant. Learning a list of words and writing it down is an artificial task, so ecological validity is low.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to our class practical
    MeasurementThe DV was operationalised as words correctly recalled out of 20, which is easy to score. Deciding what counts as correct (for example a misspelled word) needs a rule agreed before scoring.
    CausalityOnly the timing of recall was meant to differ between groups, which is what allows a cause and effect claim. Rehearsal during the wait, loose timing and differences between the two groups all weaken that claim.
    BiasSampling bias: one class at one school. Participant bias: psychology students may guess the aim and try harder in one condition.
    ChangeThe practical measures change over a very short time: how much is lost from memory in 30 seconds.
    PerspectiveThe experiment takes a cognitive perspective, based on the multi-store model. It says nothing about biological factors, such as sleep, or cultural factors, such as recalling words in a second language.
    ResponsibilityEven a low-risk task needs consent, the right to withdraw, anonymity and a debrief, and individual scores should not be shared.
