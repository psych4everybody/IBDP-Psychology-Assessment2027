    What is a cognitive explanation?

    Like any other behaviour, relationships are shaped by biological, cognitive and sociocultural factors. In Chapter 7 you looked at the biology of relationships through chemical messengers. This chapter looks at cognitive factors.

    Cognitive explanations propose that relationships are influenced by how we perceive the other person and how we interpret what happens between us. What matters is not only what happens, but what each person thinks it means and how they weigh it up. None of the models in this chapter looks at hormones or the brain. All of them look at thinking: judgements, comparisons and expectations.

    The three models below were developed mainly to explain romantic relationships, and the key studies are about them. The same reasoning applies to any commitment between people, such as a friendship, a team or a club, so the examples in this chapter use those.

    Social exchange theory

    Social exchange theory (SET) was proposed by Thibaut and Kelley (1959). It suggests that people make a rational calculation of costs and benefits to decide whether a relationship is worth continuing. It treats a relationship a little like a business: a series of exchanges in which each person hopes to gain as much as possible at the lowest cost.

    • Rewards are what the relationship gives you, such as companionship, support, fun or status.
    • Costs are what the relationship takes from you, such as time, effort, stress or giving up other options.
    • Profit is the rewards minus the costs. A relationship is worth continuing when the profit is high enough.

    Thibaut and Kelley also argued that profit is always judged against something. The comparison level is what a person expects to get from a relationship, based on past experience. The comparison level for alternatives is what they think they could get elsewhere. A relationship can feel good enough on its own and still lose out if a better option appears. This second comparison leads directly to the investment model below.

    As a lens, SET asks: what is this person getting out of it, what is it costing them, and how does that compare with their other options?

    Equity theory

    Equity theory, applied to close relationships by Hatfield and Traupmann (1980), suggests that a relationship is stable when both people see it as fair: what each one gets out of it is roughly in proportion to what each one puts in. High profit is not enough. It also has to feel fair.

    • If you feel the other person benefits more than you do, you are likely to feel dissatisfied, even resentful.
    • If you feel you benefit more than the other person does, the relationship is not stable either, because you may feel guilty or uncomfortable.

    Think of a group project in which one student does most of the work and everyone gets the same mark. The hard worker may feel used, and a student who did very little may feel awkward about it. These feelings come from what each person believes about the fairness of the exchange, so two people can see the same project very differently.

    As a lens, equity theory asks: does each person feel that what they put in and what they get out match what the other person puts in and gets out?

    The investment model of commitment

    The investment model was proposed by Caryl Rusbult (1980) to explain why people stay in relationships or leave them. It is the main example of a cognitive model in this area. It says that commitment, the intention to continue a relationship, depends on three components.

    • Satisfaction: generally positive feelings about the other person and the relationship. Satisfaction is high when rewards are high and costs are low.
    • Perceived quality of alternatives: what else is on offer. An alternative could be another relationship, but also being alone, or spending the time with family or other friends. Alternatives are judged with the same cost and benefit reasoning.
    • Investment size: everything a person has put into the relationship that would be lost or reduced if it ended, such as time, effort, money, shared friends and shared memories.

    Put simply, satisfaction and investment push commitment up, and good alternatives pull it down. The model predicts that if the three together add up to high commitment, the relationship will continue even if one component is weak. If they do not, it may break apart even if one component is very positive.

    SituationWhat the model predicts
    Large investment and no good alternatives, but satisfaction is not highThe relationship continues. Investment and the lack of alternatives keep commitment high.
    High satisfaction, but little invested and attractive alternatives availableThe relationship may end. The three components together do not add up to high commitment.

    This is why people who are unhappy often stay: they have already invested, and they do not want to lose what they have put in. In later work, Rusbult and colleagues also described maintenance mechanisms that committed people use to keep a relationship going, such as a willingness to make sacrifices, forgiveness, and holding positive illusions about the other person.

    As a lens, the investment model asks: how satisfied is this person, how good are their alternatives, and how much would they lose by leaving?

    Three lenses on one situation

    Each cognitive model asks a different question. Here they are applied to Noor from the warm-up.

    LensThe question it asksWhat it notices about Noor
    Social exchangeDo the rewards outweigh the costs, compared with the other options?Rewards: friends, the robot, what she has learned. Costs: tension, doing most of the work. The rival offer changes the comparison.
    EquityIs what she puts in roughly equal to what she gets out, compared with the others?She does most of the work, so she may feel she gives more than she gets. That predicts dissatisfaction.
    InvestmentHow satisfied is she, how good are her alternatives, and how much has she invested?Satisfaction is falling and the alternative is good, but three years of investment pull the other way. The combination decides.

    Notice what the three lenses share: none of them looks at biology, and all of them look at how Noor perceives and weighs up her situation. Notice also what they do not share: they ask different questions, and each one sees something the others miss. This is what it means to say that different models offer different perspectives, and it is why "which model is best?" is a harder question than it looks.

    Key study 1: Rusbult (1980), a role-play experiment

    AimTo test whether the factors in the investment model (the cost of the relationship, the quality of the alternative and the size of the investment) affect commitment, by changing each factor on purpose in a hypothetical situation.
    Participants171 introductory psychology students (82 men and 89 women) at a university in the USA, who took part for course credit.
    ProcedureA laboratory experiment using role play. Each participant read a four-page story and imagined themselves as the main character, who was the same sex as the participant. The character's partner had moved away to study, another person had shown interest in the character, and the character had to decide whether to stay in the current relationship. Three independent variables were changed between versions of the story. Cost: high (the partner had moved 1,000 miles away, so they could meet about once a month) or low (60 miles away, meeting once or twice a week). Quality of the alternative: the other person was described as either moderately attractive or moderately unattractive in intelligence, personality, appearance and wit. Investment size: low (together for one month), medium (together for a year) or high (together for a year, and the character worked for the partner's father, so leaving might mean giving up the job). Commitment was measured with nine-point questionnaire items: how likely the character was to leave for the other person, how attached they felt, and how committed they were.
    FindingsInvestment size and the quality of the alternative both had significant effects. Commitment rose as investment rose, and it was lower when the alternative was attractive. The effect of cost was smaller and not significant. The manipulations had the same effects for male and female participants. Mean commitment ranged only from 5.05 to 6.60 on the 9-point scale (see the table below).
    ConclusionInvestment and alternatives affect commitment, as the model predicts. Cost on its own seemed to matter less than the model expected.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example for cognitive models); Paper 1 Section C on causality, measurement or perspective; Paper 2 Section B practice (an experiment with three independent variables).
    Evaluate it withCausality: the researcher changed the factors on purpose and held the rest of the story constant, so cause and effect conclusions are possible within the study. Measurement: participants rated an imagined character, not their own relationship, so the study measures what people think they would do, not what they do. Bias: the sample was psychology students at one American university. Effect size: the differences were significant, but small in absolute terms.

    Key study 2: Rusbult (1983), a longitudinal study

    To get beyond a hypothetical story, Rusbult followed real relationships over time. Longitudinal studies suit this area, because theories of relationships try to predict how a relationship will develop.

    AimTo test whether the investment model can predict how commitment develops over time in real relationships, and who stays and who leaves.
    Participants34 undergraduates (17 men and 17 women) at a college in the USA. They were volunteers who replied to a flyer. Each was in a relationship that had begun recently (on average about four weeks before the study), and only one partner from each relationship took part.
    ProcedureA longitudinal study using repeated questionnaires. Participants completed an initial questionnaire and then 12 identical questionnaires, one about every 17 days, over roughly seven months. The questionnaires measured rewards, costs, alternatives, investment, satisfaction and commitment, mostly on 9-point scales. Participants also noted anything unusual that had happened and, if the relationship ended, how it ended. Participation stopped when the relationship ended or when the study finished.
    FindingsOverall, higher commitment went with greater satisfaction, larger investment and poorer alternatives, as the model predicts. Rusbult compared three groups. Stayers remained in the relationship: over time their satisfaction, investment and commitment rose, and their alternatives seemed less attractive. Leavers ended the relationship: their satisfaction, investment and commitment fell, and their alternatives improved. Abandoned individuals had the relationship ended by the other person: compared with stayers, their rewards and satisfaction grew less and their costs grew more, yet their alternatives declined, they kept investing heavily and they stayed until the other person left. Rusbult called this entrapment. Costs did not clearly affect commitment overall, but for men and for stayers, higher costs went with higher commitment.
    ConclusionRusbult described the results as "fairly good support" for the model. Changes in commitment over time seem to help explain who stays and who leaves.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section B (applying satisfaction, alternatives and investment); Paper 1 Section C on bias, measurement, change or causality; Paper 2 Section B practice (a longitudinal questionnaire study).
    Evaluate it withChange: repeated measures show how commitment develops, which a single questionnaire cannot. Causality: the data are correlational, so it is not clear whether investment raises commitment or commitment leads people to invest. Measurement: everything is self-report, and the same person rates every variable. Bias: a small sample of volunteers from one American college, in relationships at an early stage.

    Limitations of the research

    Rusbult's studies show three problems that are common in this whole area of research.

    1. Self-report. It is hard to find anything more objective than asking people about their satisfaction and commitment. It is also hard for participants to tell abstract constructs apart. Will every participant understand the difference between "costs" and "investments" in the same way?
    2. Correlational data. Interpretations can be speculative. In Rusbult (1983), for men and for stayers, the greater the costs, the stronger the commitment. That does not fit the model. Rusbult suggested that costs can work like an investment: people who have already paid a lot may become more determined to make it "pay off". This is an interpretation that the data do not directly test. The model might also simply be wrong on this point.
    3. Repeated measurement. Relationships change, so the same measures have to be taken many times. But answers to one questionnaire may be influenced by the last one. Writing that you are "very satisfied" may even create an expectation that you then live up to.

    Quick lens: Bias

    Read the three limitations again as questions about bias, and keep them open. You will use them in Check yourself and in the Section C plan.

    • Who agrees to answer questionnaires about their own relationship, and what might make their answers less than honest?
    • What could completing the same questionnaire twelve times do to a person's answers, or to their behaviour?
    • Whose relationships were studied, and whose were not? Most of this research uses Western samples, but the way people weigh up a relationship may be shaped by culture, for example by how much say families have.

    How useful are cognitive explanations?

    Strengths. The models explain something biology finds hard to explain: why people stay in relationships that no longer make them happy, and why commitment changes slowly over months. The investment model makes clear, testable predictions and has been supported by an experiment and by a longitudinal study. The same reasoning applies to friendships, teams and clubs, so the models are useful lenses well beyond romantic relationships.

    Limitations. The evidence depends heavily on self-report, and the experiments use hypothetical stories. The models assume that people calculate rationally, while much of what happens in relationships is emotional and fast. They say little about biological factors, and most of the samples are young, Western and studied at a single point in their relationships.

    Real life: why do people stay?

    Commitment is not only about people. Here are three everyday situations in which people keep going with something that has stopped being fun.

    1. Someone pays for a year-long gym membership and keeps paying for months without going, because they signed up for the whole year.
    2. A player keeps a 300-day streak alive in a game they no longer enjoy.
    3. A student stays in a friendship group that has become draining, because they have been in it since primary school.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to cognitive explanations
    PerspectiveCognitive and biological factors both matter, and researchers rarely try to rank them. A more useful question is how to decide which of several cognitive models describes reality better.
    BiasSelf-report dominates, samples are mostly young Western volunteers, and repeated questionnaires may shape the answers they are meant to measure.
    CausalityThe models are causal, but most of the evidence is correlational. Rusbult (1980) is an experiment, but a hypothetical one, and longitudinal studies only partly solve the problem.
    MeasurementSatisfaction, alternatives and investment cannot be observed; they are measured by asking people, who may not separate "costs" from "investments" in the same way.
    ChangeRelationships change over time, and cognitive variables are good at explaining long-term change through the interplay of satisfaction, alternatives and investment.
    ResponsibilityThe research is not invasive, but it asks about sensitive parts of people's lives. Confidentiality and the right to withdraw matter, and participants who doubt their answers are confidential may not be honest.
