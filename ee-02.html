On this page

    Aim

    A good question does half the work

    Your research question shapes everything that follows: what you search for, which studies you keep, and whether you can build an argument at all. The IB guidance asks for a question clearly focused on behaviour and limited in scope.

    By the end of this stage you can:

    • narrow a topic you care about into one focused question
    • test a question against five checks and avoid the common traps
    • check that enough research exists before you commit
    • define the key terms in your question
    Our example questionTo what extent does taking notes by hand rather than on a laptop improve students' learning from lectures?
    The checks

    Five checks for any question

    1. One focus. One factor and one outcome, not a list.
    2. A real debate. Published research disagrees, or the answer depends on something. If everyone agrees, there is nothing to argue.
    3. Behaviour or thinking. The question is about what people (or animals) do, feel or think.
    4. Limited scope. It can be answered well in 4,000 words.
    5. Answerable from published studies. Enough peer-reviewed research exists, roughly eight or more relevant studies.
    Phrasing

    "To what extent..." and "How far..." invite a weighed judgement. "What is..." and "Describe..." invite a description, which cannot earn many marks for argument and evaluation.

    Avoid these

    Common traps

    TrapExampleFix
    Too broad"Social media and mental health"Name one behaviour and one outcome
    Nature versus nurture"Is aggression genetic or learned?"Choose one specific factor and one measured outcome
    A population with no research"...in teenagers in Dubai"Only limit the population if studies of that group exist
    Comparing two treatments"Is therapy A better than therapy B?"Only if trials compare them directly
    A theory applied to something new"How does schema theory explain a viral trend?"Only if research tests the theory in that situation
    Pure biology"How does serotonin work?"Focus on the behaviour the biology affects
    Narrow it down

    From topic to question

    Start with what genuinely interests you, then narrow one step at a time. Each step removes one source of vagueness.

    1

    Topic

    Learning and technology

    2

    Narrower

    Laptops in lectures

    3

    One factor, one outcome

    Note-taking medium (hand or laptop) and test performance

    4

    A question you can argue

    To what extent does taking notes by hand rather than on a laptop improve students' learning from lectures?

    Then define the terms. In your introduction, say what "learning" means in the studies you use (scores on factual and conceptual questions), and who the "students" were.

    Before you commit

    Run a study census

    Before committing to a question, spend 30 minutes searching (see Stage 3). If you cannot find about eight relevant peer-reviewed studies, including some that disagree, go back one step and widen the question. Very recent topics often lack peer-reviewed research.

    Your question, not AI's

    Asking an AI tool to write your research question is not acceptable (IB scenario 4). Develop it from your own interests and your conversations with your supervisor. See Stage 8.

    • A question that fails the census is the most common reason an EE stalls later. Insist on it.

    Check yourself

    Five quick questions

    Why is "What are the effects of video games?" a weak question?

    It is too broad (which games, which effects, on whom?) and invites a description rather than an argument.

    When can you limit your question to a particular population?

    Only when published research on that population exists. Otherwise you will not have the studies to answer it.

    What is a study census, and why run one?

    A short search to check that enough relevant research exists, including studies that disagree, before you commit to a question.

    Why does a good question need a real debate?

    Criteria C and D reward weighing evidence. If all the research agrees, there is little to argue or evaluate.

    Can you ask an AI tool to suggest your research question?

    No. The IB treats that as not acceptable. Your question must come from your interests and your work with your supervisor.
