    Why study communication?

    Human beings are social and depend on each other, and communication is how we run a relationship from day to day. It is how we make contact, resolve conflict and move from being strangers to being close. Positive relationships are a major source of well-being and help protect people against health problems, so how people communicate matters well beyond any single conversation.

    Research on relationships was not always taken seriously. In 1975 a US senator, William Proxmire, publicly mocked a government grant for research on love by Ellen Berscheid and Elaine Hatfield, arguing that people would rather such things stayed a mystery. Today bookshops and social media are full of relationship advice, and that is exactly why scientific research matters: it checks claims that nobody has tested.

    Six principles of interpersonal communication

    Guerrero, Andersen and Afifi (2021) described six principles that apply to communication in any relationship.

    PrincipleWhat it meansExample
    1. Verbal and non-verbalCommunication uses words and also non-verbal messages such as tone of voice, facial expression and posture."I'm fine," said with crossed arms and no eye contact.
    2. Content and relationalEvery message carries content, and also says something about the relationship between the people."Are you coming to practice?" asks for information, and may also show care, pressure or doubt.
    3. GoalsPeople communicate to reach self-presentation goals (the image they want to give), relational goals (building closeness) and instrumental goals (getting help or influencing someone).Choosing words to sound confident in a presentation; messaging a friend to check in; asking a teacher for an extension.
    4. InevitableIt is impossible not to communicate. Silence is itself a message that the other person will interpret.A message that has been read but not answered.
    5. Varies in effectivenessMeaning can be lost or distorted when a message is put into words, sent and decoded by the receiver.A joke typed without a tone of voice, read as sarcasm.
    6. Symmetrical or asymmetricalIn a symmetrical exchange the replies match in tone and intensity. In an asymmetrical one they do not.Symmetrical: "See you at practice later?" "Yes, looking forward to it." Asymmetrical: "I'm so proud of you." "Thanks."

    Conflict and six styles of resolving it

    Conflict is a normal part of relationships, and it is not necessarily bad. Depending on how it is handled, people can come out of it with a stronger relationship than before. Learning to resolve conflict constructively may be more valuable than simply avoiding it.

    Guerrero, Andersen and Afifi (2021) described six styles of conflict resolution, placed along two dimensions: uncooperative to cooperative (is each person trying to win, or trying to find a solution that works for both?) and direct to indirect (do they talk about the issue openly, or avoid it?).

    UncooperativeIn the middleCooperative
    DirectCompetitive fighting: trying to win the argument.Compromising: each person gives up a little to meet in the middle.Collaborating: working together on both people's goals.
    IndirectIndirect fighting: hostile, but not openly, such as sarcasm or sulking.Avoiding: staying away from the issue.Yielding: giving in to the other person.

    The names of the styles and the two dimensions are Guerrero, Andersen and Afifi's; the descriptions are in our own plain words.

    Competitive and indirect fighting are common, even though they tend to harm relationships.

    Negative reciprocity

    Why are the harmful styles so common? Aggression tends to escalate. Once one person has acted aggressively or openly opposed the other, the other may feel they have to answer in kind. This idea, that aggression breeds aggression, is called negative reciprocity. It reinforces itself, so noticing it early matters. Six habits are known to trigger it.

    HabitWhat it isExample
    GunnysackingNot raising issues when they happen, storing them up, then dumping them all at once.A teammate says nothing for weeks about late arrivals, then lists every one after a match.
    Kitchen sinkingBringing the whole collection of old arguments into a new one."And you were late in September, and you forgot the posters in October..."
    Third partiesBringing other people into the argument, for example by comparing the person with someone else."Your brother would never do this."
    Button pushingSaying or doing something you know will be a trigger.Mentioning someone's failed audition in the middle of a disagreement.
    Empty threatsThreatening to end the relationship without meaning it."I'm leaving this group," with no plan to leave.
    Mind readingTelling the other person what they think or feel."You never cared about this event."

    Link to the content list: confirmation bias

    Confirmation bias is the tendency to seek out, interpret and remember information that fits what we already believe, and to ignore or play down information that does not. It is a Section A content term, so it can be asked about in any context. Mind reading shows it at work. Once a student believes a teammate does not care, a late reply counts as proof and an early reply goes unnoticed. The belief decides which evidence gets counted.

    The four horsemen

    John Gottman, working with Robert Levenson, carried out decades of research on the communication patterns that predict whether married couples stay together. He later founded the Gottman Institute with Julie Gottman. Couples were recorded while they talked about a range of topics, including areas of disagreement. Observers then coded the recordings using detailed checklists of behaviour, sometimes alongside physiological measures such as heart rate. The researchers followed the couples for years to see who stayed together. From this work, Gottman (1994) named four patterns of communication that, when they occur together, strongly predict separation. He called them the four horsemen of the apocalypse. The patterns can appear in any relationship, so the examples below use friends, family and teammates.

    HorsemanWhat it isThe constructive opposite
    CriticismA personal attack that assumes the other person is to blame. "You always leave the kitchen in a mess because you don't care about anyone else in this house."A complaint about the specific behaviour, ideally starting with "I". "I feel stressed when the dishes are left in the sink. Can we agree on a rota?"
    ContemptMockery, sarcasm or hostile humour that signals disrespect, such as eye-rolling while the other person speaks.Respect and appreciation, expressed openly.
    DefensivenessProtecting yourself from attack, often by shifting the blame. It is a natural reaction, but in most conflicts both sides share some responsibility.Accepting part of the responsibility.
    StonewallingWithdrawing from the interaction and shutting down. It tends to follow very intense emotions, often after conflict has happened many times.Calming down, then coming back to the conversation. Chapter 10 returns to this.

    Key study 1: Gottman and Krokoff (1989), conflict and satisfaction over time

    AimTo find out which patterns of interaction during conflict go with marital satisfaction at the time, and which predict changes in satisfaction over the following three years.
    ParticipantsTwo samples of married couples in the USA. In the first, 25 couples were chosen from a larger group of 52 so that they varied widely in satisfaction; they had been married for about 24 years on average. The second sample was 30 couples who had been married for about 4 years on average.
    ProcedureA longitudinal study using observation and questionnaires, which the researchers called microanalytic observation. At Time 1, couples completed marital satisfaction questionnaires. They were then video-recorded in the laboratory for 15 minutes while they discussed a continuing area of disagreement in their marriage. Couples in the first sample also discussed a second disagreement at home and audio-recorded it themselves. Observers coded the recordings using three different coding schemes, with categories such as agree, humour, smile, laugh, excuse, deny responsibility and criticise. About three years later (Time 2), the couples who could be contacted completed the satisfaction questionnaires again, and the change in satisfaction was the outcome measure.
    FindingsDefensiveness (including whining), stubbornness and withdrawal from the interaction predicted a decline in satisfaction over time. Two of these match the four horsemen: defensiveness, and withdrawal (stonewalling). Disagreement and the expression of anger went with unhappiness at the time, but predicted improvement in satisfaction over the three years. For wives, positive verbal behaviour and compliance went with satisfaction at the time, but predicted a decline over time. Behaviour in the laboratory was linked with how couples interacted in the home recordings.
    ConclusionThe patterns that go with satisfaction now are not the same as the patterns that predict satisfaction later. Engaging with conflict is not harmful in itself. Conflict handled with defensiveness, stubbornness and withdrawal predicts decline, while conflict that is faced openly may cause bad feelings in the moment but help the relationship in the long term.
    What happened nextWoody and Costanzo (1990) criticised the way change in satisfaction was calculated, arguing that the surprising findings could partly be a statistical artefact. Gottman's later work developed the four horsemen model.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section C on bias, measurement, causality or change; Paper 2 Section B practice (a longitudinal observational study).
    Evaluate it withBias: couples who know they are being filmed may behave more politely than usual (demand characteristics); the home recordings were one check on this. Fewer couples could be contacted again in the second sample than in the first, so the Time 2 results may not represent everyone who started. Measurement: a 15-minute conversation is turned into coded categories, which may miss meaning; using three coding schemes reduces the risk that the results depend on one scheme. Causality: the study is correlational; couples cannot be assigned to argue constructively or destructively. Bias in samples: small samples of married American couples.

    How useful is research on communication?

    Strengths. Gottman's research observes behaviour directly instead of relying only on what people say about themselves. It is longitudinal, so it can test whether patterns predict what happens later. It uses detailed coding schemes and, in Gottman and Krokoff (1989), more than one scheme and both laboratory and home recordings. Its findings have practical uses, which Chapter 10 explores.

    Limitations. Couples cannot be randomly assigned to communicate in a particular way, so the evidence is correlational. Laboratory conversations are short and observed, and coding turns rich behaviour into categories. Much of the research uses married American couples, so applying it to friendships, families or other cultures needs other evidence. Researchers who expect a pattern may see it more easily, which is a form of confirmation bias.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to research on communication
    BiasLaboratory observation risks demand characteristics, samples are small and mostly American, and researchers' expectations are hard to control. Gottman and Krokoff used three coding schemes to check that the results did not depend on one.
    MeasurementCommunication is measured by coding recorded behaviour or by self-report. Several different measures exist for the same construct, and a short conversation stands in for a whole relationship.
    CausalityResearchers cannot randomly assign couples to a constructive or an aggressive conversation: people would only be playing a role. So the research is correlational, and conclusions depend on many studies pointing the same way.
    PerspectiveCommunication is sociocultural, but also cognitive (at each turn someone decides what to say) and biological (emotions during an argument involve chemical messengers). All three are needed.
    ChangeCommunication patterns change over a relationship, and they predict whether people will stay satisfied over the years.
    ResponsibilityRecording people as they argue about sensitive topics needs informed consent, a clear right to withdraw and confidential data. Researchers also have a duty to be evidence-based voices when untested advice is everywhere.
