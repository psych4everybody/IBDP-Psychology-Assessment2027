    Memory has a biological basis

    Encoding, storage and retrieval all happen in a physical brain. A biological factor is any part of our biology that can affect a behaviour or mental process. This chapter focuses on two: brain structures, especially the hippocampus, and the way the brain changes when we learn. Two questions run through it. Are different kinds of memory handled by different parts of the brain? And how do we know?

    The hippocampus and memory consolidation

    The hippocampus is a curved structure deep inside each temporal lobe (its name comes from the Greek word for seahorse). We have two, one in each hemisphere. It sits in the medial temporal lobe, close to the amygdala and the entorhinal cortex.

    A new memory is fragile. A new classmate's name can be lost within seconds. Memory consolidation is the process by which a new, fragile memory becomes stable enough to be stored in long-term memory. The hippocampus is thought to play a central role in consolidating declarative memories: memories you can put into words. These include episodic memories (personal events, such as your first day at this school) and semantic memories (facts, such as the capital of Oman).

    One widely held view is that the hippocampus works like an index. The parts of an experience (what you saw, heard and where you were) are processed in different areas of the cortex, and the hippocampus links them so the memory can be retrieved as a whole. Over time, the memory may become stored across the cortex and depend less on the hippocampus. This would explain why hippocampal damage can leave old memories in place while making new ones very hard to form. Researchers still debate how fully this happens.

    Other kinds of memory depend on other areas. Procedural memory (skills such as typing) is linked to the cerebellum and basal ganglia. Short-term and working memory are linked to areas such as the prefrontal cortex.

    Localization of function and its limits

    Localization of function

    Localization of function is the idea that specific areas of the brain carry out specific functions. It explains why damage to one area can cause a very particular problem while other abilities stay normal. The table above is an example: different kinds of memory are linked to different areas.

    The strongest evidence comes from case studies of brain damage. If damage to area X causes problem Y, area X seems to be needed for Y. When one patient loses ability A but keeps B, and another shows the opposite pattern, this is a double dissociation: strong evidence that the two abilities rely on different systems.

    Localization has three limits as an explanation of memory.

    • Needed is not the same as stored. If hippocampal damage stops new memories forming, the hippocampus is needed to form them. That does not show memories are kept inside it.
    • Memory uses networks. Recalling a friend's face involves visual areas, language areas, the hippocampus and emotional areas working together. A better question than "where is memory?" is "which areas work together, and what does each add?"
    • Damage is rarely neat. Surgery, stroke or injury usually affects several neighbouring structures, so it is hard to say which one caused the problem.

    Neuroplasticity: the brain changes when we learn

    Neuroplasticity

    Neuroplasticity is the brain's ability to change its structure and function in response to experience. Scientists once believed the adult brain was mostly fixed. We now know it keeps changing throughout life.

    At the smallest level, learning changes the connections between neurons. When two neurons are repeatedly active together, the synapse between them can become stronger, so a signal passes more easily next time. This is long-term potentiation (LTP), one of the main proposed mechanisms of learning. Neurons can also grow new branches (dendrites), and unused connections can be weakened or removed (synaptic pruning).

    These small changes can add up to differences a brain scan can detect, such as more grey matter in a heavily used region. After a stroke, intensive practice can help other areas take over some lost functions. This is functional reorganisation. It also puts a limit on localization: if areas can change and take over from each other, the link between one area and one function is not completely fixed.

    Real-life examples

    • Learning the roads. A new delivery rider in Dubai relies on a phone map for every order. After months on the same streets, he can picture shortcuts without it. Research on London taxi drivers (Key study 2) links intensive spatial learning to change in the hippocampus. No study has scanned delivery riders, so this is a prediction, not a finding.
    • Rehabilitation after a stroke. In rehabilitation centres in Abu Dhabi and Dubai, therapists ask patients to practise movements and words many times. The idea behind this is neuroplasticity: repeated practice may help other areas take over lost functions.
    • Football training. A young player at a Sharjah academy practises a turn until it is automatic. This is procedural memory. Like HM, the player keeps the skill without needing to recall each training session.

    How brain imaging techniques are used to study memory

    Brain imaging techniques

    Before imaging, researchers could only infer brain function from damage, or study brains after death. Brain imaging techniques let them study living, healthy brains.

    MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) uses a strong magnetic field and radio waves to produce detailed pictures of the brain's structure. It shows what the brain looks like, not what it is doing. Structural MRI can measure the size of a region, for example to compare hippocampal volume in two groups, or to track change in one person over time.

    fMRI (functional MRI) uses the same scanner to detect changes in blood oxygen. An active area uses more oxygen, and oxygen-rich blood flows to it. fMRI picks this up and shows which areas are more active while a person does a task, such as learning a word list. Activity during the task is compared with activity during a control task.

    One point is easy to miss. fMRI shows that an area is active during a memory task. Damage studies show that an area is needed for it. An area can be active simply because it is connected to the area doing the main work, which is why researchers combine both kinds of evidence.

    Key study 1: Scoville and Milner (1957), and Milner's work with patient HM

    AimTo describe the memory problems of patients who had surgery on the medial temporal lobes, and to identify which region was linked to the loss of memory.
    ParticipantsThe 1957 paper described ten patients who had medial temporal lobe surgery. The clearest case was HM, later named as Henry Molaison. He had severe epilepsy that medication did not control, and in 1953, aged 27, he had surgery to reduce his seizures.
    ProcedureA case study. The surgeon, William Scoville, removed the medial temporal lobe on both sides, including much of the hippocampus, most of the amygdala and the entorhinal cortex. Brenda Milner and later researchers tested HM for decades with interviews, IQ tests, memory tests and skill tasks. In mirror drawing, HM traced a star while seeing his hand only in a mirror.
    FindingsHM had severe anterograde amnesia: he forgot daily events almost as quickly as they happened. He also lost some memories from the years before the operation (retrograde amnesia), but most childhood memories were intact. His IQ was 112 after surgery, slightly higher than the 104 measured before it. His short-term memory was spared: he could hold a short list of numbers in mind if he was not distracted. In mirror drawing (Milner, 1962), he improved steadily over three days, although he had no memory of doing the task before.
    ConclusionThe hippocampal region is needed to form new long-term declarative memories, but not for short-term memory, intelligence or learning new motor skills. Memory is not one system, and different memory systems depend on different brain areas.
    What happened nextScoville estimated that he had removed about 8 cm of tissue on each side. MRI scans (Corkin et al., 1997) later showed the removal was a little over 5 cm, and that about 2 cm at the back of the hippocampus remained, although shrunken. HM died in 2008, aged 82.

    Evaluate it withCausality: HM was tested before and after the operation, so the memory loss can be linked to the surgery. But several structures were removed, not only the hippocampus, so the study cannot show which one caused the amnesia. Measurement: the damage was first estimated by the surgeon and only measured accurately decades later with MRI. Bias: HM was one person with years of epilepsy and medication, so his brain may not have been typical. Responsibility: HM could not remember agreeing to take part, which raises questions about consent over decades of testing.

    Key study 2: Maguire et al. (2000), London taxi drivers

    AimTo investigate whether the hippocampus of people with extensive experience of navigation differs in structure from that of people without this experience.
    Participants16 right-handed male licensed London taxi drivers (mean age 44), who had driven taxis for 1.5 to 42 years (mean 14.3). All had passed "the Knowledge", a demanding test of London's streets that took about two years of training on average. The comparison group was 50 healthy right-handed men who did not drive taxis, taken from an existing database of MRI scans.
    ProcedureA natural group comparison with a correlational part. All participants had a structural MRI scan. Grey matter was compared across the whole brain by computer, and hippocampal volume was also measured slice by slice against 16 age-matched men from the comparison group. Hippocampal volume was then correlated with time spent as a taxi driver.
    FindingsTaxi drivers had a larger posterior (back) hippocampus than the comparison group. The comparison group had a larger anterior (front) hippocampus. There was no difference in the total volume of the hippocampus. The longer someone had been a taxi driver, the larger the posterior part of their right hippocampus (r = 0.6), and the smaller the anterior part.
    ConclusionThe posterior hippocampus may store a mental map of the environment, and it may grow with heavy use. This supports both localization of function (spatial memory is linked to a specific part of the hippocampus) and neuroplasticity (adult brain structure can change with experience).
    What happened nextMaguire et al. (2006) compared 18 taxi drivers with 17 London bus drivers, who follow fixed routes. The groups were matched for driving experience and stress, and taxi drivers still had more grey matter in the mid-posterior hippocampus. Woollett and Maguire (2011) then scanned 79 trainee taxi drivers and 31 controls before training and again three to four years later. At the start there were no differences in hippocampal grey matter. Only the 39 trainees who qualified showed an increase in posterior hippocampal grey matter; those who failed and the controls showed no change. The qualified drivers were also worse than controls at recalling a complex drawing after a delay, so the change may come with a cost.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example of neuroplasticity or brain imaging techniques); Paper 1 Section B; Paper 1 Section C on causality, change or measurement; Paper 2 Section B practice (a correlational study using brain imaging).
    Evaluate it withCausality: the 2000 study is correlational, so the direction is unclear: did taxi driving enlarge the hippocampus, or did people with a larger posterior hippocampus become taxi drivers? The 2011 longitudinal study supports the first answer, but trainees chose their career, so it is still not a true experiment. Measurement: MRI measured grey matter, not memory, and the 2000 study did not test navigation memory. Bias: all participants were men from one city doing one job.

    Biological reductionism

    Biological reductionism

    Biological reductionism is explaining a complex behaviour or mental process in terms of simpler biological parts, such as brain structures, neurons or chemicals. Biological research on memory is reductionist by design: it breaks memory into parts that can be measured. This gives precise, testable predictions (for example, "hippocampal damage will impair new declarative memories but not new motor skills").

    But a purely biological explanation leaves things out. The taxi drivers did not simply grow larger hippocampi: they spent years studying maps and the streets. Only about half of the trainees in the 2011 study qualified, which suggests motivation, study strategies and circumstances also mattered. Two students with similar brains may remember very different amounts from the same lesson because of how they revise or how anxious they feel. A full explanation of memory needs cognitive and sociocultural levels as well as the biological one.

    How useful is the biological approach to memory?

    Strengths. It gives some of the clearest evidence in psychology that memory is several systems, not one. Case studies such as HM and imaging studies such as Maguire et al. use very different methods but point the same way: the hippocampus is central to forming new declarative and spatial memories. Imaging lets researchers track change in healthy brains over time. The research has practical uses in surgery and rehabilitation.

    Limitations. Much of the evidence comes from a few unusual people. Brain damage is rarely limited to one structure, so causal claims about single areas are difficult. Imaging studies of groups are usually correlational, and fMRI shows activity, not necessity. Biological explanations can be reductionist, leaving out strategies, motivation and context.
