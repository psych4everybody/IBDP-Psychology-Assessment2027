On this page

    Aim

    Write so the argument and the evaluation are easy to see

    Discussion and evaluation is the highest-weighted criterion (8 of 30 marks), and the guide expects evaluation throughout the essay. Good writing makes your argument visible: concise descriptions, specific evaluation, and wording that matches the strength of the evidence.

    By the end of this stage you can:

    • write an introduction that sets up the whole essay, including a method paragraph
    • describe a study accurately in two or three sentences
    • evaluate inside each paragraph, tied to your question and a concept
    • match your wording to the evidence, and stay within 4,000 words
    Our example questionTo what extent does taking notes by hand rather than on a laptop improve students' learning from lectures?
    Start well

    What the introduction does

    1. Context. Why does the question matter, in the real world and in psychology?
    2. Key terms. Define the variables in your question as the studies measured them.
    3. The question. State your research question exactly.
    4. Method. A short paragraph on how you searched and selected studies (from your search log, Stage 3).
    5. Direction. One or two sentences on how the essay will proceed.

    Many writers draft the introduction last, once they know what the essay actually argues.

    Be concise

    Describe a study in two or three sentences

    The guide asks you to describe studies so that you show you understand the aim, method and findings, concisely and accurately. Detailed procedures and statistics are not needed.

    Who and what: [Researchers] (year) [design] [who took part] to test [aim]. Key detail: [the feature of the method your evaluation will use]. Finding: [what they found, in words].

    Too much and too little

    "The researchers did a study where they got participants and put them in two groups and gave them a lecture and a test, and the results were t(65) = 2.10, p = .04."

    Concise and useful

    "Mueller and Oppenheimer (2014) randomly assigned university students to take notes on laptops or by hand while watching short recorded talks. Laptop users copied more of the talks word for word and scored lower on conceptual, but not factual, questions."

    Evaluate

    Evaluate inside every paragraph

    Each body paragraph can follow the same pattern: point (link to your question), evidence (the study, concisely), evaluation (a specific strength or limitation and what it means for your question), link (how far this supports your claim).

    Tag each evaluative sentence with one of the six concepts. If one concept never appears, ask whether it should.

    BiasCausalityChangeMeasurementPerspectiveResponsibility
    Avoid the formula

    The IB guidance warns against discussing the same points (generalisability, ethics, gender) for every study. Evaluate what genuinely matters for each study and your question.

    Precise terms

    Terminology traps

    • "Experiment" only for studies that manipulate an independent variable. A survey or a correlational study is not an experiment.
    • "Reliable because it can be replicated." Replicability is not the same as reliability. Say what was replicated, and by whom.
    • "Ecological validity" is about the setting and tasks, not the sample. For the sample, talk about generalisability or sampling bias.
    • "Prove" almost never belongs in an EE.
    • "Credibility" is a technical term for qualitative research, not a synonym for "believable".
    The rules

    Word count and presentation

    Counts towards 4,000

    • Introduction, body and conclusion
    • Quotations
    • Footnotes that explain or comment (not just reference)

    Does not count

    • Title page and contents page
    • Tables, charts and diagrams
    • The reference list, and footnotes used only for referencing
    • The reflective statement (written separately)
    Over the limit?

    Examiners stop reading at 4,000 words, and they do not have to read appendices. Anything essential to your argument must be in the main text. Ask your coordinator how your school counts in-text citations, and plan as if they count.

    Presentation: a title page, a contents page, clear headings, page numbers, one consistent referencing style, and a final check of the PDF before you upload it.

    Finish well

    What the conclusion does

    • Answers the research question directly, including "to what extent".
    • Draws together the main strands and the most important limitations.
    • Says what is still uncertain and what future research could test.
    • Adds no new evidence.

    Exercise 1 · 20 minutes

    Fix the paragraph

    This paragraph, written for the example question, has at least five problems. List them, then rewrite it.

    Mueller and Oppenheimer (2014) did an experiment on students at Princeton. They watched talks and took notes on laptops or by hand, and then did a test. The results were significant (p < .05). This proves that handwriting is better. However, the study was a lab experiment so it lacks ecological validity, and the sample was small and only American so it cannot be generalised. It may also be unethical because participants were not told the aim.

    EE-07-1Keep your answer in your researcher's reflection space.

    Model answer

    Try the exercise first, then compare.

    What is wrong with the original: it describes the study but never links it to the question; "proves" overclaims; the statistics add nothing; the evaluation is generic (lab, small sample, American) and could be written about any study; the ethics point is unsupported and irrelevant to what the study can tell us.

    A rewritten paragraph:

    The strongest support for handwriting comes from Mueller and Oppenheimer (2014), whose students watched short recorded talks and took notes either on laptops or by hand. Laptop users copied more of the talks word for word and scored lower on conceptual questions, though not on factual ones. This suggests that the medium matters mainly for deeper understanding, which is the kind of learning my question focuses on. However, students were tested soon after the talk without reviewing their notes, so the study shows an effect at the point of encoding only. Since a direct replication with a larger sample found no significant difference (Urry et al., 2021), this evidence offers only cautious support for the claim.

    The rewrite: describes concisely, reports what matters without statistics, links to the question, evaluates a feature specific to this study, and ends by weighing the evidence.

    Exercise 2 · 15 minutes

    Hedging ladder

    Rewrite each claim so the wording matches the evidence behind it.

    1. "Mueller and Oppenheimer (2014) proved that handwriting improves learning."
    2. "A survey showed that using laptops in class causes lower grades."
    3. "Handwritten notes always lead to better test scores."
    4. "Urry et al. (2021) disproved Mueller and Oppenheimer (2014)."
    5. "These studies show that all students learn better by hand."
    6. "The interview study shows that laptops reduce learning."
    EE-07-2Keep your answer in your researcher's reflection space.

    Model answer

    Try the exercise first, then compare.

    #ProblemBetter
    1One study cannot "prove" anything.Mueller and Oppenheimer (2014) found evidence that handwriting may help conceptual learning.
    2A correlation cannot show cause.Students who used laptops more in class tended to have lower grades, although other differences between students may explain this.
    3"Always" ignores the mixed evidence.Handwriting has sometimes, but not consistently, been linked to better test performance.
    4A failed replication does not "disprove" the original.Urry et al. (2021) did not replicate the conceptual advantage, which weakens confidence in the original finding.
    5Overgeneralises from university students to everyone.Among university students tested on short talks, the advantage was small; whether it applies to school students is less clear.
    6Interviews describe experience; they do not show an effect on learning.Students in the interview study described laptops as distracting, which suggests a possible reason for differences in learning.

    Check yourself

    Five quick questions

    What goes in the method paragraph of a literature review?

    How you searched (databases, search terms) and how you selected studies (what you included and excluded, and why).

    Where should evaluation appear in your essay?

    Throughout, inside each body paragraph, not only in a final section. The guide says so explicitly.

    A correlational study finds laptop use is linked to lower grades. Which verb fits?

    "Is associated with" or "is linked to". Not "causes".

    Do tables and the reference list count towards 4,000 words?

    No. The introduction, body, conclusion, quotations and explanatory footnotes do.

    Can you put an important part of your argument in an appendix?

    No. Examiners do not have to read appendices, so anything essential must be in the main text.
