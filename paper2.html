    What Paper 2 is testing

    Paper 2 is about how psychologists find things out. Section A asks about the class practicals you ran yourself. Section B gives you a study you have never seen and asks you to discuss it through the concepts.

    35marks in total
    90minutes
    20 + 15Section A + Section B
    35% / 25%of your grade at SL / HL
    The single most important rule for Section A

    Section A marks how you ran the method, not what you found out. Write what you did and why it matters for the research. Do not write about what memory, phones or stress "turned out" to be.

    At a glance

    Section A follows the same template every year

    The method and the concept change, but the four question types stay the same. That makes Section A the most predictable 20 marks in the course.

    Rule of thumb

    About 2 minutes per mark in Section A. A half-finished Q4 can still earn marks; a perfect Q1 cannot earn more than 4. Leave the last line, not the last question.

    Method, not sub-type

    Questions name the method (an interview, an observation, an experiment), not the exact type, because different classes may have used different versions. Describe the version you actually used.

    Command terms

    The five verbs of Paper 2

    The 30-second habit

    Underline, circle, plan box

    This protects every mark in Section A. Do it before every answer.

    1. Underline the method the question names: experiment, observation or interview.
    2. Circle the concept or command term.
    3. Fill the plan box with the method and the practical before your first sentence.
    Why it matters

    The most common way to lose Q2 is to write a good explanation about the wrong practical. When a question names the experiment and the answer describes the interview, a correct explanation still earns very little. The plan box fixes this.

    Start here

    One card per practical

    Section A asks about the practicals you ran. The detail that moves an answer from 2 to 4 is detail from your own practical: a number, a step, a rule you used. Learn these cards until you can write each one from memory.

    Where a card says from your notes, add the detail recorded in class. The guide recommends keeping a short record of each practical (aim, procedure, sampling, findings and ethics). These cards are that record.

    Experiment

    Learning and cognition
    Aim
    To investigate whether a 30-second wait before recall affects how many words people remember, compared with recalling immediately.
    Method
    Experiment with two conditions. Independent samples: each participant was in one group only.
    IV
    Whether recall came after a 30-second wait or straight away.
    DV
    Number of words correctly recalled out of 20, written down.
    Procedure
    Both groups saw the same list of 20 words. One group wrote down what they remembered immediately. The other waited 30 seconds first. Scores were counted and compared.
    Data
    Quantitative: a score out of 20 for each participant.
    Ethics
    Informed consent, right to withdraw, debrief, anonymised scores. Low risk of harm.
    Watch out
    There was no task during the wait, so people could rehearse the words. The wait and the word display were not timed exactly. Both weaken internal validity.
    From your notes
    Sampling technique, group sizes, how participants were put into groups.

    Observation

    Human development
    Aim
    To investigate how people use devices in everyday settings.
    Method
    Naturalistic, covert, non-participant observation, at home or in a cafe. Covert means those observed did not know. Non-participant means the observer did not join in.
    Recorded
    In each 15-minute block: the number of uses (pick-ups), the total duration of use, and an approximate age band.
    Data
    Quantitative: counts and durations, grouped by age band.
    Ethics
    Covert, so consent could not be asked. Privacy protected: no names, no images, age bands only. Observed in everyday settings only.
    Watch out
    One observer judging what counts as one use. Ages are estimates. Who happens to be in the setting is not controlled. No IV, so patterns only, not causes.
    From your notes
    Sampling technique (time or event), the rule that decided "one use", number of observers.

    Interview

    Health and well-being
    Aim
    To investigate how a Grade 12 student describes coping with the stress of university applications.
    Method
    Semi-structured interview: an opening, a set of core questions, and follow-up questions that depend on the answers.
    Participant
    One Grade 12 student who is applying to university now and does not take psychology.
    Recording
    Audio recorded with permission, transcribed, and the transcript checked against the recording.
    Analysis
    Coding the transcript, for example into problem-focused and emotion-focused coping, or inductive content analysis into themes. Write the one you actually did.
    Data
    Qualitative: the participant's own words.
    Ethics
    Consent to take part and to recording, right to withdraw, a code instead of a name, recording deleted within 7 days, debrief.
    Watch out
    Leading questions, social desirability (wanting to look organised or calm), interviewer effects, and judgement when coding.
    From your notes
    One core question and one follow-up you asked. Two codes or themes you found, with a short quote for each.
    Check yourself

    Can you...

    State the IV and DV of our experiment.

    IV: whether recall came after a 30-second wait or immediately. DV: the number of words correctly recalled out of 20.

    Our observation was naturalistic, covert and non-participant. Explain each word in one line.

    Naturalistic: behaviour recorded where it normally happens. Covert: those observed did not know. Non-participant: the observer did not join in.

    Why could we not ask for consent in the observation, and what did we do instead?

    It was covert, so asking would have changed behaviour. We protected privacy instead: everyday settings only, no names, no images, age bands only.

    What makes our interview semi-structured rather than structured?

    The follow-up questions. Core questions stayed the same, but follow-ups depended on what the participant said.

    Q1 [4]

    Describe how you used the method

    Typical stem: "Describe how you used an interview in your class practical and its aim and procedure. [4]"

    The markscheme is clear that an answer which only describes the method in general, and not how it was used in your practical, stays in the low band. Start with your practical, not with a definition.

    The full-marks list

    The six things a 4 out of 4 contains

    1. The aim, as a statement or a research question.
    2. The type of method and what it meant in practice. For the interview: opening, core and follow-up questions.
    3. Who, and how they were chosen: the participant or sample, the sampling technique and a reason.
    4. One real detail: for the interview, a question you asked and a follow-up; for the experiment, the exact conditions; for the observation, the rule for one use.
    5. How it was recorded and processed: audio, transcript, tally sheet, and how it was checked.
    6. The ethics you actually followed.

    Q2 [4]

    Explain a concept in relation to a practical

    Typical stem: "Explain the concept of bias in relation to the interview in your class practical. [4]"

    Q2 names one concept and one practical. Research methods questions most often use Bias Causality Measurement Responsibility, but the guide does not limit Q2 to those four, so be ready for Change and Perspective too.

    Concepts

    Meaning, what it looks like, and the trap

    The first four come up most often. Change and perspective are less common on Paper 2, but they can still be asked.

    ConceptMeaningIn research it looks likeNot this
    BiasA limitation in objective thinking: a filter of experience or preference that distorts how data are collected or read.Researcher bias, participant bias such as social desirability, sampling bias, confirmation bias.Any mistake at all.
    CausalityWhether a change in one variable produces a change in another.Only a method that manipulates an IV and controls other variables can test cause and effect.Two things happening together (that is correlation).
    MeasurementHow a behaviour is operationalised so it can be recorded reliably and represented validly.Counts and scores, or transcripts and codes. Qualitative data are measured too.Generalisability.
    ResponsibilityThe researcher's ethical duties to participants and to how findings are used.Consent, withdrawal, anonymity, debrief. These can change who takes part and what they say.The participant's responsibility.
    ChangeWhether, and how, a study can show behaviour shifting over time or after an intervention.Before-and-after measures, repeated measures and longitudinal designs; practice and order effects; maturation. A one-off measurement is a snapshot, not change.Any difference between two groups measured once.
    PerspectiveThe approach and assumptions that frame a study, including whose viewpoint the data capture.Quantitative or qualitative; etic (outsider) or emic (insider); inductive or deductive analysis; the researcher's own viewpoint (reflexivity).Your personal opinion of the study.

    Meanings adapted from the IB Psychology guide (first assessment 2027).

    How to answer

    Define, link, limit

    1

    Define

    One sentence: what the concept means.

    2

    Link

    Tie it to this practical with a real detail: a variable, a number, a step.

    3

    Limit and effect

    One limitation, and what it did to the data.

    Worked example: measurement and the experiment

    DefineMeasurement is how a variable is turned into something we can record and score, and how accurately that record captures it.
    LinkIn our memory experiment, the DV was the number of words correctly recalled out of 20, written down. Both groups saw the same list and recalled it the same way, so their scores could be compared. The IV was a 30-second wait before recall, compared with immediate recall.
    LimitThe wait group had no task during the 30 seconds, so they could rehearse the words. The IV measured waiting, not blocked rehearsal, which may have raised their scores and shrunk the difference between the groups.

    Worked example: responsibility and the interview

    DefineResponsibility is the researcher's duty to protect participants and to treat their information ethically.
    LinkOur participant, a Grade 12 student, gave consent to take part and to being recorded, was told they could withdraw, was identified by a code instead of a name, and had the recording deleted within 7 days. We debriefed them at the end.
    LimitBecause the topic was stress, even neutral questions could raise worry, so the participant needed to be able to skip a question or stop. The cost is that skipped questions leave gaps in the transcript, so the data may under-represent the hardest experiences.
    Learn the moves, not the paragraph

    On the day, the concept and the practical will probably be different from these examples.

    Q4 [6]

    Design a study

    Typical stem: "Design an observation to investigate the same topic you investigated in your class practical. [6]"

    The topic-switching rule

    The question gives you the method (what you design) and the topic (what you investigate). Use the features of the method it names, on the topic of the practical it names. An experiment on device use is an experiment with an IV, not the observation again. An interview on device use is a semi-structured interview about phone use, not the observation again.

    Design checklists

    What each design needs

    Experiment

    • An aim.
    • An IV with two named conditions, and an operationalised DV: exactly what is counted or timed, and how.
    • The design: true or quasi; independent samples or repeated measures.
    • Sample and allocation: who, how many, how chosen, how put into conditions.
    • Controls and standardised instructions.
    • Ethics: consent, right to withdraw, debrief; justify any cover story.

    Observation

    • An aim, and the behaviour to be observed.
    • The type, with a reason: naturalistic or controlled; overt or covert; participant or non-participant.
    • Behaviour categories defined so another observer would record the same thing.
    • Setting and sampling: where, when, time or event sampling.
    • Recording: tally sheet or timer; number of observers; an inter-rater reliability check if there are two.
    • Ethics: public settings only, no names or images, justify any covert design.

    Interview

    • A named type with a reason, such as semi-structured, so core questions stay consistent and follow-ups explore reasons.
    • An aim on the topic named.
    • A sample with technique, reason and number.
    • Two example questions that are open and not leading.
    • Recording and transcription, checked against the audio.
    • Analysis: coding into categories or content analysis.
    • Ethics: consent to take part and to recording, withdrawal, codes, deletion, debrief.
    Is it really an experiment?

    A survey that asks about screen time and stress changes nothing, so it is a correlation. Randomly allocating people to read for 15 minutes with their phone face-up or in a bag, and counting pick-ups, has two conditions and one counted DV, so it is an experiment. Check for the IV before you write.

    Section B [15]

    Discuss an unseen study through the concepts

    You get a short description of a study you have never seen. It may be experimental or non-experimental, and it is set in one of the four contexts. The question lists four or more concepts and asks you to discuss the study with reference to two or more of them.

    The specimen question reads: "Discuss the following study with reference to two or more of the following concepts: bias, causality, measurement, and/or responsibility. [15]"

    Two concepts can be enough

    You can go deep on two concepts or wider with three. Both are accepted. Two well-developed concepts usually beat four thin ones.

    Step 1

    Read the question first, then annotate the study

    1. Circle the command term (discuss) and the concepts listed.
    2. Underline every detail that can carry a concept: the sample, the procedure, the measures, the results, the debrief.
    3. Tag each underline with a concept letter: B, C, M, R.
    4. Choose two or three concepts where the study gives you the most to say, and say to yourself why.
    Step 2

    Build each paragraph with the five moves

    1. Define the concept in your own words.
    2. Use a specific detail from the study. Quote a number or a step.
    3. Explain why it matters for the concept. Ask one more question: this is where the analysis is.
    4. Give a limitation or counterpoint.
    5. Link back to the question: what does this mean for the study's conclusion?
    What the top band adds

    Specific and effective references to the study, concept links that are well developed throughout, precise terminology, and a reasoned, clearly stated conclusion. If you never mention a detail from the study, you cannot get past the bottom bands.

    Going further

    Show how the concepts interact

    Strong answers notice where one concept affects another. The specimen markscheme gives examples like these:

    • Weak operationalisation of variables (Measurement) can undermine internal validity (Causality).
    • Gender or cultural assumptions (Bias) can raise questions about how responsibly the research was done (Responsibility).
    • Cultural assumptions (Bias) can affect how objectively behaviour is recorded (Measurement).
    Practice

    A practice study

    Fictional study, written for practice. Context: human relationships

    Researchers at a university in the UAE wanted to find out whether students change their opinions to match a group, and whether this depends on how strongly the students identify with that group. They invited 96 first-year undergraduates (all studying psychology, aged 18 to 20, 71% female) to take part in return for course credit.

    Before the main task, all participants completed a six-item questionnaire in which they rated statements such as "I am proud to be a student of this university" from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Scores were added to give an identification score from 6 to 30.

    Participants were then told that they would join an online group chat with four other students from their university and rate 12 posters for a campus festival from 1 (poor) to 10 (excellent). In reality, the other four "students" were computer-controlled accounts. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two conditions. In the majority condition, the four accounts posted their ratings first, and these were always at least 3 points lower than the average rating the same poster had received in an earlier pilot test. In the control condition, participants rated the posters with no other ratings visible. Conformity was measured as the difference between each participant's rating and the pilot average.

    In the majority condition, ratings were on average 1.9 points lower than the pilot average, compared with 0.2 points lower in the control condition. Within the majority condition, participants with identification scores above the median conformed more (mean shift of 2.4 points) than those below the median (mean shift of 1.4 points).

    After the session, all participants were told that the other accounts were computer-controlled and were offered the chance to withdraw their data. Four participants did so. Fourteen participants said they had suspected that the accounts were not real students.

    Discuss this study with reference to two or more of the following concepts: bias, causality, measurement and/or responsibility. [15]

    Three more practice studies are in the random question tool (choose Section B). Annotate the study, choose two or three concepts, and fill the planning grid below on paper. Then reveal the plan notes to compare. These are notes for a plan, not a model essay: the writing should be yours.

    Start with the question type you lost most marks on. Do each one timed (2 minutes per mark), with a plan box first.

    Q1 style [4]

    1. Describe how you used an experiment in your class practical, including its aim and procedure.
    2. Describe how you used an observation in your class practical, including its aim and procedure.

    Q2 style [4]

    1. Explain the concept of bias in relation to the observation in your class practical.
    2. Explain the concept of causality in relation to the interview in your class practical.
    3. Explain the concept of responsibility in relation to the experiment in your class practical.
    4. Explain the concept of measurement in relation to the observation in your class practical.

    Q3 style [6]

    1. Compare and contrast the research methodology of the experiment used in your class practical with the research methodology of an observation.
    2. Compare and contrast the research methodology of the experiment used in your class practical with the research methodology of an interview.
    3. Compare and contrast the research methodology of the observation used in your class practical with the research methodology of an interview.

    Q4 style [6]

    1. Design an observation to investigate the same topic you investigated in your interview class practical.
    2. Design an experiment to investigate the same topic you investigated in your interview class practical.
    3. Design an interview to investigate the same topic you investigated in your observation class practical.
    After each one

    Mark yourself in three steps

    1. Check the plan box. Did you name the right practical and method?
    2. Highlight the words that match the top band for that question. If you cannot highlight anything, you are in the lower band.
    3. Write one fix for next time, in a sentence.
