On this page

    Aim

    From a pile of studies to one argument

    Criterion C asks for a line of argument: a logical progression of ideas that begins with your question, where each point builds on the one before and leads to a well-supported conclusion. The guide also expects you to consider challenges such as counter-arguments or moderating variables.

    By the end of this stage you can:

    • state a main claim that answers your question
    • group your studies into strands that support, challenge and qualify it
    • write a fair counter-argument and answer it
    • check the order of your argument with a reverse outline
    Our example questionTo what extent does taking notes by hand rather than on a laptop improve students' learning from lectures?
    Take a position

    Your main claim

    Your main claim is your answer to the question in one or two sentences. It can change as you read, and it should. Write a first version early and revise it.

    Weak claim

    "There are arguments on both sides about laptops and handwriting."

    Strong claim

    "Handwriting may give a small advantage for conceptual learning, but the effect is smaller and less reliable than first reported, and depends mainly on whether students review their notes."

    The strong claim takes a position, says how much ("small", "less reliable") and names a condition ("depends on review"). Each part can be argued with evidence.
    Shape it

    Strands and a structure

    Group your studies by the job they do (from Stage 4), not one study per paragraph. A common shape for a psychology EE:

    1. Introduction. Context, why the question matters, key terms defined, your question, a short method paragraph (how you searched and selected studies), and where the essay is going.
    2. Background. The theory and concepts the reader needs, briefly.
    3. Body: the case for. The strongest supporting evidence, evaluated as you go.
    4. Body: the challenge. Evidence against, such as failed replications or contradictory findings.
    5. Body: it depends. Moderating variables that explain the conflict.
    6. Discussion. The evidence as a whole: recurring limitations, biases, what is still uncertain.
    7. Conclusion. A clear answer to the question that follows from everything before it. No new evidence.
    Mini-conclusions

    End each section with one sentence that says what it has shown and how it moves the argument on. It keeps you, and the examiner, on the line.

    Fair play

    Write a counter-argument

    1

    Acknowledge

    State the opposing view fairly, at its strongest.

    2

    Evidence

    Give the evidence for it.

    3

    Weaken fairly

    Show its limits with evidence or reasoning, not dismissal.

    4

    Restate

    Say what this means for your claim: does it survive, or does it need refining?

    A counter-argument that changes your claim is not a failure. It is what the IB guidance means by recognising complexity.

    Check the order

    The reverse outline

    Once you have a draft, write one sentence summarising each paragraph, in order. Then ask:

    • Does every sentence connect to the research question?
    • Does each point build on the one before? Could any two be swapped without loss? If so, the line is weak.
    • Is any paragraph doing two jobs, or no job?
    • Does the conclusion follow from the sentences above it?
    For supervisors

    Notes for supervisors

    Suggested use
    • Ask for the one-page argument map at the interim reflection session; it is the quickest way to see whether an essay has a line.
    • The paragraph-order exercise works well cut into strips on paper as well as online.

    Model answer

    Try the exercise first, then compare.

    QuestionTo what extent does taking notes by hand rather than on a laptop improve students' learning from lectures?
    Main claimHandwriting may give a small advantage, mainly for conceptual learning, but the effect is smaller and less reliable than first reported and seems to depend on whether students review their notes.
    Strand 1: supportMueller and Oppenheimer (2014): laptop users copied more of the lecture word for word and did worse on conceptual questions. Proposed reason: less processing while taking notes.
    Strand 2: challengeMorehead, Dunlosky and Rawson (2019) and Urry et al. (2021): replications found a weaker difference or no significant difference in test performance.
    Strand 3: qualifyFlanigan et al. (2024): across 24 studies, handwriting was linked to higher achievement mainly when notes were reviewed. Kiewra's (1989) distinction between encoding and reviewing explains why.
    Strongest counter-argumentLab studies remove distraction. In real classrooms laptops allow messaging and other off-task use, so the true cost of laptops may be larger than these studies show. (Find and evaluate studies of off-task use in real classes.)
    VerdictTo a limited extent: the medium matters less than how students process and review their notes, and most evidence comes from university students and short talks.
    Exercise 2 · 10 minutes

    Put the argument in order

    These six topic sentences come from one essay on the example question, shuffled. Give each a position from 1 to 6 to build a line of argument, then check.

    A. Taken together, the evidence suggests that the medium matters less than what students do with their notes afterwards.

    B. The case for handwriting rests mainly on one influential set of experiments.

    C. However, the largest direct replication found no significant difference in quiz performance.

    D. Note-taking is one of the most common ways students learn from lectures, and many schools now debate whether to allow laptops.

    E. A recent meta-analysis helps explain the conflict: the handwriting advantage appears mainly when students review their notes.

    F. One limitation runs through all of these studies: most tested university students on short recorded talks.

    EE-06-2Write one sentence explaining why your conclusion has to come where it does.

    Model answer

    Try the exercise first, then compare.

    One strong order: D, B, C, E, F, A.

    1. D sets the context and why the question matters.
    2. B presents the case for handwriting.
    3. C challenges it with the replication evidence.
    4. E explains the conflict with a moderating variable.
    5. F evaluates a limitation shared by all the studies.
    6. A reaches the conclusion, which follows from everything before it.

    Each step builds on the one before. That is what Criterion C calls a line of argument. E before C would not work: you cannot explain a conflict before you have shown there is one.

    Exercise 3 · 20 minutes

    Write a counter-argument

    Imagine an essay that argues laptops are as good as handwriting. Write the counter-argument paragraph it needs, using the four steps: acknowledge, evidence, weaken fairly, restate.

    EE-06-3Keep your answer in your researcher's reflection space.

    Model answer

    Try the exercise first, then compare.

    Model counter-argument paragraph (for an essay arguing that laptops are as good as handwriting):

    Acknowledge. It could be argued that laptops are worse in real classrooms than these experiments suggest. Evidence. In the lab studies, laptop users watched a single short talk with no access to messages or other apps, whereas interviews with secondary students describe constant distraction from notifications on laptops. Weaken fairly. However, this is an argument about distraction, not about typing: a laptop with notifications switched off removes most of it, and the interview evidence describes students' experiences rather than measuring learning. Restate. The counter-argument therefore refines rather than overturns the claim: the note-taking medium matters less than how the device is used and whether students review their notes.

    Notice that the paragraph takes the counter-argument seriously before answering it. A counter-argument that is easy to knock down earns little in Criterion D.

    Check yourself

    Five quick questions

    What does Criterion C mean by a line of argument?

    A logical progression of ideas that starts from your question, where each point builds on the last and leads to a supported conclusion.

    Why group studies by strand rather than one study per paragraph?

    Strands make each paragraph do a job in the argument. One study per paragraph tends to become a list of summaries.

    Your counter-argument makes you change your main claim. Is that a problem?

    No. Refining your claim in response to evidence shows you recognise complexity, which the guide rewards.

    What is a moderating variable, in an EE argument?

    A condition the effect depends on, such as whether students review their notes. It often explains why studies disagree.

    What does a reverse outline check?

    That every paragraph links to the question, does one job, and builds on the one before, so the conclusion follows.
