    What is a psychological treatment?

    In Chapter 8 you studied a biological treatment for major depressive disorder (MDD): antidepressant medication, which aims to change brain chemistry. A psychological treatment aims to reduce symptoms by changing how a person thinks, behaves or relates to others, usually through sessions with a trained therapist. The World Health Organization lists behavioural activation, cognitive behavioural therapy, interpersonal psychotherapy and problem-solving therapy as effective psychological treatments for depression. This chapter focuses on cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), one of the most researched, which grows out of the cognitive model you studied in Chapter 2.

    From Beck's model to CBT

    Aaron Beck's cognitive model proposes that depression is maintained by negative thinking. Remember the three parts:

    • The negative cognitive triad: negative views of the self ("I am a failure"), the world ("nobody cares") and the future ("it will never get better").
    • Negative schemas: deep beliefs, often formed through earlier experience, that act as filters. When a stressful event activates them, information that fits the schema is noticed and information that does not fit is ignored.
    • Negative automatic thoughts and cognitive distortions: quick thoughts that pop up in a situation and feel true, such as overgeneralisation ("I failed one quiz, so I fail at everything") or catastrophising ("this means my future is ruined").

    The logic of CBT follows from this. If distorted thinking keeps low mood going, then teaching a person to notice and test that thinking should reduce the low mood. CBT adds a behavioural part, because depression also changes what people do. People who feel low often withdraw from friends and routines, so they have fewer rewarding experiences, mood drops further, and the negative thoughts seem confirmed.

    CBT is collaborative (client and therapist work as a team), structured, focused on the present and time-limited (usually weekly sessions over a few months), with homework between sessions.

    Technique 1: identifying and challenging automatic thoughts

    The first step is to catch automatic thoughts. A therapist may ask the client to keep a thought record: each time their mood drops, they write down the situation, the thought, the feeling and how strongly they believe the thought.

    The second step is to test the thought. The therapist does not simply say "that is not true". They ask questions that help the client examine it, a method called Socratic questioning: What is the evidence for and against this thought? Which distortion might this be? What would you say to a friend who thought this?

    The third step is to replace the thought with a more balanced one that fits the evidence. "I am useless at science" might become "I did badly on this quiz because I revised the wrong topic. My last two tests were fine." This is called cognitive restructuring. Over time, the aim is to weaken the negative schema itself, not only individual thoughts. A client may also try a behavioural experiment, a small real-life test of a prediction. If the thought is "if I message my friend, she will ignore me", the client sends the message and records what happens.

    Technique 2: behavioural activation

    Behavioural activation targets withdrawal. People with depression often wait until they feel like doing things, but the motivation does not come. Behavioural activation reverses the order: do the activity first, and mood may follow. The client and therapist plan small, specific activities into the week, a method called activity scheduling. The client rates each activity for mastery (a sense of achievement) and pleasure. A walk with a cousin after Maghrib prayer or helping a parent cook can count.

    Behavioural activation links back to Beck's model. Each completed activity is evidence against thoughts such as "I cannot do anything" or "nothing is enjoyable any more". In this way, changing behaviour helps to change thinking. Behavioural activation is also used on its own as a treatment, and research suggests it works about as well as full CBT.

    How is effectiveness measured?

    Saying that a treatment "works" is a claim about measurement.

    • Randomised controlled trials (RCTs). Participants are randomly allocated to the treatment or to a control condition. Common controls are a waiting list, care as usual, a pill placebo or another treatment.
    • Symptom scales. Depression is measured with rating scales. Some are scored by a clinician in an interview, such as the Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression. Others are self-report questionnaires, such as the Beck Depression Inventory.
    • Response and remission. Response usually means a large drop in symptom scores, often at least half. Remission means the score has fallen so low that the person has few or no symptoms left.
    • Relapse. Relapse means symptoms return after a person has improved.
    • Effect size and meta-analysis. A meta-analysis combines the results of many trials. It reports an effect size, such as Hedges' g, which shows how large the difference between groups is. As a rough guide, 0.2 is small, 0.5 is medium and 0.8 is large.

    Psychological treatment trials have a special measurement problem. In a drug trial, neither the patient nor the doctor needs to know whether the tablet is real or a placebo (a double-blind design). In a CBT trial, the client and the therapist always know which treatment is being given. The best trials make sure the person scoring symptoms does not know the client's group, but the client's expectations cannot be hidden.

    Key study 1: DeRubeis et al. (2005), cognitive therapy versus medication

    AimTo compare cognitive therapy (the form of CBT developed by Beck) with antidepressant medication for moderate to severe depression, in a trial that also included a pill placebo group.
    Participants240 outpatients aged 18 to 70 with moderate to severe MDD, at research clinics at the University of Pennsylvania and Vanderbilt University in the USA.
    ProcedureAn RCT. Participants were randomly allocated to 16 weeks of medication (120 people), 16 weeks of individual cognitive therapy (60 people) or 8 weeks of a pill placebo (60 people). The medication was paroxetine, an SSRI, with a second medication added for some patients who did not improve. Symptoms were measured with the Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression, which was used to decide who had responded and who had reached remission.
    FindingsAt 8 weeks, response rates were 50% for medication, 43% for cognitive therapy and 25% for placebo. Response rates for both treatments were significantly better than placebo, although on the continuous symptom scores only the medication advantage was significant at that point. At 16 weeks, 58% responded in both treatment groups, and remission rates were 46% for medication and 40% for cognitive therapy. Results differed by site: at Vanderbilt, medication did better than cognitive therapy, and the authors linked this to differences in the patients and in how experienced the cognitive therapists were.
    ConclusionCognitive therapy can be as effective as medication for the first treatment of moderate to severe depression, but this may depend on a high level of therapist experience.
    What happened nextHollon et al. (2005) followed the 104 patients who had responded. Cognitive therapy patients stopped treatment (up to three booster sessions were allowed). Medication responders were randomly allocated to keep taking medication or to be switched, under medical supervision, to a placebo. Over the next 12 months, 31% of the cognitive therapy group relapsed, compared with 76% of those switched to placebo and 47% of those who kept taking medication. The difference between cognitive therapy and continued medication was not statistically significant. The authors concluded that cognitive therapy has a lasting effect.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example for cognitive models); Paper 1 Section B; Paper 1 Section C on measurement, causality or perspective; Paper 2 Section B practice (an RCT).
    Evaluate it withMeasurement: a clinician-rated scale with set cut-offs for response and remission gives clear, comparable outcomes, but a 25% placebo response shows that improvement is not only caused by the specific treatment. Causality: random allocation and a placebo group support a causal claim, but clients in cognitive therapy could not be blind to their treatment. Perspective: the site difference suggests the "treatment" includes the therapist's skill, so findings from expert research clinics may not generalise to everyday services.

    Key study 2: Cuijpers et al. (2023), a meta-analysis of 409 trials

    AimTo combine all available randomised trials of CBT for depression and compare CBT with control conditions, other psychotherapies, medication and combined treatment.
    Participants409 randomised trials with 52,702 patients in total, including adults and, in some trials, children and adolescents.
    ProcedureA meta-analysis. The researchers collected RCTs of CBT for depression, rated each for risk of bias, and calculated pooled effect sizes (Hedges' g) for each comparison. They checked for publication bias and repeated the analyses using only the best quality trials.
    FindingsCompared with control conditions such as a waiting list or care as usual, CBT had a large effect (g = 0.79). The effect was smaller, though still meaningful, in trials with a low risk of bias. Fewer than half of the patients who received CBT were classed as responders. CBT was only slightly more effective than other psychotherapies (g = 0.06). Compared with antidepressant medication, there was no significant difference at the end of treatment. At 6 to 12 months, CBT had a small advantage (g = 0.34), but this was based on few trials and was not found in every sensitivity analysis. Combining CBT with medication was more effective than medication alone, but not more effective than CBT alone. Unguided self-help CBT had a smaller effect (g = 0.45).
    ConclusionCBT is an effective treatment for depression, with short-term effects similar to medication and possibly longer lasting benefits. However, its effects are smaller in the best quality trials, and many patients do not respond.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example for cognitive models); Paper 1 Section B; Paper 1 Section C on measurement, bias or perspective.
    Evaluate it withMeasurement: combining 409 trials gives a precise estimate, but trials used different scales and control groups, so the results varied a lot between studies. Bias: lower quality trials and publication bias can make effects look larger, which is why the authors checked the low risk of bias trials separately. Perspective: an average effect across 52,702 people does not tell any one person whether CBT will work for them.

    CBT or medication?

    Chapter 8 showed that SSRIs are thought to work by increasing the availability of serotonin, and that their advantage over placebo is smallest at the less severe end of the range studied, and close to zero for moderate depression (Kirsch et al., 2008). How does CBT compare?

    FeatureCBTAntidepressant medication
    How it is thought to workChanges negative automatic thoughts, schemas and withdrawal (cognitive approach)Changes neurotransmitter activity, for example serotonin (biological approach)
    Short-term effectSimilar to medication (DeRubeis et al., 2005; Cuijpers et al., 2023)Similar to CBT
    After treatment endsLower relapse in some studies (Hollon et al., 2005)Higher relapse when medication is stopped
    What it asks of the personAttending sessions, practising skills, completing homeworkTaking medication regularly; possible side effects
    AccessNeeds a trained therapist; may be costly or have waiting listsNeeds a doctor to prescribe and monitor

    This is not a contest with one winner. The best option depends on the person, the severity of their depression, their preferences and what is available. Decisions about medication are always made with a doctor, and stopping medication should only ever happen with medical advice.

    Relapse: does the effect last?

    Depression often returns, so relapse prevention is one of the strongest arguments for CBT. In Hollon et al. (2005), people who recovered with cognitive therapy relapsed much less than people switched to placebo, and about as often as people who stayed on medication. One explanation is that CBT teaches skills that the person keeps after therapy ends, while medication helps for as long as it is taken.

    The picture is not completely clear. Cuijpers et al. (2023) found a small advantage for CBT over medication at 6 to 12 months, but it rested on few trials. Over longer follow-ups, the advantage of CBT over control groups became smaller.

    Access and cultural adaptation

    A treatment can only help people who can get it. The World Health Organization reports that even in high-income countries, only about one third of people with depression receive mental health treatment. It names a lack of investment, a lack of trained providers and social stigma as barriers.

    CBT was developed in the USA, so researchers have asked whether it works in other cultures. Cultural adaptation means changing how a therapy is delivered so that it fits a group's language, values and context, while keeping its core techniques. A systematic review by Chowdhary et al. (2014) found that most adaptations changed the language, the examples used and who delivered the treatment, and that culturally adapted psychological treatments were effective for depression in populations other than those they were first developed for.

    In practice, adaptation might mean working in the client's first language, using examples from their family life, involving family when the client wishes, and respecting religious beliefs as a source of support. In a country as diverse as the UAE, adaptation is about the individual client, not assumptions about their nationality.

    Tele-therapy

    Tele-therapy means delivering therapy at a distance, by video call, telephone or an internet programme. It can reduce travel, waiting times and the stigma of visiting a clinic. A meta-analysis by Carlbring et al. (2018) compared guided internet-delivered CBT (an online programme with support from a therapist) directly with face-to-face CBT in 20 studies with 1,418 participants, across several psychiatric and physical health conditions. The two produced equivalent overall effects. Support matters: unguided self-help CBT had a smaller effect in Cuijpers et al. (2023). Tele-therapy also raises questions about privacy and about helping someone at a distance if they become very unwell.

    How useful is CBT as a treatment for depression?

    Strengths. CBT is supported by a very large evidence base, including hundreds of RCTs. Its short-term effects are similar to medication, and some evidence suggests lower relapse after treatment ends. It has no physical side effects, teaches skills people can keep, and can be adapted for different cultures and delivered online. Because it applies Beck's model, theory explains why the techniques should work.

    Limitations. Many people do not respond to CBT, and effects are smaller in the best quality trials. Trials cannot be fully blind, so expectations may explain part of the effect. Results may depend on the therapist's skill, as the site difference in DeRubeis et al. (2005) suggests. CBT asks a lot of the client, such as concentration and homework. Finally, the fact that CBT works does not prove that negative thinking caused the depression. Thinking might be a symptom that improves when the depression lifts, rather than its cause.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to psychological treatment of depression
    MeasurementEffectiveness depends on which scale is used, who scores it, what counts as response or remission, and how long people are followed up.
    CausalityRCTs can show that CBT causes improvement, but not that negative thinking caused the depression in the first place.
    BiasClients and therapists cannot be blind, lower quality trials show larger effects, and publication bias can inflate results.
    ChangeCBT aims for lasting change in thinking and behaviour, and relapse studies test whether that change lasts after treatment ends.
    PerspectiveCognitive and biological treatments both reduce symptoms, and patients, therapists and health services may judge "effective" differently.
    ResponsibilityTherapists must adapt treatment respectfully to each client, protect privacy in tele-therapy, and never pressure anyone to stop medication without medical advice.
