    Start here

    What Paper 1 is testing

    Paper 1 checks whether you can take what you know about the biological, cognitive and sociocultural approaches and use it: first to explain a term, then to make sense of a situation you have never seen, and finally to think critically through one of the six concepts.

    Here's the thing. Each section asks for something different, so the same paragraph will not work in all three. Section A wants a clear explanation with an example. Section B wants you to apply that explanation to a new scenario. Section C wants an argument. Learn the difference and you stop losing marks for answering the wrong kind of question.

    Start here

    What you need to be able to do

    Section A gives you a psychological term from one of the three approaches and asks you to describe or explain it with one example. It is the shortest section, but it is where careless marks disappear fastest.

    By the end of this section, you should be able to:

    • Explain any term from the guide's content tables accurately, in your own words, in two or three sentences.
    • Choose one example (a study or a realistic, hypothetical situation) that genuinely shows the term at work.
    • Write the sentence that says why the example demonstrates the term. That sentence is the difference between 3 and 4 marks.
    How this shows up on the exam

    Each question carries an approach label, such as "Cognitive approach to understanding human behaviour", followed by a stem like "Explain anchoring bias with reference to one example of human behaviour. [4]" No context is named, because the term could have been taught in any of them.

    Not assessed

    Details of research studies. You can use a study as your example, but you do not get marks for its procedure, sample size or date. A realistic, hypothetical example is equally acceptable if it clearly shows the term.

    The markband

    How Section A is marked

    There are only two bands, so the jump from 2 to 3 is the one that matters.

    MarksWhat your answer looks like
    0Nothing relevant to the question.
    1 to 2Limited knowledge. The example is relevant but not explained. This is where you land if you define the term with no example, or name an example without connecting it to the term.
    3The term is correct and the example is described in detail, but you never say why the example demonstrates the term.
    4The term is explained accurately, the example is detailed, and you state explicitly how the example shows the term working.
    One example only

    If the question asks for one example and you give two, only the first is credited. Pick your best example and develop it instead of listing several.

    How to answer

    The TAG method

    Use these three moves, in this order, every time.

    T

    Term

    State the term precisely in your first sentence. Do not ease into it with a story or a general opener like "In psychology, many things affect behaviour".

    A

    Anchor

    Name your example straight after the term: a study or a realistic situation.

    G

    Glue

    Write the sentence that connects them: "This demonstrates [term] because [how it works here]." This sentence is the exact difference between a 3 and a 4.

    See the difference

    Weak

    Schema theory is when people use prior knowledge to understand things. Bartlett's War of the Ghosts study is an example.

    Strong

    Schema theory proposes that prior knowledge is organised into mental frameworks that shape and can distort recall. Bartlett's War of the Ghosts study demonstrates this because participants recalling an unfamiliar story changed unfamiliar details to fit story structures familiar from their own culture, showing that memory is reconstructive rather than an exact copy.

    The weak version names an example. The strong version explains the term and says what in the example shows it.
    Worked example

    "Explain anchoring bias with reference to one example of human behaviour." [4]

    This is the cognitive approach question from the specimen paper. Notice that the command term is explain, so you need a reason or cause, not just a definition.

    MoveWhat it sounds like
    TermAnchoring bias is a cognitive bias in which people rely too heavily on the first piece of information they receive (the anchor) when making a judgement, and then adjust away from it too little.
    AnchorFor example, a shopper in a Dubai mall sees a jacket labelled "Was AED 600, now AED 299".
    GlueThis demonstrates anchoring bias because the first number, AED 600, becomes the reference point for judging value. Using fast, effortless System 1 thinking, and with little time or information to compare prices elsewhere, the shopper judges AED 299 against the anchor instead of against what similar jackets usually cost, so it feels like a bargain even if it is the normal price.
    Why this reaches 4

    The term is accurate, the example is specific, and the glue gives the cause (System 1 thinking, limited information) and shows it operating in the example. A hypothetical example like this is fully acceptable.

    Describe or explain?

    Describe

    A detailed account of the key features. For "Describe how one chemical messenger plays a role in one human behaviour", say what the messenger does and how that shows up in the behaviour, step by step.

    Explain

    A detailed account including reasons or causes. Everything in describe, plus why it happens. If your answer has no "because", it is not yet an explanation.

    Check yourself

    Can you...

    A classmate defines conformity perfectly and stops. What mark range are they in, and what is missing?

    1 to 2 marks. A definition with no example cannot leave the bottom band. They need one example and a sentence explaining how it shows conformity.

    Does a Section A example have to be a published study?

    No. A realistic, hypothetical example is equally acceptable, as long as it clearly shows the term and you explain how.

    Why will a Section A question never say "in the context of health and well-being"?

    Because content can be taught in any context. Section A tests the term itself, so you can take your example from wherever you know it best.

    What single sentence most often separates a 3 from a 4?

    The glue sentence: "This demonstrates [term] because..." It shows the examiner why the example fits, instead of leaving them to work it out.

    Start here

    What you need to be able to do

    Section B hands you a short scenario or study you have never seen, set in one of the four contexts, and asks you to use a theory, model or term to explain it. The marks are for application: how well you make the theory explain this situation.

    By the end of this section, you should be able to:

    • Spot the detail in a scenario that signals a particular theory or term.
    • Explain, step by step, how that theory produces the behaviour described.
    • Keep every paragraph tied to the people and details in the scenario.
    How this shows up on the exam

    Each question is labelled with a context, for example "Context: Human development". The specimen paper shows two common shapes: "With reference to this study, explain the value of cognitive models for understanding cognitive processes", and "Explain how social learning theory could be used to achieve this goal", where you suggest a strategy for a school.

    The markband

    How Section B is marked

    MarksWhat your answer looks like
    1 to 2Limited knowledge, and only a thin link to the scenario. A correct definition with no application stays here, however accurate it is.
    3 to 4Knowledge has some detail and is mostly accurate. You connect the theory to the scenario, but the link is generic: it could fit several different scenarios, not just this one.
    5 to 6Accurate, detailed knowledge, and the application is well developed. The mechanism of the theory is shown working through the details of the scenario.
    The difference from Section A

    Section A rewards an accurate explanation and a relevant example. Section B rewards reading the scenario as evidence and showing exactly how the theory produces the behaviour described. Explanation is only there to support the application.

    How to answer

    The CLUE method

    C

    Call out the concept

    Name the theory or term in your very first sentence, with a brief, accurate explanation.

    L

    Locate the evidence

    Point to the exact detail in the scenario that signals it. Paraphrase it briefly. Do not copy the scenario out.

    U

    Unpack the mechanism

    Explain, step by step, how and why that detail produces the behaviour, using accurate terminology.

    E

    Echo back to the scenario

    Close by tying the mechanism back to the named people or setting. Connect it; do not just retell the story.

    The cover test

    Cover the scenario with your hand. Does your answer still make complete sense without it? If yes, you have written a Section A-style definition, not a Section B application. Go back and name the specific details you are explaining.

    Worked example

    An unseen scenario, step by step

    Context: Learning and cognition

    A secondary school in Sharjah is concerned that many Grade 9 students submit homework late. The head of year introduces a new system: every week that a class submits at least 90% of its homework on time, the whole class earns 15 minutes of free reading or board games on Thursday afternoon. After six weeks, late submissions have fallen sharply. During the exam period the reward is paused, and late submissions begin to rise again.

    Explain how operant conditioning could explain the change in the students' behaviour. [6]

    Try planning it yourself first, then reveal the plan.

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    MovePlan
    COperant conditioning is learning in which the consequence of a behaviour changes how likely it is to be repeated. A pleasant consequence that strengthens a behaviour is positive reinforcement.
    LThe free time on Thursday only happens if the class submits at least 90% of homework on time. Late submissions fell over six weeks, then rose when the reward was paused.
    UThe free time is a positive reinforcer that depends on the behaviour, so students learn that on-time submission leads to a reward and the behaviour becomes more frequent. When the reinforcement stops, the behaviour no longer produces the consequence and begins to weaken (extinction). Because the reward comes days after the homework is handed in, the link is weaker than with immediate reinforcement, which could explain why some students did not change.
    ESo the Grade 9 classes submitted homework on time more often because it reliably earned free time, and slipped again in the exam period because the reinforcement was removed, which is what operant conditioning predicts.
    What lifts this to 5 or 6

    Two mechanisms (reinforcement and extinction) each matched to a specific detail, accurate terms, and one point that shows depth: the delay before the reward. Every sentence would collapse if you covered the scenario, which is exactly what you want.

    Question shapes

    Two shapes to be ready for

    1. "With reference to this study, explain..."

    You get a study and a model or theory. Use the study as evidence: say which parts of the model it supports, and which parts it does not actually test.

    In the specimen paper's imagination study, children were seen selecting and combining toy parts, which supports the stages of the model. But the model says the combining is random, and the study never tests that. Spotting what a study does not show is application too.

    2. "Explain how [theory] could be used to..."

    You are asked to suggest a strategy, often for a school or community. Choose one realistic strategy and link each part of it to a specific part of the theory.

    For social learning theory, a peer-mentor programme works well because you can link each step to attention, retention, reproduction and motivation, and to identification with a model students admire.

    Learning and cognition (6 scenarios)
    Scenario 1Classical Conditioning

    Mia, a university student, had a stressful experience during her very first classroom presentation. She lost her place mid-way, noticed classmates exchanging glances, and felt deeply embarrassed. Over the following weeks, she noticed something unexpected: whenever the professor announced that presentations would take place the following day, her heart would begin racing and her palms would sweat, even before she had started preparing.

    With reference to the scenario above, explain the role of classical conditioning in Mia's anxiety response. [6]

    Scenario 2Anchoring Bias

    Kofi is a Grade 11 student looking for a laptop for schoolwork. He visits an electronics store where the salesperson first presents a high-end model priced at AED 11,000. The salesperson then shows Kofi a mid-range model at AED 6,000, describing it as "excellent value." Kofi had originally planned to spend no more than AED 3,000, but he ended up purchasing the AED 6,000 laptop, telling himself it was a reasonable deal compared to the first one he had seen.

    With reference to the scenario above, explain how anchoring bias may have influenced Kofi's decision. [6]

    Scenario 3Schema Theory

    During final examination week, a group of students briefly witnesses an argument between two classmates in a school hallway. Weeks later, when asked to describe what happened, several students reported details that were not part of the actual event (including who "started it" and the tone of the exchange) that closely align with how arguments between classmates typically unfold in their experience.

    With reference to the scenario above, explain how schema theory may account for the students' inaccurate recall of the event. [6]

    Scenario 8Operant Conditioning

    Khalid used to submit his homework late on a regular basis. His teacher began publicly praising him in front of the class on the rare occasions he submitted on time, and stopped commenting at all when he submitted late. Within a few weeks, Khalid was submitting his homework on time far more consistently, and told a friend he “just wanted to hear his name again.”

    With reference to the scenario above, explain the role of operant conditioning in the change in Khalid’s homework submission behaviour. [6]

    Scenario 9Dual Processing Theory

    Noor was scrolling through a food delivery app and saw a meal advertised as “90% fat-free.” She added it to her cart immediately, feeling reassured about her choice. It was only later, when a friend pointed out that the same meal was also “high in sugar and sodium,” that Noor paused and reconsidered, realising that the framing of the advertisement had shaped her initial, fast decision rather than a careful comparison of nutritional value.

    With reference to the scenario above, explain the role of dual processing theory in Noor’s decision-making. [6]

    Scenario 10Social Learning Theory

    Omar’s older brother regularly volunteers at a local community kitchen on weekends and speaks proudly about it at family gatherings, often receiving warm praise from relatives. Omar, who had never previously expressed interest in volunteering, began asking to join his brother, and within a few months started volunteering independently at the same kitchen, saying he wanted to “be looked at the way people look at” his brother.

    With reference to the scenario above, explain the role of social learning theory in Omar’s adoption of volunteering behaviour. [6]

    Start here

    What Section C is actually asking

    Section C gives you two questions, each from a different context, and you answer one. Each question is built from three fixed ingredients: a context, an area of study from that context, and one of the six concepts. The command term will usually be evaluate, discuss or to what extent.

    Context

    Health and well-being, human development, human relationships, or learning and cognition.

    Area of study

    A topic from that context's table in the guide, such as attachment, conformity or the dual processing model.

    Concept

    Bias, causality, change, measurement, perspective or responsibility.

    Two specimen-style examples show how the ingredients combine:

    • "In the context of human development, evaluate one or more strategies used by developmental psychologists to measure theory of mind." Measurement
    • "One claim in the psychology of health and well-being is that a single perspective is not enough to explain mental health. Discuss this claim with reference to one biological explanation of one or more disorders." Perspective
    Why you cannot pre-write this essay

    Four contexts, each with around ten areas of study, six concepts and three command terms give you hundreds of possible questions. A memorised essay will not fit the exact sentence printed on the paper. The guide is also clear that knowledge of specific study details is not assessed here. What is assessed is whether you can make a concept do real analytical work on content you know well.

    Command terms

    Evaluate, discuss, to what extent

    Evaluate

    Weigh strengths against limitations. You need at least one real strength, at least one real limitation, and an explicit act of weighing them to reach a judgement. Two lists are not an evaluation.

    Discuss

    A balanced review of a range of arguments or factors, ending in a clear conclusion that the rest of the essay supports.

    To what extent

    Test a claim. The answer is not yes or no but a position on a scale (largely, partly, minimally), stated early so the examiner knows where you are heading.

    The shared trap

    All three fail in the same way: the content is described accurately but never weighed or judged. The jump from 7 to 9 up to 10 to 12 is not more content. It is the same content, actually weighed, leading to a conclusion that matches your argument.

    The six concepts

    Use the concept as a lens, not a label

    The same concept looks different depending on the area of study, but the questions you ask with it stay the same. Use the trigger questions to get your thinking started.

    ConceptCore ideaTrigger questionsA shallow use to avoid
    BiasBehaviour, theories and research are shaped by systematic distortions in who is studied, how findings are interpreted and which explanation is favoured.Who produced this finding, and what might have skewed it? Whose perspective is missing? Is the theory one-sided?Naming "sampling bias" without saying how it narrows the conclusion.
    CausalityBehaviour rarely has one cause. Much research is correlational, and correlations are often mistaken for causes.Is this cause and effect, or an association? Could the direction be reversed? Could a third variable explain both?"Correlation is not causation" as a slogan, without applying it.
    ChangeBehaviour changes gradually or suddenly, by choice or by forces outside our control. Free will and determinism sit here.Gradual or sudden? Voluntary or determined? Over what timeframe was it measured, and does that support the claim?"Behaviour can change" without saying what changes, over what period, or what shows it.
    MeasurementBehaviour is hard to observe and quantify. Researchers must operationalise it, which raises questions of reliability and validity.How exactly is this captured? What does the method let you see, and what does it miss? Direct or indirect?"It's hard to measure feelings" without naming a method and its limits.
    PerspectiveNo single approach explains behaviour on its own. Each rests on its own assumptions about what counts as a cause.What does this approach assume? What would another approach say? Whose cultural or theoretical viewpoint shapes this?Listing two approaches side by side without showing where they disagree or fill each other's gaps.
    ResponsibilityPsychologists hold power over participants and over how findings are used in public.Who could be harmed, and whose duty is it to prevent that? Where does an ethical safeguard trade off against research quality?Reciting "consent, withdrawal, debrief" without linking it to the area of study.
    Two things that surprise students

    The same idea can serve more than one concept: reductionism can be an argument about bias or about causality, depending on how you use it. And you are not limited to the related terms listed in the guide. Any link is accepted if it is relevant, explained and justified.

    How to plan

    The 8-step thinking and planning protocol

    Spend five to seven minutes on steps 1 to 6 before you write a sentence. It feels slow. It is what stops essays stalling in the middle bands.

    1. Unpack the question. Underline the context, the area of study and the concept separately. They are three different jobs.
    2. Recall the content on its own. Before bringing in the concept, list what you actually know about the area of study.
    3. Apply the concept's trigger questions. Aim for two or three points where the concept genuinely changes how you read the content.
    4. Ask "so what?" of each point. What does it mean for how convincing, complete or useful the explanation is? Skipping this step is the most common reason essays stall at 7 to 9.
    5. Decide your position. Having weighed your points, where do you land?
    6. Write your thesis statement. State your position in your opening sentence.
    7. Build and order your paragraphs. One point and its "so what" per paragraph. Put your strongest point last, just before the conclusion.
    8. Write a conclusion that matches your thesis. Restate your position. Leave time for it: an essay that stops mid-paragraph cannot reach the top band.
    Opening sentence

    Thesis templates

    A strong first sentence shows you understood the area of study, uses the concept as a lens, and tells the examiner where you are heading. Every template has a position word (strong, limited, largely, partly). That word is what signals evaluation.

    For "discuss"

    A. While area of study can be explained through the lens of concept, this explanation is strong / limited because reason, and is best understood alongside counterpoint.
    B. Area of study illustrates concept in several ways; weighing these together suggests that position.

    For "evaluate" and "to what extent"

    C. Although area of study demonstrates concept in way, its strength lies in X, while it is undermined by Y, leading to the conclusion that judgement.
    D. To a large / moderate / limited extent, concept can explain area of study, because main reason, although counter-consideration limits this conclusion.

    For "One claim is that..."

    E. This claim holds strongly / largely / partly / weakly when applied to area of study, since supporting reason, although limiting reason qualifies this position.
    Inside each paragraph

    The five moves of a strong paragraph

    1. Define the concept in your own words, as it applies here.
    2. Use a specific detail from the area of study: a finding, a method, a theory's claim.
    3. Explain why it matters for the concept. Ask yourself one more question. This is where the analysis happens.
    4. Give a limitation or counterpoint.
    5. Link back to the question and your position.
    Reading your feedback

    If you see these letters in the margin: C means the concept is missing or only named; S means a specific detail is missing; W means "why does this matter?" is missing; L means the link to the question is missing. A tick means the move is there.

    Weak and strong, concept by concept

    Both versions mention the concept. Only one does any work with it.

    Bias: weak

    There could be sampling bias in this research.

    Bias: strong

    Because the sample was drawn entirely from urban, English-speaking adults, any claim that this explanation is universal is limited by bias; it may describe one cultural group's experience rather than a human pattern.

    Causality: weak

    This is correlation, not causation.

    Causality: strong

    The relationship could run in either direction, or both variables could be products of a third factor such as family income, so treating one as the cause of the other overstates what a correlational design can show.

    Change: weak

    Behaviour can change over time.

    Change: strong

    Because the change was measured only once, immediately after the intervention, it is unclear whether it is a lasting shift or a short-lived response to being studied, which limits any claim about long-term change.

    Measurement: weak

    It's hard to measure depression.

    Measurement: strong

    A self-report questionnaire captures how a person describes their state at one moment; it cannot observe the underlying biological or cognitive processes, so it is only ever a proxy for the construct it claims to measure.

    Perspective: weak

    There are different perspectives in psychology.

    Perspective: strong

    A cognitive account explains the behaviour as a product of distorted thinking, while a sociocultural account frames it as a response to social expectations; they do not contradict each other, but each points to a different intervention.

    Responsibility: weak

    The researchers should have got consent.

    Responsibility: strong

    Revealing the full aim in advance would have undermined the results, so the researchers faced a real trade-off between informed consent and the validity of the design, one resolved only by a thorough debrief afterwards.

    The markband

    The markbands in plain language

    MarksWhat it means for your essay
    1 to 3You wrote about the topic, but the concept barely shows up and there is no real argument.
    4 to 6You described the content correctly and gestured at the concept, but did not yet do anything with it. Mostly description, with a simple conclusion.
    7 to 9You explained how the concept connects to the content, but did not push the "so what" far enough, or your conclusion does not quite match what you argued.
    10 to 12You weighed the concept against the content and reached a conclusion that follows from your own points. Terms mostly used well.
    13 to 15Everything in 10 to 12, sustained across the whole essay, with your points working on each other rather than sitting side by side. Precise terminology and a clearly stated, reasoned conclusion.
    The boundary most students get stuck on

    From 7 to 9 up to 10 to 12, the links between concepts go from "explained, and partly relevant" to "included and explained", and the conclusion goes from "not always consistent" to "consistent with the arguments presented". Step 4 of the plan, asking "so what?" of every point, is what closes that gap.

    Worked examples

    The plan in action: one question per context

    Each example runs the 8-step protocol on a real-style question. They stop short of a finished essay on purpose: a model essay gets memorised and recycled, which is exactly what Section C punishes. This is the thinking behind a strong answer, not the answer itself.

    Health and Well-being: Bias

    In the context of health and well-being, evaluate the role of bias in one or more biological explanations of a mental health disorder. [15]

    Area of study: biological explanations. Concept: bias. Command term: evaluate.

    1. UnpackContext: health and well-being. Area of study: biological explanations of a mental health disorder. Concept: bias. Command term: evaluate, so the goal is an appraisal that weighs strengths against limitations.
    2. Recall the contentBiological explanations might include genetic inheritance, neurotransmitter explanations, brain imaging findings, and the diathesis-stress model.
    3. Apply the trigger questionsWho produced this finding, and what might skew it? Most biological research draws on diagnosed clinical populations, often in higher-income, Western settings, so findings may be culturally and diagnostically narrow. Is the theory itself one-sided? A purely biological account risks reductionism, treating a mental health disorder as solely a brain disorder and sidelining environmental or social contributors.
    4. So what?The cultural and sampling narrowness limits how confidently the explanation generalizes beyond the population studied. The reductionist framing may strengthen the case for medication, but at the cost of underselling non-biological causes and treatments, which has real consequences for how the disorder is understood and treated.
    5. Decide a positionA balanced appraisal: real strength in measurable, replicable physiological evidence, but limited by a tendency toward reductionism and reliance on culturally narrow samples.
    6. Thesis"Although biological explanations of a mental health disorder rest on measurable, replicable physiological evidence, this strength is undermined by a tendency toward reductionism and a reliance on culturally narrow samples, leading to the conclusion that biological accounts are a necessary but incomplete part of explaining the disorder."
    7. Paragraph planP1: the strength (specific physiological evidence). P2: limitation one (reductionism, sidelining social and environmental causes). P3: limitation two (sampling and cultural bias in the research base). Conclusion: restate the qualified position.
    Band differentiatorA 7 to 9 response states these points but only loosely connects “bias” to them. A 10 to 12 response explains the bias clearly for each point. A 13 to 15 response goes further and shows the two limitations interacting, for example, noting that the reductionist tradition itself shaped which biological factors got studied in the first place, producing one integrated argument rather than two separate lists.
    Human Development: Change

    In the context of human development, discuss how stage theories and continuous models differ in their explanation of change across the lifespan. [15]

    Area of study: stage theories and continuous models. Concept: change. Command term: discuss.

    1. UnpackContext: human development. Area of study: stage theories and continuous models. Concept: change. Command term: discuss, so the goal is a balanced review across a range of arguments, ending in a clear, supported conclusion.
    2. Recall the contentStage theories propose qualitatively distinct, sequential periods of development. Continuous models propose gradual, incremental, cumulative development without sharp breaks between periods.
    3. Apply the trigger questionsIs the change gradual or sudden? Stage theories assume sudden, qualitative shifts between stages. Continuous models assume a gradual, quantitative increase. How would we know change had happened? Stage theories predict universal, age-linked timing; continuous models allow more individual variation in rate.
    4. So what?If development really is staged, then interventions and teaching should be timed to specific developmental windows. If development is continuous, that logic does not hold as strongly, and gradual support across ages may matter more than hitting one particular stage-linked moment. This is a real, practical consequence of which model is closer to correct.
    5. Decide a positionBoth models capture something real, but evidence that supposedly later abilities can appear earlier under simplified testing conditions favours a more continuous account over a strictly staged one.
    6. Thesis"Stage theories and continuous models offer two different accounts of how change occurs across the lifespan, one as a series of qualitatively distinct shifts and the other as gradual accumulation; weighing the evidence for each suggests that development reflects elements of both, with stronger support for a continuous pattern than the strict stage view originally proposed."
    7. Paragraph planP1: Explain the stage account of change. P2: explain the continuous account of change. P3: weigh both against evidence that challenges strict staging. Conclusion: restate the balanced position.
    Band differentiatorA 7 to 9 response describes both models accurately but does not clearly contrast how each one treats change differently. A 10 to 12 response makes that contrast explicit and reaches a conclusion. A 13 to 15 response uses a specific, named piece of evidence (not just “studies show”) to justify why one position is favoured, while still crediting what the other model gets right.
    Human Relationships: Causality

    In the context of human relationships, to what extent can social identity theory be used to explain the causes of group behaviour? [15]

    Area of study: social identity theory. Concept: causality. Command term: to what extent.

    1. UnpackContext: human relationships. Area of study: social identity theory. Concept: causality. Command term: to what extent, so the goal is a defensible position on how much the claim holds, not a yes or no answer.
    2. Recall the contentSocial identity theory proposes that categorizing self and others into in-groups and out-groups, social comparison, and the drive for positive self-esteem through group membership produce behaviours such as in-group favouritism and discrimination.
    3. Apply the trigger questionsIs this cause and effect, or correlation? Experimental designs such as the minimal group paradigm manipulate categorization directly and observe the resulting behaviour, which supports a genuinely causal claim under controlled conditions. Could a third variable explain the real-world version? Naturalistic group conflict is also shaped by competition, history, and individual personality, so categorization alone may be one cause among several, not the whole story.
    4. So what?The experimental support strengthens the causal claim, but the artificiality of those experiments limits how confidently the same causal claim can be extended to real-world group behaviour, which carries historical and economic weight that a laboratory setting does not.
    5. Decide a positionTo a moderate extent, categorization is a genuine, experimentally demonstrated causal contributor, but it is rarely sufficient by itself outside the laboratory.
    6. Thesis"To a moderate extent, social identity theory identifies a genuine causal mechanism behind group behaviour, since experimental manipulation of group categorization reliably produces in-group favouritism, though this causal claim is limited by the difficulty of isolating categorization from other contributing factors, such as competition or historical context, in real-world settings."
    7. Paragraph planP1: evidence for the causal claim (experimental support). P2: the limitation of that claim (artificiality, multiple real-world causes). P3: weigh and state the extent. Conclusion: restate the moderate-extent position.
    Band differentiatorA 7 to 9 response might say “this is supported by research” without distinguishing experimental support from correlational support, which is the actual causality issue. A 10 to 12 response uses causality explicitly to test the strength of the claim. A 13 to 15 response explains precisely why experimental control strengthens the causal claim, while still placing a clear, reasoned boundary on how far it can be extended.
    Learning and Cognition: Perspective

    One claim in learning and cognition psychology is that schema theory reflects a particular perspective on how knowledge is organized. Discuss this claim with reference to the role of schema in behaviour and/or cognition. [15]

    Area of study: schema theory. Concept: perspective. Command term: discuss (claim-based architecture).

    1. UnpackContext: learning and cognition. Area of study: the role of schema. Concept: perspective. Command term: discuss. Note the architecture: the claim itself is already stated, so the job is to test how far it holds, not to introduce it from scratch.
    2. Recall the contentSchema theory proposes that prior knowledge is organized into mental frameworks that guide perception, memory, and behaviour, for example, Bartlett's work on reconstructive memory, or social schemas that guide inference about other people.
    3. Apply the trigger questionsWhat does this approach assume? Schema theory assumes a cognitive perspective: knowledge organization happens through internally built mental structures, and errors such as distorted recall result from schema-driven interpretation. What would a different approach say instead? A sociocultural perspective would emphasize that schema content is culturally transmitted, not purely individually constructed.
    4. So what?If schema theory is read narrowly as a purely individual-cognitive account, it undersells how much schema content is shaped by culture, which means the claim in question is broadly accurate: schema theory is one particular perspective, not a culture-neutral, all-encompassing account of knowledge organization.
    5. Decide a positionThe claim holds largely true. Schema theory is best read as a cognitive-perspective account, valuable for explaining structure and process, but more complete when read alongside a sociocultural account of where schema content actually comes from.
    6. Thesis"This claim holds largely true when applied to the role of schema in behaviour and cognition, since schema theory is fundamentally a cognitive account of how prior knowledge structures perception and memory, although this perspective is incomplete without acknowledging the sociocultural origins of schema content."
    7. Paragraph planP1: Show how schema theory reflects a cognitive perspective (mental structures, an example such as reconstructive memory). P2: show the limits of that perspective alone (where schema content actually originates). P3: weigh and state how far the claim holds. Conclusion: restate the position.
    Band differentiatorA 7 to 9 response explains schema theory accurately but does not connect it explicitly to “perspective” as a concept. A 10 to 12 response names the cognitive perspective and contrasts it with at least one alternative. A 13 to 15 response integrates the alternative perspective into one coherent argument about how complete the original claim is, rather than presenting the two perspectives as separate, unconnected halves.

    The "so what" is missing. The concept is connected to the content but not pushed further, or the conclusion does not match the argument. Ask what each point means for how convincing the explanation is.

    Why does every thesis template include a word like "largely" or "limited"?

    Because it commits you to a position. It tells the examiner from your first sentence that you are going to evaluate, not describe.

    Should you describe the procedure of a study in detail to show knowledge?

    No. Study details are not assessed in Section C. Use a study only for the finding or method that your concept says something about.
