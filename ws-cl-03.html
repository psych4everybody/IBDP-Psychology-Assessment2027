    What is a schema?

    Schema theory

    A schema (plural: schemas or schemata) is a mental framework of knowledge about something: an object, a place, an event, a group of people, or yourself. Schemas are built from past experience and stored in long-term memory. Your schema for a classroom includes desks, a board, a teacher, other students and the rule that you put your hand up to speak. You do not have to think about any of this.

    Schema theory is a cognitive model. It proposes that what we already know shapes how we take in, store, remember and act on new information. This is called top-down processing: knowledge already in memory works from the "top" to guide how we interpret what comes in through our senses. Schemas let us make sense of a situation in seconds, fill in missing details and predict what will happen next.

    The idea goes back to Bartlett (1932), whose research on memory you will meet in Chapter 8. In the 1970s, researchers used schema theory to explain how people understand and remember what they read.

    Schemas have three features that are worth learning:

    • They are organised. Knowledge is stored in linked clusters. Hearing the word "airport" makes related knowledge (passports, queues, gates, boarding passes) easier to reach.
    • They create expectations. A schema tells you what is likely to be there or to happen next, so you notice quickly when something is missing or out of place.
    • They fill gaps. When information is missing, a schema supplies the most likely details. This saves effort, but it can also produce errors.

    In the warm-up, the words were simple, but there was no schema to hang them on. Most people rate the passage as hard to understand and remember only a few disconnected pieces. Once the title is known, the right schema is activated and almost every sentence clicks into place.

    Types of schema

    Three types of schema are useful for this course.

    Scripts are schemas for events: the usual order of actions in a familiar situation. Schank and Abelson (1977) used the example of a restaurant script (enter, be seated, order, eat, pay, leave). Scripts let us act smoothly without planning each step.

    Self-schemas are schemas about ourselves: our traits, abilities and roles. Markus (1977) introduced the idea that people organise knowledge about themselves in this way. A student who sees herself as "good at science" is likely to notice and remember her science successes, choose science options and volunteer for science competitions.

    Social schemas are schemas about people, groups and social roles. A stereotype is a social schema about a group, a set of beliefs about what members of that group are like. Stereotypes are quick and easy to use, but they are often inaccurate or unfair, and they can lead us to judge an individual by the group they belong to.

    How schemas change: assimilation and accommodation

    Schemas are not fixed. The terms used to describe how they change come from Piaget, who studied how children's thinking develops.

    Assimilation happens when new information fits an existing schema, so it is added without changing the schema. A young child with a schema for "bird" (feathers, wings, flies) sees a falcon for the first time and recognises it as a bird. The schema stays the same.

    Accommodation happens when new information does not fit, so the schema has to be changed or a new one created. The same child sees a bat and calls it a bird, because it has wings and flies. When an adult explains that a bat is a mammal with no feathers, the child has to change the "bird" schema and build a new one for bats.

    Accommodation takes more effort, so people often try to assimilate first. This helps explain why stereotypes are hard to change. When someone meets a person who does not fit a stereotype, they may treat that person as an exception ("she is not like the others") rather than change the schema itself.

    How schemas guide memory: encoding, retrieval and reconstruction

    Schemas affect memory at three points.

    Encoding. Schemas decide what we pay attention to and how we make sense of it. Information that fits an active schema is easier to understand, so it is easier to encode. Information that cannot be linked to any schema may not be understood at all, and then it is poorly encoded. This is what Bransford and Johnson (1972) tested, and it is what you experienced in the warm-up.

    Retrieval. A schema can act as a plan for searching memory. If you are asked to remember a story "as a burglar would", the burglar schema reminds you to look for details about valuables and ways in. A different schema at recall can bring back different details from the same memory. This is what Anderson and Pichert (1978) tested.

    Reconstruction. Memory does not replay events like a video. It is rebuilt each time we remember, and schemas fill the gaps with what usually happens. If you try to remember a visit to a clinic, you may "remember" that a nurse checked your temperature because that usually happens, even if it did not happen that time. These are called schema-consistent errors. Chapter 8 looks at reconstructive memory in more detail.

    How schemas affect behaviour

    Schemas also shape what we do.

    • Scripts guide actions. At a drive-through café, you know to pull up, order, pay and collect, in that order. You can do this while thinking about something else.
    • Self-schemas guide choices. A student who sees himself as "not a maths person" may avoid harder maths options, practise less, and then perform worse, which seems to confirm the schema.
    • Social schemas guide how we treat others. If a teacher expects a quiet student to have little to say, the teacher may ask that student fewer questions, so the student gets fewer chances to speak.

    Behaviour guided by schemas is fast and efficient, which links to System 1 thinking, which you will study in Chapter 4. It goes wrong when the situation does not match the schema: a visitor whose script says "wait to be seated" may stand at the door of a café where everyone orders at the counter.

    Real-life examples

    • A new weekend. In 2022 the UAE government moved its official weekend from Friday and Saturday to Saturday and Sunday. People had to accommodate their schema of "the week": when to book appointments, when schools were open, when to plan family time. In the first few weeks, it would have been easy to slip back into planning around the old pattern.
    • New residents. A family who has just moved to Abu Dhabi may bring scripts for shopping or using public transport that do not quite fit. Small surprises, such as how people queue, push them to change those scripts.
    • Reading a textbook. A student who skims the headings and summary of a chapter before reading it is activating a schema, which makes the detail easier to understand and remember. A student who reads without a framework may remember very little.

    Key study 1: Bransford and Johnson (1972), context and comprehension

    AimTo investigate whether knowing the topic of a passage (an activated schema) before hearing it improves understanding and recall of the passage.
    Participants52 participants in this experiment (Experiment 2 of the paper), in three groups: No Topic (17), Topic After (17) and Topic Before (18).
    ProcedureAn independent samples design. All participants listened to a recording of a short passage about washing clothes. The passage was written in vague terms, so the topic was never named (for example, it describes sorting things into different groups and warns against doing too many at once). The Topic Before group was told the topic before listening. The Topic After group was told the topic after listening. The No Topic group was never told. All participants then rated how well they had understood the passage on a scale from 1 to 7, and had five minutes to write down as much as they could remember. Recall was scored against a list of 18 idea units decided before the experiment.
    FindingsThe Topic Before group rated their comprehension higher (a mean of 4.50 out of 7) than the Topic After group (2.12) and the No Topic group (2.29). The Topic Before group also recalled about twice as many idea units (a mean of 5.83 out of 18) as the Topic After group (2.65) and the No Topic group (2.82). Being told the topic afterwards gave no benefit. An earlier experiment in the same paper found the same pattern using a different passage, with a picture instead of a title.
    ConclusionPrior knowledge helps only if the relevant schema is activated at the time of encoding. Being given the schema afterwards is too late, because the passage was not understood when it went into memory.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example of schema theory at encoding); Paper 1 Section B; Paper 1 Section C on measurement or causality.
    Evaluate it withCausality: the only planned difference between the groups was when (or whether) they were told the topic, which supports a causal claim, but each group had only 17 or 18 people. Measurement: comprehension was a self-rating, and recall depended on judges deciding whether a sentence matched an idea unit; the schema itself was never observed. Bias: the passage was written to be confusing, and real texts usually have titles and clues, so the size of the effect may be larger than in everyday reading.

    Key study 2: Anderson and Pichert (1978), burglar or home buyer

    AimTo investigate whether a schema affects retrieval as well as encoding, by changing participants' perspective between two recalls of the same story.
    Participants39 undergraduate students on an educational psychology course at the University of Illinois, who took part to meet a course requirement (Experiment 1).
    ProcedureParticipants were randomly assigned to read a 373-word story with one of two perspectives: a burglar or a person thinking of buying a home. The story was about two boys who skip school and go to one boy's house, because his mother is never there on Thursdays. It contained 72 idea units. Some details mattered to a burglar (a side door that was always unlocked, three ten-speed bicycles in the garage, a tall hedge that hid the house from the road). Others mattered to a home buyer (a leaking roof, a musty basement, attractive grounds). Participants had two minutes to read the story, then did a 12-minute vocabulary task to stop them rehearsing it, then wrote down as much of the story as they could. After a five-minute task, they recalled the story a second time. Half kept the same perspective. The other half were asked to switch to the other perspective.
    FindingsAt first recall, participants remembered more of the information that was important to their own perspective. At second recall, the group that switched recalled 7.1% more of the information important to the new perspective, which they had not written down the first time. The group that kept the same perspective did not show this gain. In a second experiment with 16 participants, all of whom switched, the gain was 10%. Interviews afterwards gave some support to the idea that participants used the new perspective as a plan for searching their memory.
    ConclusionSchemas affect retrieval, not only encoding. Information that was encoded but not recalled can be brought back when a new schema provides different cues.
    What happened nextKardash, Royer and Greene (1988) used a version of the same story with 127 undergraduates, giving the perspective either at reading or only at testing. They concluded that the perspective affected retrieval but not encoding. This supports the retrieval finding, but it sits awkwardly with Bransford and Johnson's evidence for encoding effects. One possible explanation is that a story about a house is easy to understand without a perspective, whereas the washing clothes passage was not.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example of schema theory at retrieval); Paper 1 Section B; Paper 1 Section C on perspective or measurement; Paper 2 Section B practice (an experiment with two recall tests).
    Evaluate it withPerspective: the researchers assigned the perspective; in everyday life people choose their own, and may hold more than one at once. Causality: random assignment and a same-perspective control group support a causal claim about the shift. Measurement: written recall may underestimate what people know, because they may not write down details they think are unimportant. Bias: participants were American undergraduates taking part for course credit, and they may have guessed what a burglar "should" notice rather than remembered it.

    How useful is schema theory?

    Strengths. It explains a wide range of behaviour with one idea: why we understand some things quickly, why we remember some details and forget others, why memory errors follow patterns, and why stereotypes affect how we treat people. It is supported by experiments that manipulate the schema directly (giving a topic, assigning a perspective) and measure the effect on memory. It has practical uses. Teachers who give students the big picture before the details, and textbooks that open each chapter with headings and a summary, are using schema theory.

    Limitations. Schemas cannot be observed directly. Researchers infer them from what people recall or do, which risks a circular argument: a schema is used to explain a memory, and the memory is then used as evidence that the schema exists. The theory is vague about how schemas are formed in the first place, how they are stored, and how the mind chooses which schema to use in a given moment. Alba and Hasher (1983) argued that memory often keeps much more detail than schema theory predicts, including details that do not fit any schema. Much of the evidence comes from short, artificial texts in laboratory conditions, which may not reflect everyday life.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to schema theory
    MeasurementSchemas are never observed; they are inferred from recall scored in idea units, from comprehension ratings and from behaviour.
    CausalityBoth key studies manipulated a schema (the topic, the perspective) and measured recall, which supports causal claims, but the samples were small.
    BiasStereotypes are social schemas that bias judgement; schemas also produce schema-consistent memory errors. Both key studies used American students.
    ChangeSchemas change through accommodation, and a change of perspective changed what people could recall from the same story.
    PerspectiveA person's point of view acts as a schema that shapes what they remember. Schema theory itself is a cognitive perspective; biological and sociocultural approaches add how schemas are stored in the brain and learned in a culture.
    ResponsibilityTeachers, employers and the media have a responsibility not to strengthen unfair stereotypes, because social schemas shape how people are treated.
