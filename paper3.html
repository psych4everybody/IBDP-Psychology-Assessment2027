    What Paper 3 is testing

    Paper 3 gives you a claim and five sources of research on it. You do not need to recall studies for most of the marks. You need to read data carefully, judge how far a study can be trusted, and weigh evidence for and against a claim.

    30marks in total
    105minutes
    5sources: 1 on the paper, 4 in the booklet
    4questions, always in the same order

    The claim is about one HL extension (culture, motivation or technology) inside one context (health and well-being, human development, human relationships, or learning and cognition). The data may come from real studies or may be created for the exam, and it will include both quantitative and qualitative research, experimental and non-experimental.

    Remember

    The four question templates are published, so there are no surprises in the wording. What changes is the claim, the sources and, in Q3, which of the three research considerations you get.

    Reading and planning

    Use the 40 minutes on purpose

    Forty minutes feels like a lot of reading time. It is not reading time: it is Q4 planning time. Read every source once with one question in mind: does this support the claim, challenge it, or complicate it?

    1

    Circle the claim

    Underline the key words. "Can", "may" and "for adolescents" all narrow what you must prove.

    2

    Label each source

    Method, sample, quantitative or qualitative, and one word: for, against or mixed.

    3

    Find one weakness each

    Sample, setting, measure, design. This becomes your evaluation in Q4.

    4

    Answer Q1 to Q3 fast

    They are worth 15 marks between them. Do not let them eat Q4 time.

    5

    Fill the source grid

    One row per source: what it shows, how strong it is, which side it supports.

    6

    Decide your answer

    Write your conclusion first, in one sentence, so every paragraph argues towards it.

    Source grid: Source | method and sample | finding | for / against / mixed | main limitation | links to (theory)
    Toolkit

    Words you need for every Paper 3

    Quantitative sources

    • Mean, median, mode: the typical score. The median resists outliers.
    • Standard deviation, range, interquartile range: how spread out scores are.
    • Overlap: if two groups' spreads overlap a lot, a gap in means matters less.
    • Skew: a long tail on one side pulls the mean towards it.
    • Correlation (r): direction and strength, never cause.
    • Significance: the result is unlikely to be due to chance (p < 0.05, or a test value above its critical value).
    • Effect size: how big the difference is, not just whether it is real.

    Qualitative sources

    • Credibility: do the findings truly reflect the participants' experiences?
    • Bias: did the participants or the researcher shape the data?
    • Transferability: could the findings apply to other people and settings?
    • Triangulation: checking with other methods, researchers, sources or theories.
    • Member checking: participants confirm the researcher's interpretation.
    • Reflexivity: the researcher records how they may have influenced the study.
    • Thick description: rich detail about the sample and setting, so readers can judge transfer.
    What Q1 asks

    One issue that limits how we can read the data

    Source 1 is printed on the question paper, usually as a graph or table. Q1 asks you to explain one thing about how the data is presented or summarised that stops us reading it with confidence.

    The examiner lesson

    Q1 is about interpreting the data as shown, not about the study's validity. In the IB-marked samples, explaining a shortened y-axis earned full marks. Listing general validity issues such as sample size or demand characteristics does not answer the question unless you show how it limits reading the graph or table itself.

    MarkWhat it takes (paraphrased from the guide)
    1You name a relevant issue.
    2You describe it clearly, pointing to where it is in the source.
    3You explain it: what a reader might wrongly conclude, and why.
    The structure

    Identify, point, explain

    1

    Identify

    Name the issue in one phrase: "The y-axis starts at 40, not 0."

    2

    Point

    Use the actual values from the source: "so the bars for 46 and 48 look very different."

    3

    Explain

    Say what a reader would wrongly conclude: "This exaggerates a 2-point difference, so we cannot judge how meaningful it is."

    Frame: "One issue is that . In source 1, (quote values or labels). This limits interpretation because a reader might conclude , when in fact ."
    Only one

    The question asks for one issue. Writing three short ones does not earn more than one well-explained one. Pick the clearest and develop it.

    The issue menu

    What to look for in source 1

    Look atIssueWhy it limits interpretation
    The y-axisStarts above zero (truncated)Small differences look large.
    Axis labels and unitsMissing, vague ("Score"), or scale not explainedWe cannot tell what was measured or whether high is good or bad.
    Group sizesPercentages with no n70% of 10 people and 70% of 1,000 people are not equally trustworthy.
    SpreadMeans with no SD, range or error barsWe cannot tell whether groups overlap, so a small gap may mean nothing.
    The x-axisUnequal time intervals or bin widths drawn evenlyTrends look steeper or flatter than they are; distributions look a different shape.
    RatesRates without a base (per 100 or per 1,000?)Groups cannot be compared fairly.
    Two y-axesDifferent scales on left and rightLines can be made to cross or match by choosing the scales.
    PercentagesDo not add to 100, or categories overlapWe cannot tell how many people chose each option or whether people were counted twice.
    The titleCausal wording ("X improves Y") on correlational dataInvites a cause-and-effect reading the data cannot support.
    What is shownOnly extreme groups, or different time windowsThe comparison is not representative or not like for like.

    Checklist

    Before you move on from Q1

    What Q2 asks

    Analyse, then conclude

    "Analyse" means break the findings down to bring out what matters. Q2 has two jobs: a detailed analysis of the numbers in source 2, and a conclusion that is explicitly linked to those findings and to the claim.

    MarksAnalysisConclusion
    1 to 2Limited, or contains mistakes.Attempted but not relevant.
    3 to 4Accurate but lacks detail or development.Stated, but the link to the findings is unclear.
    5 to 6The data is analysed in detail.Explicitly linked to the findings.

    Paraphrased from the guide's Q2 markband.

    The examiner lesson

    In the IB-marked samples, responses lost marks for not mentioning the overlap or the skew of the data, and for a conclusion that was "very general". One response also went beyond the data, which is never rewarded.

    The moves

    Five analysis moves

    You will rarely need all five. Use every one the data allows.

    MoveWhat you write
    1. Compare the centres"The online group's mean (12.4) was 1.1 points lower than the face-to-face group's (13.5)."
    2. Compare the spread"But the online group was more varied (SD 4.8 vs 2.9), so the mean hides a wide range of outcomes."
    3. Look at overlap and shape"The interquartile ranges overlap almost completely. The online group is positively skewed: a few high scores pull its mean up."
    4. Check significance"The calculated U of 41 is above the critical value of 37, so the difference is not significant at p < 0.05."
    5. Note what the data cannot tell us"Only 18 participants were in each group, and the study did not include a follow-up, so we cannot judge whether the effect lasted."
    Check the direction of the test

    For most tests (t, chi-squared, z) the result is significant when the calculated value is larger than the critical value. For Mann-Whitney U and Wilcoxon T it must be smaller. Read what the source says before you decide.

    The conclusion

    Link it twice: to the findings and to the claim

    Frame: "Because (the key finding with its number), source 2 partly supports / supports / does not support the claim that . However, this only applies to (sample, measure or setting)."

    Do

    • Repeat the key number in the conclusion.
    • Use the words of the claim.
    • Hedge to match the evidence: "suggests", "for this sample".

    Do not

    • Claim a cause from a correlation.
    • Bring in other sources here. Q2 is about source 2 only.
    • Write "the claim is proven".

    What Q3 asks

    One of three questions about a qualitative source

    You might getWhich really asks
    Discuss how the researcher could improve the credibility of the findings in source 3.How could we be more confident that the findings truly reflect the participants' experiences?
    Discuss how the researcher could avoid bias in source 3.Where could participants or the researcher have distorted the data, and what would prevent it?
    To what extent are the findings from source 3 transferable to other populations or contexts?Who and where could these findings apply to, and what stops them applying further?
    MarksUnderstandingUse of the source
    1 to 2Limited understanding of the consideration.Limited or missing.
    3 to 4Some understanding, with some inaccuracies.Implicit.
    5 to 6Detailed understanding.Explicit.

    Paraphrased from the guide's Q3 markband.

    The examiner lesson

    In the IB-marked samples, a "list-like" answer was held at 4, and an answer with general knowledge of focus groups but no supporting evidence from the source got 2. Every point must name something specific in source 3.

    The structure

    Two or three developed points, not a list

    1

    Point

    Name the strategy or threat: "The researcher could use member checking."

    2

    Source

    Quote the detail that makes it necessary: "Only one researcher coded the 14 interviews."

    3

    Why

    Explain how it changes the findings: "Her expectations could shape which themes she saw."

    4

    Limit

    Discuss it: "But participants may agree to please her, so it works best with a second coder."

    "Discuss" and "to what extent" both reward weighing up, so the limit step is what lifts an answer above a list of strategies.

    Strategy bank

    What to say for each consideration

    ConsiderationLook for in the sourceStrategies to discuss
    CredibilityOne researcher; one method; short interviews; no checking with participants; few direct quotes.Method triangulation (add observation or a survey); researcher triangulation (second coder, agreement check); member checking; reflexivity; longer or repeated contact; reporting cases that do not fit the themes.
    BiasResearcher's role or relationship with participants (teacher, manager, clinician); leading questions; group setting; sensitive topic; researcher knew the hypothesis; self-selected sample.Participant bias: anonymity, rapport, neutral wording, interviews in private, an interviewer with no power over participants. Researcher bias: reflexivity, a second coder blind to the hypothesis, an inductive approach, an audit trail of coding decisions.
    TransferabilitySample size and type; one school, clinic or company; one country or culture; age range; how participants were recruited; whether the setting is described in detail.Sampling: purposive or snowball sampling for a wider range; data saturation; thick description so readers can judge similarity; repeating the study in other settings. Discuss which groups the findings plausibly do and do not transfer to, and why.
    Do not mix them up

    Transferability is the qualitative idea closest to generalisability, but answer in qualitative terms: thick description, sampling and context. A credibility question is not answered by "use a bigger sample".

    What Q4 asks

    To what extent can we conclude the claim?

    Q4 is half the paper. You interpret at least three of sources 2 to 5, add your own knowledge of the HL extension, discuss different points of view, and argue to a conclusion about how far the claim holds.

    MarksUnderstanding of the claimUse of the sourcesPoints of view and conclusion

    10 to 12Some detail and development.Critical analysis that lacks development; two or more sources.Some discussion of points of view; conclusion consistent with the argument.

    The examiner lesson

    One IB-marked response reached the top band for understanding and for use of the sources, but its points of view were "more descriptive rather than an evaluation", which held that strand at 10 to 12. Saying what each side thinks is not enough. Say which side has the stronger evidence, and why.

    Points of view

    What counts as a point of view

    A point of view is a position someone could reasonably take on the claim. Paper 3 sources are chosen so that they do not all agree, which gives you points of view to work with.

    Where points of view come from

    • Sources that support the claim versus sources that challenge it.
    • Subgroups inside one source (younger and older, different activities, different countries).
    • Different theories that predict different outcomes.
    • Different stakeholders: patients and clinicians, students and teachers, employees and managers.

    How to evaluate them

    • Which side has the stronger design (experiment versus self-report)?
    • Which has the larger or more relevant sample?
    • Do the qualitative and quantitative sources agree (triangulation)?
    • Can both be true, for different people or conditions?

    Own knowledge

    Bring in what you know, briefly and relevantly

    Own knowledge is anything not in the booklet: theories, concepts and studies about the HL extension and the context. Use it to explain why a source found what it did, or to show that a pattern holds or fails elsewhere. One or two sentences per idea is usually enough.

    Culture

    Cultural dimensions (individualism and collectivism), independent and interdependent self, emic and etic approaches, imposed etic, WEIRD samples, acculturation strategies.

    Motivation

    Self-determination theory (autonomy, competence, relatedness), intrinsic and extrinsic motivation, overjustification effect, goal-setting, expectancy and self-efficacy.

    Technology

    Displacement hypothesis, the "Goldilocks" idea of moderate use, cognitive load and multitasking, cognitive offloading, active versus passive use, digital divide.

    Do not write a Paper 1 essay

    Own knowledge supports your interpretation of the sources. If a paragraph never mentions a source, it is probably drifting.
