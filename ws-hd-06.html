    What is enculturation?

    Enculturation

    Enculturation is the process by which a person learns the values, beliefs, norms and practices of their own culture, usually from birth and through childhood. It is how a baby becomes a member of a particular community: someone who knows how to greet people, what to wear for a wedding, how to behave in a classroom, and what counts as polite or rude.

    Most of enculturation happens without a lesson plan. The child watches, copies, is praised or gently corrected, and slowly the rules start to feel "normal". By adolescence, many of these rules feel less like rules and more like "who I am". That is why this topic belongs in the study of the development of self.

    Enculturation is often confused with acculturation. Acculturation is the process of cultural and psychological change that happens when a person, or a group, comes into contact with a second culture, for example after moving to a new country. Berry (1997) described different ways people handle this contact, such as keeping their heritage culture while also taking part in the new one. In a UAE school you can see both side by side: a child who grew up in Abu Dhabi is being enculturated, while a classmate who has just moved from Manila, Mumbai or Manchester is going through acculturation.

    Social norms: the unwritten rules

    A social norm is a shared expectation about how people in a group should behave in a particular situation. Norms are usually unwritten and are not laws. They tell us what is expected (stand when an elder enters) and what is not accepted (interrupting a guest).

    Three features of norms matter for this chapter:

    • They are learned, not inborn. A baby does not know which hand to use when passing a cup. The rule has to be acquired.
    • They vary between cultures and groups. Taking shoes off at the door is expected in many homes in the UAE and in many other countries, but not everywhere.
    • They are kept in place by other people's reactions. Following a norm usually brings approval, or no reaction at all. Breaking one brings a raised eyebrow, a correction, or embarrassment. These reactions are how children find out where the rules are.

    Gender roles are one important set of social norms. A gender role is a set of shared expectations about the activities, behaviour and dress that a culture sees as typical for men and women, or for boys and girls. In the UAE, for example, national dress differs for men (the kandura and ghutra) and women (the abaya and shayla), and children learn these expectations early. Gender roles differ between cultures and change over time, which is evidence that they are learned. This chapter describes how children learn them. It does not judge which gender roles are right.

    How are norms learned? Three processes

    You met social learning theory in the Cognition and Learning workbook. Bussey and Bandura (1999) applied the same ideas to gender development and described three main ways children learn what is expected of them. The same three processes work for almost any social norm.

    ProcessWhat happensExample
    Direct tuitionSomeone tells the child what to do or not do"Say salam to your grandfather first."
    Modelling (observational learning)The child watches others, often admired or similar models, and copies themA girl watches her aunt pour coffee for the eldest guest first, then copies it in play
    Enactive experience (reinforcement)The child tries a behaviour and learns from the reactions it bringsA boy is smiled at and praised when he stands up for a guest, so he does it again

    Children do not only absorb norms; they also go looking for them. This is where cognitive explanations, such as gender schema theory, come in.

    Agents of enculturation

    An agent of enculturation is any person, group or institution that passes on the norms of a culture. Four are especially important in childhood and adolescence.

    • Family. The first and, for young children, the strongest agent. Parents, grandparents and older siblings model behaviour every day and react to what the child does. In many Gulf families, grandparents and the wider extended family play a large part in teaching norms of respect and hospitality.
    • School. Schools teach explicit rules (uniform, lining up, raising your hand) and also a "hidden curriculum" of norms, such as how to address a teacher, when to speak and when to stay quiet. Morning assembly and the national anthem teach norms about belonging to a nation.
    • Peers. Friends and classmates become more influential as children grow older. Peers react quickly when someone breaks a group norm, for example by teasing a child who chooses an "unusual" game. You will study peer influence in more detail in a later chapter.
    • Media. Television, games, apps and social media show children what people "like them" wear, say and do. Media can also carry the norms of other cultures.

    Real-life examples

    • Greetings at a family gathering. At a family gathering in Al Ain, a six-year-old moves around the room greeting the oldest relatives first. Nobody gave him a list. He has watched his father do it many times (modelling), was once reminded "your grandmother first" (direct tuition), and is smiled at when he gets it right (reinforcement).
    • Hospitality. In many Emirati homes, guests are offered Arabic coffee and dates. Children learn by watching and helping: the coffee is served with the right hand, the eldest or most senior guest is often served first, and a guest gently shakes the small cup when they have had enough. A child who helps serve is learning a norm that is part of the family's identity as good hosts.
    • School routines. At a school in Dubai, a student who has just arrived from another country watches classmates stand still for the national anthem and greet the teacher as she walks in. For her, this is acculturation. For the Grade 1 children in the same assembly, it is enculturation: they are learning the routine for the first time.

    Gender schema theory

    Schema theory

    Social learning explains how norms are passed on. Gender schema theory adds the child's own thinking. A schema is a mental framework that organises knowledge and guides what we notice and remember (you met schema theory in the Cognition and Learning workbook). A gender schema is a schema about what boys and girls, or men and women, are like and what they do.

    Bem (1981) and Martin and Halverson (1981) both developed versions of gender schema theory. Martin and Halverson's version makes three main claims:

    • Children first build an "in-group/out-group" schema. This is a simple sorting rule: some toys, clothes and activities are "for boys", others are "for girls".
    • Children then build an "own-sex" schema. This is a more detailed schema for their own group: how to do the activities that "people like me" do.
    • Schemas guide attention and memory. Children pay more attention to information that fits their own group and remember it better. Information that does not fit may be ignored, forgotten or changed in memory so that it fits.

    The important idea is that the child is active. Once a child knows "I am a girl" or "I am a boy", the child seeks out information about their own group. A norm is then no longer only "what adults expect". It is "what people like me do".

    Key study 1: Fagot (1978), parents' reactions to toddlers

    AimTo find out whether parents react differently to the same behaviours depending on whether their toddler is a boy or a girl.
    Participants24 white American families, each with one child aged 20 to 24 months: 12 boys and 12 girls. Both parents lived in the home.
    ProcedureA naturalistic observation in the families' own homes. Each family was observed five times, for one hour each time, over five weeks. Trained observers used a checklist of 46 child behaviours (such as playing with dolls, playing with blocks, running and jumping, asking for help) and recorded whether each parent's reaction was positive, negative or neutral. Afterwards, parents rated each of the 46 behaviours as more suitable for boys, for girls, or for both, and completed a questionnaire about their views on raising boys and girls.
    FindingsAcross most behaviours, parents treated boys and girls in a similar way. Clear differences appeared for behaviours seen as typical of one sex. Girls received more positive reactions for playing with dolls, and boys received more negative reactions for it. Boys received more positive reactions for playing with blocks. Girls received more negative reactions for large movements such as running, jumping and climbing. Parents reacted more positively when girls asked for help, and more negatively when boys did. Yet parents had not rated asking for help as a behaviour more suitable for girls.
    ConclusionParents pass on gender roles through small, everyday positive and negative reactions. Some of this may happen without parents being aware of it, because their reactions did not always match their stated views.
    What happened nextLytton and Romney (1991) combined the results of 172 studies of how parents treat sons and daughters. Most differences were small. Out of 19 areas of parenting, the only one where both mothers and fathers in North American studies clearly treated boys and girls differently was encouraging sex-typed activities, such as play with certain toys. Fathers made more distinctions than mothers. This supports Fagot's main finding, while showing that parents treat sons and daughters similarly in many other ways.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example of enculturation through reinforcement); Paper 1 Section B; Paper 1 Section C on measurement, causality or responsibility; Paper 2 Section B practice (a naturalistic observation with a checklist).
    Evaluate it withMeasurement: a checklist turns everyday family life into countable reactions, which can be compared across families. Five hours per family is still a small slice of a child's life. Bias: the families knew they were being observed, so they may have behaved differently (demand characteristics). 24 white American families from the 1970s is a small sample from one culture and one period. Causality: the study shows that reactions differed, not that these reactions caused the children's later gender roles. Children may already have been behaving differently, and parents may have been responding to that.

    Key study 2: Martin and Halverson (1983), gender schemas and memory

    AimTo test whether gender schemas affect what young children remember, and in particular whether children distort information that does not fit their schemas.
    Participants48 children aged 5 to 6 (24 boys and 24 girls), from local kindergartens.
    ProcedureEach child was shown 16 pictures, one at a time. Half showed gender-consistent activities, such as a boy playing with a truck. The other half showed gender-inconsistent activities, such as a girl chopping wood. One week later, the children's memory for the pictures was tested. They were asked about the activities they had seen and whether a boy or a girl had done each one, and they rated how confident they were.
    FindingsChildren remembered the sex of the person in gender-consistent pictures well. For gender-inconsistent pictures, they often distorted the memory by changing the sex of the person, for example remembering that a boy, not a girl, had chopped the wood. Children were more confident about gender-consistent pictures. When they did distort an inconsistent picture, they were often as confident in the wrong answer as they were in correct answers about consistent pictures.
    ConclusionGender schemas affect memory. Information that does not fit a schema is likely to be changed so that it does fit. This helps explain why gender roles can be hard to change: a child may "remember" the world as more traditional than it really was.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example of enculturation, or of schema theory); Paper 1 Section B; Paper 1 Section C on measurement, perspective or change.
    Evaluate it withMeasurement: a schema cannot be seen. The researchers inferred it from errors in memory, and the confidence ratings added useful detail. Perspective: this is a cognitive explanation that treats the child as active, which balances the sociocultural focus on parents and other agents. Bias: pictures shown in a test are not the same as everyday life, so the task may lack ecological validity, and the sample came from one culture.

    How do norms become part of the self?

    The learning objective asks about the role of enculturation in the development of self. The self includes our self-concept ("what I am like"), our identity ("which groups I belong to") and the standards we use to judge our own behaviour. Enculturation shapes all three. A useful way to picture this is as a move from outside to inside:

    1. External control. A young child follows a norm because an adult is watching, praising or correcting. The rule belongs to other people.
    2. Self-regulation. The child starts to follow the norm when no one is watching, and may correct others ("That is not how we do it!"). Bussey and Bandura (1999) argued that, over time, children take on the standards they have learned and use them to guide and judge their own behaviour.
    3. Identity. The norm becomes part of how the person describes themselves: "In my family, we always look after guests." Breaking it now feels uncomfortable, even in private, because it would go against who they are.

    Your answer to question 2 in the warm-up may be an example of the third step.

    How useful is research on enculturation?

    Strengths. The research combines different perspectives. Fagot (1978) shows the role of the family, a sociocultural factor, and Martin and Halverson (1983) show the role of the child's own thinking, a cognitive factor. Together they explain both how norms are passed on and why they stick. Naturalistic observation gives a realistic picture of family life, and the research has practical uses, such as helping schools make "unwritten rules" visible for new students.

    Limitations. The classic studies used small samples from one Western culture, so we cannot assume the same processes work in the same way in every culture or in every family. Much of the research is correlational: it shows that parents or peers react differently, but not that those reactions cause children's later behaviour or identity. Enculturation takes years, but most studies take a snapshot of a few hours or one week. The research also focuses mostly on gender roles, while norms such as respect for elders have been studied less.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to enculturation of social norms
    MeasurementNorms and schemas are not visible. Fagot counted parents' reactions on a checklist; Martin and Halverson inferred schemas from memory errors.
    CausalityObservations show that parents react differently to boys and girls, but not that these reactions cause later gender roles. Children may already behave differently.
    BiasSmall Western samples, families who know they are being watched, and observers who know the child's sex can all bias the findings.
    ChangeNorms change across generations and when people move between cultures (acculturation). Within one child, a norm changes from an external rule to part of the self.
    PerspectiveA sociocultural perspective focuses on agents of enculturation; a cognitive perspective focuses on the child's own schemas. Researchers from one culture may also misread the norms of another.
    ResponsibilityResearchers who observe young children at home must protect privacy and get parents' consent. They must also describe a culture's norms fairly, without treating their own culture's norms as the "correct" ones.
