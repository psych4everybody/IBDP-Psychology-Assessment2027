    Learning as a change in behaviour

    In psychology, learning is a relatively lasting change in behaviour that comes from experience. Growing taller is not learning; fastening your seatbelt without thinking, after hundreds of car journeys, is.

    In the early twentieth century, a group of psychologists called behaviourists argued that psychology should study only what can be observed: stimuli in the environment and the responses people and animals make to them. John B. Watson was one of the first to make this argument. Behaviourists studied two kinds of learning by association, called conditioning.

    • Classical conditioning: learning that two stimuli go together. It works on automatic responses, such as salivating, feeling afraid or feeling excited.
    • Operant conditioning: learning that a behaviour leads to a consequence. It works on voluntary behaviour, such as tidying a room, raising a hand or handing in homework.

    In the warm-up, Hamdan's story is classical conditioning. Rashid's brother and Fatima are examples of operant conditioning.

    Classical conditioning: learning by association

    Classical conditioning

    Ivan Pavlov was a Russian physiologist who won the 1904 Nobel Prize for his research on digestion. While he was measuring how much saliva dogs produced, he noticed that they began to salivate before the food arrived. This led him to study how a new stimulus could come to trigger a reflex. He trained hungry dogs to salivate at the sound of a metronome or a buzzer that had been repeatedly presented just before food.

    Classical conditioning has five key terms. Learn them in this order.

    • Unconditioned stimulus (UCS): something that triggers a response naturally, with no learning. For Pavlov's dogs, the food.
    • Unconditioned response (UCR): the natural, unlearned response to the UCS. Salivating at food.
    • Neutral stimulus (NS): something that does not trigger the response before learning. The sound of the metronome.
    • Conditioned stimulus (CS): the neutral stimulus after it has been paired with the UCS many times. It now triggers the response on its own.
    • Conditioned response (CR): the learned response to the CS. Salivating at the sound alone.

    Notice that the UCR and the CR are often the same behaviour (salivating). What changes is the stimulus that triggers it. The UCR is a response to the food; the CR is a response to the sound.

    StagePavlov's dogsHamdan (Warm-up, story A)
    Before conditioningFood (UCS) produces salivation (UCR). The metronome (NS) produces no salivation.Lunch (UCS) makes his mouth water (UCR). The tune (NS) means nothing.
    During conditioningThe metronome is presented just before the food, many times.The tune plays just before lunch, every school day.
    After conditioningThe metronome (CS) alone produces salivation (CR).The tune (CS) alone makes his mouth water (CR), even in a shopping centre.

    After the learning: extinction, recovery, generalisation and discrimination

    A conditioned response is not fixed. It can weaken, return, spread or narrow.

    • Extinction. If the CS is presented again and again without the UCS, the CR gradually weakens and stops. If Hamdan's school played the tune every day but lunch came an hour later, his mouth would eventually stop watering at the tune.
    • Spontaneous recovery. After extinction and a rest period, the CR can suddenly return, usually weaker than before. After the summer holiday, Hamdan might find his mouth watering at the tune again on the first day back.
    • Generalisation. Stimuli that are similar to the CS can also trigger the CR. A tune with a similar melody might make Hamdan hungry too.
    • Discrimination. With experience, the learner responds only to the CS and not to similar stimuli, because only the CS is followed by the UCS. If a similar tune is played for assemblies and never before lunch, Hamdan learns to tell the two apart.

    Operant conditioning: learning from consequences

    Operant conditioning

    Edward Thorndike (1898) placed hungry cats in a puzzle box, a crate with a latch or loop that opened the door. Food was outside the box. At first the cats moved about at random. When a random movement opened the door, they escaped and reached the food. Over repeated trials, the time they took to escape fell gradually. Thorndike explained this with the law of effect: behaviour that is followed by a satisfying consequence becomes more likely to happen again in that situation, and behaviour that is followed by an unpleasant consequence becomes less likely.

    B. F. Skinner (1938) developed this idea into operant conditioning. He studied rats pressing a lever, and later pigeons pecking a key, inside an operant chamber (often called a Skinner box). The animal "operates" on its environment, and the consequence that follows shapes what it does next. Skinner described two kinds of consequence:

    • Reinforcement makes a behaviour more likely.
    • Punishment makes a behaviour less likely.

    Each can be positive (something is added) or negative (something is taken away). Positive and negative do not mean good and bad. Think of them as plus and minus.

    Behaviour becomes MORE likely (reinforcement)Behaviour becomes LESS likely (punishment)
    Something is ADDED (positive)Positive reinforcement. A student answers a question and the teacher praises her. She answers more often.Positive punishment. A student talks during a test and is given extra work. He talks less in tests.
    Something is TAKEN AWAY (negative)Negative reinforcement. Fatima fastens her seatbelt and the beeping stops. She fastens it sooner next time.Negative punishment. A driver is fined for speeding and loses money. He speeds less.

    The most common mistake in exams is to treat negative reinforcement as a kind of punishment. It is not. Negative reinforcement always makes a behaviour more likely, because the behaviour removes something unpleasant.

    Schedules of reinforcement and shaping

    How often reinforcement arrives changes the pattern of behaviour. Continuous reinforcement (a reward every time) produces fast learning, but the behaviour also stops quickly once the reward stops. Partial reinforcement (a reward only some of the time) is slower to build, but the behaviour lasts longer when rewards stop. Skinner described four partial schedules.

    ScheduleThe ruleEveryday examplePattern of behaviour
    Fixed ratioReward after a set number of responses.A café loyalty card: a free juice after every ninth stamp.High rate of responding, with a short pause after each reward.
    Variable ratioReward after an unpredictable number of responses.Fishing from a boat off the coast: you never know which cast will catch a fish.High, steady rate. The hardest pattern to extinguish.
    Fixed intervalReward for the first response after a set amount of time.A vocabulary quiz every Thursday: many students revise only on Wednesday night.Little responding after a reward, then a rise as the next one gets close.
    Variable intervalReward for the first response after an unpredictable amount of time.Unannounced quizzes: you cannot tell when the next one is coming.Slow but steady responding.

    Shaping is used to teach a behaviour that the learner would not produce on their own. The trainer reinforces successive approximations: small steps that get closer and closer to the target. A swimming coach at a club in Sharjah might first praise a nervous child for putting her face in the water, then for floating, then for kicking while floating, and only later for swimming a width.

    Real-life examples

    • Loyalty cards and apps. Many cafés and supermarkets in the UAE give points or stamps for each purchase. A reward after a set number of purchases is a fixed ratio schedule of positive reinforcement. Its aim is to make the behaviour (coming back) more likely.
    • Traffic fines. A speeding fine takes money away after a behaviour. That is negative punishment. Speed cameras make the consequence more likely, and a likely, quick consequence works better than a rare or delayed one.
    • Advertising. A drink brand shown again and again with the excitement of a football match is using classical conditioning. The brand (NS) is paired with something that already produces good feelings (UCS), so the brand alone may come to produce a small positive feeling (CR).

    Applying operant conditioning to change behaviour

    Because operant conditioning focuses on behaviour that can be observed, it can be turned into a plan. Most behaviour change programmes follow similar steps: define the target behaviour clearly (for example, "stays in seat during the lesson" rather than "behaves well"); measure how often it happens before anything changes (the baseline); reinforce the target behaviour quickly and consistently; then reduce the rewards gradually so that the behaviour continues without them.

    A token economy is one well-known example. People earn tokens (points, stars or stickers) for target behaviours. The tokens have no value on their own, but they can be exchanged for things that do, such as free time or a privilege. Tokens become secondary reinforcers, because they are linked with rewards that people want. Ayllon and Azrin (1968) described one of the first token economies, on a ward of a psychiatric hospital in the USA. House points and merit systems in schools work on the same principle.

    Classroom programmes can also use group contingencies, where the consequence depends on how a whole group behaves. The Good Behaviour Game, the second key study below, is the best-known example.

    Key study 1: Watson and Rayner (1920), "Little Albert"

    AimTo find out whether fear could be classically conditioned in a human infant, whether the fear would transfer to other objects, how long it would last, and how it might be removed.
    ParticipantsOne infant, known as "Albert B.", who had been raised almost from birth in a children's hospital in the USA where his mother worked. The researchers described him as calm and unemotional, which was one reason they chose him.
    ProcedureAt about nine months old, Albert was shown a white rat, a rabbit, a dog, a monkey, masks, cotton wool and burning newspaper. He showed no fear of any of them. The researchers then struck a steel bar with a hammer behind his head. This loud noise (UCS) made him cry (UCR). At 11 months old, each time Albert reached for the white rat (NS), the bar was struck behind him. He received two pairings, then five more a week later: seven in total.
    FindingsAfter the seven pairings, Albert cried and moved away when the rat was shown on its own. Five days later, he reacted negatively to the rabbit, the dog, a fur coat, cotton wool and a Santa Claus mask, but not to his toy blocks. His reactions were weaker when he was tested in a different room. When he was tested again a month later, he still reacted to several of these objects, but less strongly. Albert left the hospital that day, and no attempt was made to remove his fear.
    ConclusionAn emotional response such as fear can be classically conditioned in a human, and it can generalise to similar stimuli.
    What happened nextHarris (1979) showed that many textbooks exaggerated the study, for example by claiming that Albert developed a lasting phobia. Harris argued that the fear was hard to produce and that the results were hard to interpret. Researchers still disagree about who Albert was: Beck and colleagues (2009) proposed one child, and Powell and colleagues (2014) argued for another. In 1924, Mary Cover Jones reduced a young boy's fear of rabbits by bringing a rabbit closer to him step by step, an early example of using conditioning to remove a fear.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example of classical conditioning); Paper 1 Section B; Paper 1 Section C on responsibility or causality.
    Evaluate it withResponsibility: the researchers deliberately frightened a baby. They wrote that they knew he would soon leave the hospital, yet they did not try to remove the fear before he left. Albert could not consent, and his mother worked for the hospital, which raises questions about whether she felt free to say no. The study would not be allowed under modern ethics codes. Causality: there was only one child and no control condition, so other explanations for his reactions cannot be ruled out. Measurement: fear was judged by the researchers watching his crying and movements, with no standard measure.

    Key study 2: Barrish, Saunders and Wolf (1969), the Good Behaviour Game

    AimTo test whether a classroom game, in which a team's consequences depended on each member's behaviour, would reduce disruptive behaviour.
    ParticipantsA class of 24 fourth-grade students (about 9 to 10 years old) in a school in Kansas, USA. Several students in the class had been described as "problem children".
    ProcedureObservers recorded two behaviours, minute by minute, during maths and reading lessons: being out of seat and talking out without permission. After a baseline period, the class was split into two teams. Each time a team member broke a rule, the teacher put a mark against that team on the board. The team with fewer marks won, and both teams could win if they stayed below a set number of marks. Winners earned privileges such as extra break time, being first in the lunch queue, time for special projects, stars and victory tags. The game was played first in maths only, then in reading only (it was removed from maths), and finally in both lessons.
    FindingsAt baseline, disruptive behaviour happened in most observation intervals. When the game was played in maths, talking out and being out of seat fell sharply in maths but stayed high in reading. When the game moved to reading, disruption in reading fell and disruption in maths rose back towards baseline. When the game was played in both lessons, disruption was low in both.
    ConclusionGroup consequences for individual behaviour can reduce disruptive behaviour in a classroom. Because behaviour changed only in the lesson where the game was played, the change can be linked to the game itself.
    What happened nextResearchers led by Sheppard Kellam ran randomised trials of the game in first-grade classrooms in Baltimore, USA, starting in 1985, in a trial involving more than 2,300 children (some classes played the game and others were comparison classes). The game reduced aggressive behaviour in the early school years, and follow-ups into early adulthood linked it with fewer later behavioural and mental health problems.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example of operant conditioning applied to change behaviour); Paper 1 Section B; Paper 1 Section C on change or causality; Paper 2 Section B practice (a field experiment with one class).
    Evaluate it withCausality: behaviour changed when the game was introduced and changed back when it was removed, which is strong evidence that the game caused the change. However, it was one class with one teacher. Change: in the original study, behaviour improved only while the game was being played; the later Baltimore trials were needed to show longer-term change. Responsibility: a whole team loses out because of one student, which could create peer pressure on that student. Teachers need to decide which behaviours count as "disruptive", and students have no say in this.

    How useful is conditioning as an explanation of learning?

    Strengths. Conditioning theories make clear predictions that can be tested with observable behaviour, so research on them is easy to measure and repeat. They explain a wide range of everyday learning, from emotional reactions to habits. They also have practical uses, such as token economies, the Good Behaviour Game and step-by-step treatments for fears.

    Limitations. Conditioning leaves out thinking. People also learn by watching others and by expecting consequences that they have never experienced themselves. Social learning theory explains this kind of learning. Much of the early research used animals, and it is not certain that a rat in a box learns in the same way as a student in a classroom. Some associations seem to be learned far more easily than others, for example linking a food with feeling ill after a single experience, which suggests that biology prepares us to learn some things more than others. In applied programmes, behaviour may stop when the rewards stop, and rewarding people for something they already enjoy may reduce their interest in doing it for its own sake. Punishment can stop a behaviour without teaching a better one, and it raises ethical concerns.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to conditioning
    MeasurementBehaviourists measured only what they could observe, such as drops of saliva, lever presses or minutes out of seat. Fear in Little Albert was judged by the researchers watching him.
    CausalityBarrish et al. switched the game on and off in different lessons, which helps to show cause and effect. Watson and Rayner had one child and no control condition.
    BiasTextbooks have repeatedly exaggerated the Little Albert findings, which shows how a famous study can be distorted as it is retold.
    ChangeConditioning is a theory of change: behaviour is learned, and it can be weakened through extinction or changed through new consequences.
    PerspectiveConditioning is a behaviourist perspective. It focuses on the environment and says little about thoughts, culture or biology.
    ResponsibilityResearchers who caused fear in a baby, and teachers or designers who use rewards and penalties to change behaviour, must consider consent, harm and whose goals are being served.
