On this page

    Aim

    Find the research your essay will stand on

    A psychology EE is a review of published, peer-reviewed research. You are not allowed to run your own experiments, interviews or surveys, so the quality of your essay depends on the quality of what you find.

    By the end of this stage you can:

    • tell which kinds of source can carry your argument and which can only point you to one
    • build a search string and run it in at least two databases
    • use citation chaining to find older and newer studies on the same question
    • keep a search log that becomes the method paragraph in your introduction
    Our example question on every EE pageTo what extent does taking notes by hand rather than on a laptop improve students' learning from lectures?

    Know your sources

    What counts as a source

    Not every source does the same job. The IB guidance is clear that reading abstracts alone is not enough: you need to read the studies themselves, especially the method and the discussion.

    SourceUse it toCan it carry your argument?
    Original peer-reviewed studiesGive the evidence: what was done, to whom, and what was foundYes. These are your core sources.
    Meta-analyses and systematic reviewsSee the overall pattern across many studies, and how big an effect isYes, and they help you find the original studies
    Textbooks and this siteUnderstand an area and its key terms before you searchNo. Use them to learn, then go to the studies
    Preprints (for example PsyArXiv)See very recent workOnly with care: they are not yet peer reviewed, so say so
    News stories, blogs, videosFind out that a study existsNo. Find and read the original paper
    Rule of thumb

    Most of your core evidence should come from the last 15 to 20 years. Older classic studies are fine for context and theory. Eight to fifteen core studies is a sensible range, but this is advice, not an IB rule.

    Paywall?

    Ask your librarian, look for the author's own copy on their university page, or email the author politely. Researchers are usually happy to share. Do not use websites that copy paywalled papers illegally.

    Search like a researcher

    Build a search string

    Split your question into its main ideas, list other words for each idea, then join them with a few simple operators.

    &

    AND narrows

    Every result must contain both ideas: laptop AND memory.

    |

    OR widens

    Any of the words will do. Use it for other words that mean the same idea: (recall OR memory OR learning).

    “

    Quotation marks keep a phrase together

    "note taking" finds the phrase, not pages that mention notes and taking separately.

    *

    An asterisk catches word endings

    replicat* finds replicate, replication and replicated. Most library databases accept this; Google Scholar ignores it.

    ( )

    Brackets group the synonyms

    Keep each idea's OR list inside brackets so the database reads it as one idea.

    For our example question:

    (laptop OR typing OR typed) AND ("note taking" OR "note-taking" OR notetaking) AND (recall OR "test performance" OR learning)
    Search for the other side on purpose

    Run the same search again with AND (replicat* OR meta-analysis OR "no difference") added. Evidence that challenges your argument is as important as evidence that supports it, and it rarely turns up on the first page by itself.

    Build and run strings quickly with the search-string builder.

    Follow the trail

    Citation chaining

    One good paper leads to many others. Chaining is often faster and more precise than keyword searching.

    Backward: older work

    Read the reference list of a strong paper. It shows you the studies and theories that paper was built on.

    Forward: newer work

    In Google Scholar, click "Cited by" under a paper. It shows you later studies that used, tested or challenged it, including failed replications.

    Forward chaining matters most for famous studies. For our example question, the famous 2014 laptop study has been cited more than a thousand times, and some of those later papers tried to repeat it and could not. You only find that out by looking forward.

    A routine that works

    Six steps, then stop

    1. Read one recent review article on your topic to get the map of the field and the key terms researchers use.
    2. Build two or three search strings from those key terms and run them in at least two databases.
    3. Skim titles and abstracts to decide what to open. Log every search as you go.
    4. Chain backward and forward from the two or three strongest papers.
    5. Save the full text and the DOI straight away. Finding a paper twice wastes an evening.
    6. Stop when the same studies keep appearing. That is a good sign you have found the core of the field.
    Keep a search log

    Your log becomes your method

    For a literature review, the "research methods" part of Criterion A is how you searched and selected your studies. A search log gives you that paragraph almost for free. Record the date, where you searched, the exact string, roughly how many results, and how many you kept.

    Weak method paragraph

    "I searched online for studies about laptops and memory and chose the most relevant ones."

    Strong method paragraph

    "I searched Google Scholar and a library database using three groups of terms (note-taking medium, note-taking, learning outcomes). I included peer-reviewed experiments and meta-analyses that compared handwritten and typed notes and measured test performance, and excluded studies of reading on screens. Forward citation searching from Mueller and Oppenheimer (2014) added two replications."

    The strong version says where, how, what was included and excluded, and why. A reader could repeat it.

    Keep your log in the search log tool or in your researcher's reflection space.

    Be a sceptic

    Spot a doubtful journal

    Some "journals" publish almost anything for a fee. No single sign proves a journal is doubtful, but several together should worry you.

    Warning signs

    • Peer review promised in days, not weeks
    • Invitations to publish sent by unrequested email
    • A scope covering many unrelated fields
    • Editors with no named universities
    • No DOIs, no ISSN, no received and accepted dates
    • Claims of an "impact factor" from an unknown website

    Good signs

    • Indexed in PubMed, PsycInfo or Scopus
    • Listed in DOAJ if it is open access
    • A named editorial board at real institutions
    • A clear peer-review and ethics policy
    • DOIs on every article
    • Published corrections when mistakes are found

    Charging authors a fee to publish open access is normal for many good journals. It is only a warning sign when other signs come with it.

    • Ask to see the search log at the second supervision meeting. A thin log usually means a thin literature base.

    Exercise 1 · 20 minutes

    Search sprint

    Use our example question, or your own if you already have one.

    1. Split the question into two to four main ideas. List at least two other words for each idea.
    2. Build three search strings: a simple one, a full one with OR groups, and a "challenge" one that looks for replications or meta-analyses.
    3. Run them in at least two different databases.
    4. Log every search: date, where, the exact string, roughly how many results, how many you kept, and why you changed the string next.
    5. Finish with a list of the studies you would read in full.

    Citation chaining

    1. Backward. Open Morehead, Dunlosky and Rawson (2019) and find three older studies or reviews in its reference list that look useful for the example question. Note what each one is about.
    2. Forward. Search Google Scholar for Mueller and Oppenheimer (2014) and click "Cited by". Find three newer studies that tested or reviewed its finding. Note what each one found.
    3. Write two sentences: what did chaining show you that a keyword search might have missed?

    Exercise 3 · 10 minutes

    Spot the risky journal

    Four journals, all invented for this exercise. For each, decide: fine to use, be careful, or avoid. Give two reasons.

    Journal A

    You received an email inviting you to publish. It promises peer review within 72 hours and asks for a fee before review. It publishes psychology, engineering and agriculture. The editors are listed by name only, with no universities. There is no ISSN.

    Journal B

    Indexed in PsycInfo and Scopus. The editorial board lists professors at named universities. Its website explains its peer-review process, which takes about three months. Every article has a DOI, and it has published several corrections.

    Journal C

    Open access and listed in DOAJ. Its website states the fee authors pay if their article is accepted. Peer review takes 8 to 12 weeks. Articles include a statement about where the data can be found.

    Journal D

    A very professional website. The article you want has no DOI and no received or accepted dates. The home page quotes an "impact factor" from a website you have never heard of. It says it is open access, but you cannot find it in DOAJ.

    Check yourself

    Five quick questions

    Why is reading the abstract not enough?

    An abstract leaves out the details you need to judge and use a study: who took part, how the variables were measured, and the authors' own limitations. The IB guidance says abstracts alone cannot support an EE.

    What is the difference between AND and OR in a search?

    AND narrows: results must contain both ideas. OR widens: results can contain any of the words, so you use it for synonyms of one idea.

    What does forward chaining find that backward chaining cannot?

    Newer work that cites the paper, including replications, critiques and meta-analyses. Backward chaining only finds older work the paper was built on.

    Can you use a preprint as a core source?

    Only with care. A preprint has not been peer reviewed yet. If you use one, say so in your essay and treat its findings as less certain.

    Why keep a search log?

    It becomes the method paragraph of your introduction (part of Criterion A), it stops you repeating searches, and it shows your supervisor how you built your literature base.
