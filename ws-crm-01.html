    What is psychology?

    Psychology is the scientific study of behaviour and mental processes. Behaviour is what people do and say, which others can observe. Mental processes are what goes on inside: memory, attention, perception, emotion, thinking and decision-making. These cannot be seen directly, so psychologists have to find ways to study them through behaviour, through what people report, or through measures of the body and brain.

    Psychology counts as a science because it is empirical: claims are tested against evidence that is collected systematically, through observation and research, rather than accepted because they sound right, because they are traditional, or because someone important said so. The aims of psychological research are usually described in four steps: to describe behaviour, to explain it, to predict it and, where it helps people, to change it.

    Why not just use common sense? Because common sense often contradicts itself ("birds of a feather flock together", but "opposites attract") and because our intuitions are easily fooled. The warm-up showed one way this happens: a vague, flattering description feels personal even when it is not. Research gives us ways to check our intuitions, and the six concepts in this chapter give us ways to check the research.

    Three rules about concepts are worth knowing from the start:

    • Exam questions use only the concept word. A question will say "causality", never a related term such as "reductionism". The related terms in this chapter are there to help your thinking.
    • Any idea can link to any concept, if you explain the link. Reductionism, for example, can be argued as a problem of bias, of causality or of perspective. What earns marks is the explanation of the link.
    • Concepts are not about memorising study details. Studies are useful evidence, and one well-used study is plenty, but the exam rewards how well you think about them, not how many dates and sample sizes you remember.

    Bias

    The question it asks: in what ways might our knowledge of behaviour be distorted?

    A bias is a systematic error: something that pushes thinking or findings away from the truth in one particular direction. Compare two faulty kitchen scales. One gives slightly different readings each time you weigh the same bag of rice, sometimes a little high and sometimes a little low; that is random error, and it tends to cancel out over many measurements. The other always reads 200 grams too heavy; that is bias, and weighing the bag a hundred times will not fix it. Bias is dangerous precisely because repeating the research does not remove it.

    Bias shows up in three places.

    • In research. Researcher bias: the researcher's expectations shape how data are collected or interpreted. Participant bias: participants change their behaviour because they are being studied, for example by guessing the aim (demand characteristics) or giving answers that make them look good (social desirability). Sampling bias: the people studied do not represent the people the conclusion is about. Confirmation bias: noticing evidence that fits what you already believe and overlooking evidence that does not. Publication bias: studies with striking results are more likely to be published than studies that found nothing, so the published picture can look stronger than the truth.
    • In theories. A theory can lean too heavily towards one kind of explanation, for example explaining all behaviour through genes (biological determinism) or through upbringing (environmental determinism), or reducing a complex behaviour to one simple cause (reductionism). Gender and cultural bias can also affect how disorders are diagnosed and counted.
    • In everyday behaviour. People take mental shortcuts (heuristics) when time, information or motivation is short. These shortcuts usually work, but they also produce systematic errors called cognitive biases, such as anchoring on the first number you hear.

    Researchers use controls to reduce bias: random sampling, keeping the aim hidden until the debrief, having two observers check each other (inter-rater reliability), and reflecting on their own assumptions (reflexivity). Spotting a bias is only the first step. The skill that earns marks is explaining how the bias could change the findings or the conclusion.

    Causality

    The question it asks: how can we know what causes a behaviour?

    Causality is the relationship in which one variable produces a change in another. To claim that X causes Y, a researcher needs three things: X and Y must change together; X must come before Y; and other possible causes must be ruled out. The research method designed to do this is the experiment, where the researcher changes one variable (the independent variable), keeps everything else as constant as possible, and measures the effect on another (the dependent variable). You will study experiments in CRM-03.

    Much research in psychology cannot do this. It measures two things and finds that they go together, which is a correlation. A correlation alone does not show cause, for two reasons:

    • Bidirectional ambiguity: the direction is unclear. Students who sleep more get better marks. Does sleep improve learning, or do students who are on top of their work sleep better because they are less worried?
    • A third variable: something else may cause both. Countries that eat more fish report lower rates of depression, but wealth, climate, diet as a whole and the way depression is diagnosed all differ between those countries too.

    Human behaviour is also complex. Often there is no single cause but several factors that interact: a gene may raise risk only in a stressful environment. Good answers about causality ask how strong the evidence for a cause is, what has been controlled (for example with a placebo, a double-blind design or a waiting-list group), and whether a cause found in a laboratory would still operate in everyday life. That last point is about internal validity (did the study show what caused what, inside the study?) versus external validity (does the finding hold outside it?).

    A note on statistical significance, because it is often misunderstood. A result with p < .05 means that if there were really no effect, results this strong would turn up less than 5% of the time. It does not mean there is a 95% chance the hypothesis is true, and it does not tell you how large or important the effect is.

    Change

    The question it asks: how and why does behaviour change, and can we change it on purpose?

    Change is about how behaviour, mental processes and the brain shift over time. It comes in two main kinds in this course.

    • Change that happens naturally: development and maturation, such as a child's language growing, the brain reorganising after experience (neuroplasticity), or a person adapting to a new culture.
    • Change that is the result of an intervention: a treatment for a disorder, a programme to prevent a health problem, a strategy to improve memory, or a technique to persuade people.

    Change can be gradual or sudden, planned or unplanned, lasting or short-lived. The concept also raises one of psychology's oldest debates: how far do we freely choose our behaviour (free will, sometimes called agency), and how far is it shaped by biology and environment (determinism)? Psychologists also study barriers to change, the reasons people keep doing something even when they want to stop.

    Studying change needs the right design. A longitudinal study follows the same people over months or years; a repeated measures design tests the same people before and after an intervention. Change can also be measured across whole populations, for example how the prevalence of a health problem rises or falls over time or differs between countries. A useful question for any finding: is this change the result of the intervention, or would it have happened anyway?

    Measurement

    The question it asks: how can we turn complex mental processes into something we can observe and record?

    Many things psychologists study, such as intelligence, stress, memory or loneliness, are constructs: ideas that cannot be seen directly. To study them, researchers must operationalise them, which means stating exactly how they will be measured. "Memory" is a construct; "the number of words recalled from a list of 20 after a 30-second delay" is an operationalised variable. Every choice of operationalisation includes some parts of the construct and leaves others out.

    Two qualities make a measurement trustworthy:

    • Reliability: it gives consistent results, for example when the test is repeated or when two observers score the same behaviour.
    • Validity: it measures what it claims to measure. A test can be reliable without being valid: a scale that is always 200 grams heavy is perfectly consistent and always wrong.

    Measurements can be direct (counting how often a child shares a toy) or indirect (inferring brain activity from changes in blood flow in a brain scan, or inferring stress from a hormone level). Data can be quantitative (numbers) or qualitative (words and meanings), and they can be self-reported, observed or recorded by instruments. Each kind has strengths and weaknesses, which is why researchers often combine methods, known as triangulation, to check that different measures point the same way. Measurement is not only about numbers: deciding how to code interview transcripts into themes is a measurement decision too.

    Perspective

    The question it asks: what different ways are there of explaining the same behaviour?

    A perspective is a coherent way of looking at behaviour, built on its own assumptions about what matters and how to study it. The course uses three broad approaches:

    ApproachWhat it focuses onTypical evidence
    BiologicalThe brain, nervous system, hormones and genesBrain imaging, twin studies, animal research
    CognitiveMental processes such as memory, thinking and perceptionExperiments on memory and decision-making, cognitive models
    SocioculturalOther people, groups and cultureObservations, interviews, cross-cultural comparisons

    No single approach explains behaviour fully on its own. Take a student who becomes very anxious before exams. A biological explanation might point to the body's stress response; a cognitive one to the way the student interprets the exam ("if I fail, everything is ruined"); a sociocultural one to family expectations and comparison with classmates. Each is coherent, each captures something real, and together they give a fuller picture than any one alone.

    Perspective also covers different theories and models of the same behaviour, alternative explanations of the same finding, inductive research (starting from the data and building a theory) versus deductive research (starting from a theory and testing it), and emic versus etic approaches: studying a culture from the inside, in its own terms, versus applying the same measures across many cultures to compare them. It also includes the perspectives of Indigenous peoples, whose understandings of mind and well-being may differ from those built into most published research.

    Responsibility

    The question it asks: what do psychologists owe to the people they study and to society?

    Psychologists study people, and their findings shape treatments, school policies and public opinion. That gives them power, and responsibility is about using it well: during research, when reporting results, and when results are applied.

    During research, ethical guidelines protect participants: informed consent; the right to withdraw, including withdrawing their data; anonymity and confidentiality; protection from harm; avoiding deception unless it is essential and harmless; and a full debrief afterwards. Researchers weigh costs against benefits: is the knowledge gained worth any discomfort caused? Extra care is needed with children, with research in public places where people are observed without being asked, and with animals, where researchers try to replace animals with alternatives, reduce the numbers used and refine procedures to minimise harm.

    Ethics can also affect the results. Asking for informed consent may tell participants enough to change how they behave. The right to withdraw may mean the people who stay are different from those who leave. Anonymity can make people more willing to answer honestly.

    After the research, responsibility continues: reporting findings honestly, including their uncertainty; thinking about how a finding on a sensitive topic might be used, for example to stigmatise a group; and, where research shows something could help people, sharing it with those who make decisions.

    Perspective is not bias, and concepts connect

    This is one of the most common mistakes in exam answers, so it gets its own section. A perspective is a coherent framework that can be partly right: a biological and a sociocultural explanation of anxiety can both be valid. A bias is a distortion that pushes findings away from the truth. Calling a perspective "biased" because it only looks at one kind of cause confuses the two. The accurate point is usually that relying on one perspective alone can lead to a reductionist explanation, which you can then argue as a limitation.

    The concepts also connect, and showing a connection is a sign of strong thinking. Some examples:

    • Bias and perspective: a sample drawn from one culture limits how far a finding can claim to show something universal.
    • Measurement and causality: if the measure of the outcome is not valid, an experiment cannot show that the treatment caused a real change.
    • Responsibility and bias: letting participants withdraw is the right thing to do, and it can also leave a biased sample.
    • Change and causality: people improve after a treatment, but would they have improved anyway?

    Key study: Forer (1949), the fallacy of personal validation

    AimTo show how readily people accept a vague, general personality description as an accurate account of themselves.
    Participants39 students in Bertram Forer's introductory psychology class in the USA.
    ProcedureStudents completed a personality test and were told that each of them would receive a personal description based on their answers. A week later every student was given the same description, made of 13 general statements that Forer had taken largely from a newsstand astrology book. Students rated how well it described them on a scale from 0 (poor) to 5 (perfect). Only after the ratings were collected did Forer reveal that the descriptions were identical, and he used this to discuss the problem with the class.
    FindingsThe mean accuracy rating was 4.26 out of 5. Most students gave a 4 or 5, and nobody gave lower than 2.
    ConclusionPeople tend to accept vague, generally positive statements as personally meaningful, especially when they believe the statements come from an expert or a test about them. Forer called this the fallacy of personal validation. It is now usually called the Barnum effect, a name suggested by Paul Meehl in 1956.
    What happened nextThe effect has been found many times since, which matters for anyone who uses personality tests: a client saying a description "fits" is not evidence that the test works.
    Use it forPractising concepts (this chapter); Paper 2 Section B (evaluating a study with concepts); a link to cognitive biases in CL-05.
    Evaluate it withBias: participants wanted the result to be about them, and they rated a description given by their own teacher, which may have raised scores. Measurement: a single 0 to 5 rating of "accuracy" is a simple measure of a complex judgement. Responsibility: students were deceived; the deception was mild and was explained in the same lesson.

    Each scenario below connects most directly to one concept. For each, name the concept and explain the link in one sentence. Use a detail from the scenario, not a definition.

    1. A sports psychologist tests whether motivational coaching improves performance, using players from the team he coaches. Two coached players score below average, so he leaves their results out because he thinks they were unwell on the day.
    2. A survey finds that countries where people eat more fish report lower rates of depression. A magazine headline says: "Eating fish prevents depression."
