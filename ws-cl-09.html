    What counts as an environmental factor?

    Memory has three stages: encoding (getting information in), storage (keeping it) and retrieval (getting it back out). This chapter asks how much the world around us shapes what we remember.

    An environmental factor is anything outside the material itself that is present when we learn or when we try to remember. External factors are in the surroundings: the room, sounds, smells and lighting. Internal factors are the state of the person, such as mood or tiredness. The evidence for each is very different in strength.

    The learning objective says "potential". Your job is not to prove that the environment always changes memory, but to explain how it could and judge how strong the evidence is.

    The encoding specificity principle

    Tulving and Thomson (1973) proposed the encoding specificity principle. It states that memory works best when the cues present at retrieval match the cues that were present at encoding. When you learn something, you also store details of the situation, often without noticing. Later, those details can act as retrieval cues that help you find the memory.

    This explains the warm-up. When Omar decided to fetch the charger, he was at his desk, looking at his laptop. The intention was encoded together with that setting. In the kitchen, none of those cues were present, so the memory was there but he could not reach it. Back at the desk, the cues matched again and the memory returned. Psychologists call this cue-dependent forgetting: the memory is available (it is stored) but not accessible (it cannot be retrieved without the right cue).

    Encoding specificity as a cognitive model

    A cognitive model is a simplified description of how a mental process works. It breaks the process into parts or stages and shows how information moves between them, often as boxes and arrows. Nobody can watch memory directly, so researchers change the input, record the output, and infer what happens in between.

    Encoding specificity can be drawn as a simple model with three steps:

    1. Encoding: the material is stored together with cues from the setting and from the person's state.
    2. Retrieval attempt: the cues present now are compared with the cues stored with the memory.
    3. Output: the better the match, the more likely the memory is to be recalled.

    The model predicts that people remember more when tested in the context in which they learned, which is what Godden and Baddeley tested. Notice that the "match" is never observed. It is inferred from how much people recall. Keep this in mind for the Concept Workout.

    Context-dependent and state-dependent memory

    Context-dependent memory is the finding that recall is better when the external environment at retrieval matches the environment at encoding. The context is the place and its features: a room, the sounds in it, even the smell of it.

    State-dependent memory is the finding that recall is better when a person's internal state at retrieval matches their state at encoding. Classic studies changed physical state with substances, and are not covered here. A version you can discuss is mood-dependent memory: something learned while calm might be recalled best when calm again. However, this effect is harder to find. Eich (1995) reviewed the research and described mood-dependent memory as controversial, because it appears in some studies and not in others. He suggested it is more likely when the mood is strong and real.

    When does context matter less?

    Smith and Vela (2001) combined the results of many studies of environmental context-dependent memory in a meta-analysis. Overall, the effect was reliable: memory tended to be better when learning and testing happened in the same environment. But they also found conditions that made the effect smaller:

    • Mental reinstatement. When people imagined the original learning room before the test, the effect of changing rooms shrank. You do not always need to go back to the place. Picturing it can work.
    • Stronger cues. When other cues, not linked to the room, were present at learning or at testing, the room mattered less. If the material itself gives you plenty of cues, you do not need the setting.

    The type of test also matters. Context effects are much larger in recall (producing the answer) than in recognition (picking it from options). Godden and Baddeley (1980) found that changing the environment made little difference when divers were tested with recognition. A multiple-choice question gives you cues; an essay question does not.

    Key study 1: Godden and Baddeley (1975), context-dependent memory in divers

    AimTo test whether recall is better when the environment at recall matches the environment at learning, using two very different natural environments: on land and underwater.
    Participants18 members of a university diving club (13 men and 5 women).
    ProcedureA field experiment in open water off the coast of Scotland, with a repeated measures design. Each diver heard lists of 36 unrelated two- and three-syllable words, played twice through a recording. Divers learned a list either sitting at the water's edge (dry) or about 20 feet (6 metres) underwater (wet). After a short delay filled with copying numbers, they had 2 minutes to write down as many words as they could, in any order (free recall), either in the same environment or in the other one. Every diver completed all four conditions (dry-dry, dry-wet, wet-wet, wet-dry), one per session, about a day apart. The order of conditions and lists was counterbalanced.
    FindingsWords learned on land: a mean of 13.5 recalled on land, and 8.6 recalled underwater. Words learned underwater: a mean of 11.4 recalled underwater, and 8.4 recalled on land. Recall was higher when the two environments matched. A second experiment checked whether the disruption of moving between land and water could explain this. Divers who dived between learning and recall on land recalled about the same as divers who stayed on land, so disruption did not explain the effect.
    ConclusionThe environment at learning becomes a retrieval cue. Returning to it helps recall, which supports the encoding specificity principle and context-dependent memory.
    What happened nextMurre (2021) repeated the experiment with 16 divers in Amsterdam. This time the divers learned and recalled in a shallow, heated indoor pool, and all testing happened on one day. Recall in the same environment was not better than recall in the other environment, so the original effect was not replicated. Murre pointed to differences between the studies and argued that more replications are needed.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example for cognitive models); Paper 1 Section B; Paper 1 Section C on measurement, causality or bias.
    Evaluate it withCausality: the design is a true experiment with counterbalancing, and the second experiment ruled out one alternative explanation, so a causal claim about context is reasonable for this sample. Measurement: memory was measured as free recall of unrelated words after a few minutes. That is very different from recalling a biology answer weeks later. Bias: 18 experienced divers is a small and unusual sample, and famous results from small studies can be repeated in textbooks long before anyone tests them again.

    Environmental noise and learning

    Noise works differently from context: it acts during learning and may make encoding harder. Psychologists separate acute noise (a short burst, such as a lorry passing) from chronic noise (exposure over months or years, such as a school under a flight path).

    Researchers have suggested several ways chronic noise could affect learning: it interrupts teaching, makes speech harder to hear (which matters for children learning to read) and causes annoyance. These are possible explanations; the studies below test whether there is a link at all.

    The strongest evidence comes from field studies around airports. Hygge, Evans and Bullinger (2002) used a rare natural event in Munich: an old airport closed and a new one opened. They tested 326 children before and after the switch. Children who became newly exposed to aircraft noise near the new airport showed poorer long-term memory and reading. Children near the old airport, whose noise exposure ended, improved. Measuring the same children before and after the change gives better evidence about cause than a single snapshot, such as RANCH.

    Key study 2: Stansfeld et al. (2005), the RANCH study

    AimTo investigate whether long-term exposure to aircraft noise and road traffic noise at school is linked to children's reading comprehension, memory, attention and health. RANCH stands for Road traffic and Aircraft Noise exposure and children's Cognition and Health.
    Participants2,844 children aged 9 to 10, from 89 primary schools around three major airports: Heathrow (UK), Schiphol (the Netherlands) and Barajas (Spain).
    ProcedureA cross-national, cross-sectional study: each child was tested once. Schools were chosen to cover a range of aircraft and road traffic noise levels, and schools were matched within each country for socioeconomic status. The noise level outside each school was estimated from noise maps, models or direct measurements, depending on the country. Children completed standardised reading tests, memory tasks (recall and recognition of stories heard on a CD), a working memory task, an attention task and questionnaires. Parents reported on family background so this could be taken into account.
    FindingsAircraft noise: the more aircraft noise at a school, the lower the children's reading comprehension and recognition memory scores. The link was a straight-line (linear) one, and it remained after taking socioeconomic factors into account. A 5 dB increase in aircraft noise was equivalent to a reading delay of about 2 months in the UK and about 1 month in the Netherlands. Road traffic noise: no link with reading comprehension or recognition memory. Unexpectedly, more road traffic noise was linked with higher scores on episodic memory (story recall). No link for either type of noise with sustained attention, working memory or children's self-reported health. Both types of noise were linked with more annoyance.
    ConclusionChronic aircraft noise at school may impair children's reading comprehension. The authors described this as a small effect and suggested, but did not claim to prove, that the relationship is causal. Noise did not harm every kind of memory: the effects were specific.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section B (noise and learning); Paper 1 Section C on causality or measurement; Paper 2 Section B practice (a large cross-sectional study).
    Evaluate it withCausality: each child was tested once, so the study shows a link, not a cause. Families who live near airports may differ in ways the researchers did not measure. The Munich study, which followed children before and after an airport moved, makes a causal explanation more convincing. Measurement: noise was estimated outside the school, not inside each classroom or at home, and different countries used different noise maps and different reading tests. Bias: it is easy to report only "noise harms memory". An accurate summary includes the null results and the surprising road traffic finding.

    Other environmental factors you will recognise (weaker evidence)

    Two factors come up in every conversation about studying. The evidence is weaker, so say so if you use them in an exam.

    Phones on the desk. Evidence: mixed. Ward et al. (2017) asked university students to leave their phone on the desk, in a pocket or bag, or in another room. Students whose phone was in another room scored highest on a working memory task, and those with the phone on the desk scored lowest. However, Ruiz Pardo and Minda (2022) ran a pre-registered direct replication with the same tasks and found no difference between phone locations. The claim is not settled.

    Sleep and the bedroom environment. Evidence: strong for sleep, weaker for bedroom factors. Many laboratory studies show that sleep after learning helps memories become more stable (consolidation). The link to the bedroom environment is less direct. Screens and light at night may delay sleep, and less sleep may then harm memory, but most evidence for this chain comes from correlational studies, and many other things differ between late screen users and others.

    Real-life examples

    • Revising for the mock exams. Mariam revises every evening at the same desk at home in Abu Dhabi, then sits her exams in a large sports hall. Encoding specificity predicts a small cost from the change. Smith and Vela suggest a fix: picture the desk before starting.
    • Schools near busy roads and flight paths. Some schools in large cities, including in the UAE, are close to busy highways or flight paths. RANCH suggests that where noise is very high, reading may suffer a little over time, which is one reason architects use double glazing and sound insulation in classrooms. But check what RANCH found for road traffic before making claims about highways.
    • Driving lessons. Hamdan practises with an instructor on quiet streets in Al Ain. On test day, the busy route and the examiner in the passenger seat are new cues. Practising in varied settings makes his skills less tied to one context.

    How useful is research on environmental influences on memory?

    Strengths. Encoding specificity makes clear, testable predictions and explains everyday experiences such as forgetting why you entered a room. Smith and Vela (2001) found context effects reliable across many studies and showed when they shrink, which is useful advice for students. Field studies such as RANCH and the Munich study tested large samples of children in their own schools, so the findings can inform where schools are built and how classrooms are insulated.

    Limitations. The best-known context study did not replicate when repeated in a pool (Murre, 2021), and context effects are much weaker for recognition than for recall, so the effect may matter less in real exams than textbooks suggest. Much of the research uses unrelated words recalled after minutes, not meaningful material recalled after weeks. Noise research is often cross-sectional, so it cannot show cause. Popular claims about phones run ahead of the evidence.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to environmental influences on memory
    MeasurementMemory is inferred from recall or recognition scores, and the type of test changes the result. Noise is often estimated from maps outside the school rather than measured in the classroom.
    CausalityGodden and Baddeley used an experiment to test cause; RANCH tested each child once, so it shows a link, not a cause. The Munich before-and-after study strengthens the causal case for noise.
    BiasSmall, striking studies (the divers, the phone study) are more likely to be published and repeated before they are checked. Reports of RANCH often leave out the null findings.
    ChangeChildren near Munich's old airport improved once the noise stopped, which suggests the effects of noise on learning can change when the environment changes.
    PerspectiveEncoding specificity is a cognitive explanation. A sociocultural perspective would ask why some families live near airports and what else differs for those children.
    ResponsibilityIf noise may harm learning, governments, airports and school planners share responsibility for where schools are built and how they are insulated. Psychologists must report small effects as small.
