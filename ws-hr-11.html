    Part A: Surveys, a recap

    What is a survey?

    A survey collects self-report data: people answer questions about their own attitudes, opinions, experiences or behaviour. Strictly, the survey is the whole process of collecting the answers, and the questionnaire is the list of questions. The IB uses the two words interchangeably, so you can use either in the exam.

    Surveys are the method of choice when you want to know what many people think, quickly and cheaply. You have already met a very large one: Hofstede's values questionnaire, answered by more than 100,000 IBM employees (HR-04).

    Two limits are worth learning now. A survey records what people say, not what they do. And a survey changes nothing: it measures things as they already are, so on its own it can describe patterns and relationships but cannot test cause and effect.

    Closed and open questions

    Closed questionsOpen questions
    What they look likeFixed choices: yes or no, tick boxes, a rating from 1 to 5Participants answer in their own words
    What you getQuantitative data that are quick to count and compareQualitative data with reasons and detail
    StrengthEasy to analyse, and the same for everyone (standardised)Can show ideas the researcher did not think of
    LimitationCan only find what the researcher thought to askSlower to analyse, and the researcher has to interpret each answer

    Many questionnaires mix the two: closed items for numbers that can be compared, then one or two open items at the end to explain them.

    Likert scales and ordinal data

    A Likert scale asks people to rate agreement or importance on a fixed scale, for example from 1 (not at all important) to 5 (extremely important). Labelling every point, not just the ends, helps everyone read the scale the same way.

    The numbers on a Likert scale have an order, so the data are ordinal. But nothing guarantees that the gap between 4 and 5 means the same as the gap between 1 and 2, and one person's 4 may be another person's 5. That is why Likert data are usually summarised with the median (the middle rating) and the mode (the most common rating), or with the percentage of people who chose the top points. A mean can still be worked out, but a few very low or very high ratings pull it around, and it treats the gaps as if they were equal.

    Writing good items

    • One idea per item. "Funny and supportive" is two qualities, so an answer cannot tell you which one the person was rating (a double-barrelled item).
    • No hint about the answer you want. "Don't you agree..." pushes people towards yes (a leading item).
    • Plain, specific words. A term like "emotional intelligence" means different things to different people. Describe the behaviour instead, so the item is operationalised.
    • Clear, complete choices. "Often" and "sometimes" are read differently by different people; give choices with clear meanings, and make sure every answer has somewhere to go.
    • Sensitive or personal items last, and optional. Early personal questions can put people off or change how they answer everything after them.
    • Short. Tired participants rush, and rushed answers are less accurate.

    Response biases

    BiasWhat happensOne way to reduce it
    Social desirability biasPeople give the answer that makes them look good, not the true oneAnonymity; the researcher steps away while people answer
    Acquiescence biasPeople tend to agree, or rate everything highly, whatever the item saysClear, specific wording; some items that need real thought
    Central tendency biasPeople avoid the ends of the scale and keep choosing the middleA label on every point of the scale
    Order effectsEarlier items colour answers to later ones, or people tire towards the endShuffle the order of items; keep it short

    Sampling for surveys

    The guide sets a minimum of 10 participants for a survey class practical. Who those participants are matters as much as how many.

    TechniqueHow participants are chosenWatch for
    OpportunityWhoever is available and willing at the timeResearchers tend to ask people like themselves, often friends
    Self-selectedPeople volunteer, for example by answering an online linkThose who choose to respond may care more about the topic
    RandomEveryone in the target population has an equal chance of being chosenNeeds a full list of the population, which is rarely available
    StratifiedThe sample matches the population's make-up, for example the same share from each gradeNeeds to know the population's make-up in advance
    SnowballParticipants recruit others they knowParticipants end up similar to each other

    Cross-sectional and longitudinal surveys

    A cross-sectional survey compares different groups at one point in time, like a snapshot: for example, Grade 11 and Grade 12 students answering in the same week. It is quick, but the groups differ in more ways than their grade, so a difference between them cannot be read as change.

    A longitudinal survey asks the same people the same questions more than once, for example in Grade 11 and again in Grade 12. It can show change within people, but it takes much longer, and some participants drop out along the way.

    Ethics in surveys

    The guide notes that surveys raise fewer ethical issues than many methods, but consent must still be given before anyone answers.

    • Explicit informed consent: participants read what the survey is about and tick to agree. Simply filling it in does not count as consent.
    • Age: participants under 16 need written consent from a parent or guardian.
    • Anonymity: no names, and no details that together could identify someone in a small sample.
    • The right to withdraw: stop at any time, skip optional items, and have answers removed afterwards. With anonymous data, a made-up code is one way to make this possible.
    • No distressing questions, and data stored privately, used only for the study and deleted when it is finished.

    How useful are surveys?

    StrengthsLimitations
    Reach many people quickly and cheaplySelf-report: what people say may not match what they do
    Standardised: everyone answers the same items, so groups can be comparedResponse biases such as social desirability and acquiescence
    Anonymity can make honest answers easierCannot follow up an interesting answer, unlike an interview
    Can measure attitudes and values, which cannot be observed directlyClosed items only find what the researcher thought to ask
    Easy to repeat with another groupNo IV, so patterns only, not causes

    Part B: Our practical

    Our practical: the write-up

    AimTo investigate which qualities Grade 11 and Grade 12 students rate as most important in a close friend, and whether the ratings differ between the two grades.
    Research questionWhich qualities do Grade 11 and Grade 12 students at an international school in the UAE rate as most important in a close friend, and do the two grades rate them differently?
    Method and designA questionnaire on a Google Form. Cross-sectional: two existing groups compared at one point in time. Grade was not manipulated, so this is a survey, not an experiment.
    SamplingOpportunity sampling: each of us surveyed one Grade 11 and one Grade 12 student who was available and agreed. Participants had to be 16 or older (so no parental consent was needed), not take psychology (so they were less likely to guess what we expected) and not be in our class. With the whole class, that gave about 34 participants.
    DataQuantitative: ratings from 1 to 5 and top-three choices. Qualitative: two optional open answers.
    AnalysisMedian, mode and the percentage rating each quality 4 or 5, compared between the grades; top-three choices counted; open answers coded into themes by inductive content analysis.

    Our questionnaire

    PartWhat it askedWhy
    Information and consentWhat the study is about, then a tickbox to agree and a tickbox to confirm age 16 or olderExplicit consent; anyone who said no went straight to the end
    Researcher number and codeThe researcher's number, and a 4-character code the participant made upLets the teacher check each of us reached both grades; the code allows withdrawal while staying anonymous
    About youGrade, and roughly how long they had known their closest friendGrade is the comparison; nothing else that could identify anyone
    What matters in a close friendEight qualities, each with a short description, rated from 1 (not at all important) to 5 (extremely important): loyalty, keeping things private, humour, shared interests, being easy to talk to, honesty, making time, and supporting my goalsThe descriptions make sure everyone rates the same thing; the order was shuffled for each person
    Your top threeChoose exactly three of the eight qualitiesForces a choice, which separates qualities if most ratings are high
    In your own wordsTwo optional open questions: what makes someone a good friend, and something a friend did that made the friendship stronger (no names)Reasons in participants' own words, and a check on what our list missed

    Piloting

    Before collecting any data, we tried the Form on each other, timed it, and noted every word that made us hesitate. A pilot checks the instrument, not the topic: psychology students know the aim, so their answers could not count as data, but they can still find confusing words, missing choices and items that feel too personal. Your workbook records what our pilot changed.

    Procedure, step by step

    1. Each of us was given a researcher number and the Form link.
    2. At break, lunch or after school, we asked a Grade 11 or Grade 12 student, using the same short script, whether they would take part.
    3. If they agreed, we entered our researcher number, handed over the device, and stepped away while they read the information and answered.
    4. When they finished, we thanked them and reminded them that their code lets them withdraw within 7 days.
    5. We repeated this with a student from the other grade, and kept a record of grades and dates with no names.
    6. The teacher closed the Form, removed any responses without consent, and shared an anonymised copy of the data with the class.

    Analysing the results

    For each of the eight qualities, and for each grade, we worked out three numbers: the median, the mode and the percentage who rated it 4 or 5. Percentages are the fair way to compare the grades, because the two groups may not be the same size. We then counted how often each grade chose each quality in its top three, and drew a bar chart with a Grade 11 bar and a Grade 12 bar for each quality.

    The open answers were analysed with a short version of the inductive content analysis from the interview practical (HW-11): read every answer, give each one a short code, group the codes into themes, and count the themes for each grade. The themes are then compared with the ratings: do they agree, and did participants mention anything our eight qualities left out?

    Ethics in our practical

    • A consent tickbox and an age check on the first page; filling in the Form was not treated as consent.
    • No names, emails, gender or nationality, so no answer can be traced to a person.
    • A made-up 4-character code: give it to the researcher or the teacher within 7 days, and the answers are deleted.
    • A neutral topic, with the only personal question optional and asking for no names.
    • Collected at break, lunch or after school, never in someone else's lesson, and no photos of anyone or their screen.
    • The Form responses and the class data are deleted at the end of the practical; we keep only group results.

    How good was our survey?

    What workedWhat limits it
    Every participant rated the same eight described qualities, in a shuffled order, so the grades could be compared fairlyOpportunity sampling: most of us asked people we knew, who may not represent their grade
    Anonymity and stepping away gave participants less reason to answer for showSocial desirability may still push up qualities that sound good, such as loyalty and honesty
    About 34 participants in a few days, well above the minimum of 10Every item pointed the same way, so acquiescence could raise all the ratings; if most ratings are 4 or 5, a ceiling effect hides differences
    The top-three question and the open answers added detail the ratings could not giveCross-sectional: a difference between the grades is not proof that being in Grade 12 causes it

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to our class practical
    Measurement"What you value in a friend" cannot be seen, so each quality was described and rated from 1 to 5. The ratings are ordinal, so we used medians, modes and percentages, and watched for a ceiling effect.
    BiasSampling bias from asking people we knew; social desirability and acquiescence in the answers. Anonymity, stepping away and shuffled items reduce but do not remove them.
    CausalityGrade was an existing group, not a condition we assigned, so the survey can describe a difference between the grades but cannot say what causes it.
    ChangeComparing two grades at one time is a stand-in for change, not a measure of it. A longitudinal survey of the same students would show whether priorities change.
    PerspectiveThe eight qualities were our categories (etic). The open answers came closer to the participants' own view (emic), and could show what our list missed.
    ResponsibilityExplicit consent, an age check, anonymity, a code for withdrawal, and deleting the data at the end.

    Using AI to learn

    Make AI question you, not write for you

    Using AI to learn

    Try one of these after you have attempted the chapter yourself:

    • "Give me eight questionnaire items about friendship, one at a time. Some should be leading, double-barrelled or vague. For each, ask me what is wrong with it, if anything, and wait for my answer before telling me."
    • "Give me a small set of invented 1 to 5 ratings and ask me to find the median, the mode and the mean. Then ask me which one best describes the group and why. Do not give me the answers until I have tried."
    • "Here is my answer to Paper 2 Section A Question 3 comparing a survey with an interview. Tell me which of my points are similarities and which are differences, and ask me two questions that would make the comparison more detailed. Do not rewrite it."

    Remember: check every study an AI tool names against this chapter or your textbook, because these tools sometimes invent studies and figures. Never paste in our class data, a participant's answers or anyone's name, and do not use AI to draft or mark your IA.
