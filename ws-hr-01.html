    What acculturation is

    Models of acculturation

    Acculturation is the process by which people from one culture come into contact with another culture and take on some of its behaviours, norms and values. It usually follows a move from one culture into another, for migration, study or work. What matters is sustained contact with an unfamiliar set of norms, not the distance travelled to get there.

    Not to be confused with enculturation. You met enculturation in Human Development: learning your own culture as you grow up, through instruction and social learning. Acculturation is adjusting to a different culture after you come into contact with it. The two are not opposites. A person can keep learning their heritage culture while adapting to a new one, and Berry's model below allows for exactly that.

    From one line to two dimensions

    Models of acculturation

    Before the 1980s, most researchers treated acculturation as a single line. The more of the new culture a person took on, the more of their heritage culture they were assumed to lose.

    From the 1970s onwards, John Berry developed a model with two separate dimensions (Berry, 1980; summarised in Berry, 1997). First, how receptive is the person to the new culture? Second, how far do they keep links to their culture of origin? Because the two dimensions are independent, a person can be high on both, low on both, or high on one and low on the other. That gives four acculturation strategies.

    StrategyReceptive to the new culture?Keeps links to the culture of origin?Example
    IntegrationYesYesChidinma: Igbo and Nigerian news at home, local friends and clubs at school.
    AssimilationYesNoA person who stops using their first language at home and takes on the host culture fully.
    SeparationNoYesHamda's cousin: close ties to home, and little contact with the host culture.
    MarginalisationNoNoA person who feels connected to neither culture.

    A strategy is not simply a free choice. It also depends on how welcoming the host society is, and on how much choice a person has at all. A refugee resettled at short notice has a different range of options from a student who chose to study abroad.

    Key study 1: Berry, Phinney, Sam and Vedder (2006), immigrant youth in 13 countries

    AimTo find out how adolescents from immigrant families acculturate, and how the way they acculturate is linked to how well they adapt.
    ParticipantsMore than 5,000 adolescents aged 13 to 18 from immigrant families, living in 13 countries (including Canada, Australia, the USA and several European countries), plus a comparison group of young people from the host societies. The project is known as the International Comparative Study of Ethnocultural Youth.
    ProcedureA large questionnaire study. Participants answered structured questions about their attitudes to acculturation, their cultural identity, the languages they used, who their friends were and their family values. Adaptation was measured in two ways: psychological adaptation (for example life satisfaction and self-esteem) and sociocultural adaptation (for example how well they were doing at school). The researchers used a statistical technique called cluster analysis to group young people with similar patterns of answers into profiles.
    FindingsFour profiles appeared, close to Berry's strategies: an integration profile, an ethnic profile (oriented mainly to the heritage culture), a national profile (oriented mainly to the host culture) and a diffuse profile, the closest match to marginalisation. Young people with the integration profile tended to show the best psychological and sociocultural adaptation. Those with the diffuse profile tended to show the poorest.
    ConclusionBeing involved in both the heritage culture and the host culture goes with better adaptation for immigrant youth, across many countries.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section B (supporting Berry's model); Paper 1 Section C on measurement or causality; Paper 2 (an example of a questionnaire study).
    Evaluate it withMeasurement: strategies were inferred from self-report answers grouped into profiles, so the result depends on the questions asked and on where the researchers drew the line between profiles. Causality: strategies were measured as they already were, not assigned, so the study shows a link between integration and adaptation, not that integration causes it. Bias: the 13 countries were mostly Western host societies, so the findings may not describe acculturation in, for example, the Gulf.

    Quick lens: causality. Integration goes with better adaptation. Does integration cause it? Keep these questions open. You will come back to them in the Section C plan.

    • In the study above, was each young person's strategy assigned by the researchers, or measured as it already was?
    • Could the arrow run the other way? Could adolescents who were already coping well find it easier to make friends in a new culture?
    • What else might shape both a person's strategy and their well-being, such as how the host society treats newcomers?

    How acculturation is measured

    Acculturation cannot be seen. A researcher can only record things that might stand for it: what someone eats, which language they use at home, who they spend weekends with, how they describe themselves. There are two main approaches: questionnaires and proxy measures.

    ApproachWhat it recordsWhat it assumes
    One-dimensional questionnaireItems on a single line running from one culture to the other (for example "Spanish only" to "English only", with "both equally" in the middle), as in the original Acculturation Rating Scale for Mexican Americans (ARSMA).Acculturation is a single line. A high score for one culture means a low score for the other.
    Two-dimensional questionnaireMatching items answered separately for each culture, as in the later ARSMA-II.The two dimensions are independent, so a person can score high on both.
    Proxy measureSomething recorded in place of acculturation itself: years of residence, generation (first or second), or the share of life spent in the host country.Time spent in a culture turns into taking on its values.

    Cabassa (2003) compares the two ARSMA scales.

    Notice the pattern in the right-hand column. Every tool is built on a theory, and the theory decides which results are possible. A one-dimensional questionnaire cannot tell integration apart from marginalisation: someone strongly involved in both cultures and someone involved in neither can both score in the middle of the line. Questionnaires are also self-reports, so they depend on what people are willing and able to say about themselves. Proxy measures are quick to collect for very large samples, which is why big health studies often use them.

    Key study 2: Shah et al. (2015), years of residence and health in the UAE

    Many studies of acculturation look at health, and one finding keeps appearing. It is called the immigrant paradox: more acculturation goes with worse health. Most of the studies behind it measured acculturation with a proxy. This study, carried out in Abu Dhabi, is an example.

    AimTo investigate whether acculturation, measured by years of residence, is linked to obesity and other risk factors for heart disease among South Asian migrant men in the UAE.
    ParticipantsA random sample of 1,375 men from India, Pakistan and Bangladesh, recruited at a residency visa health screening centre in Abu Dhabi.
    ProcedureA cross-sectional study: each man was measured once. Years of residence in the UAE was used as a stand-in (proxy) for acculturation. Researchers recorded body measurements, such as waist size, and other health risk factors, and compared men who had lived in the UAE for different lengths of time, adjusting for other factors.
    FindingsCompared with men who had lived in the UAE for one to five years, men who had lived there for six to ten years, and for more than ten years, were more likely to have central obesity (extra fat around the waist).
    ConclusionLonger residence in the host country goes with a higher risk of obesity in this group, which fits the immigrant paradox.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section C on measurement (a proxy measure) or causality; a local example of the immigrant paradox.
    Evaluate it withMeasurement: years of residence records time, not what a person has taken on or kept; a man with fifteen years in Abu Dhabi might still eat, speak and socialise much as he did at home. Causality: a cross-sectional design cannot show that acculturation caused the weight gain; long-term residents differ in other ways, such as type of work and daily routine. Bias: only men from three countries, recruited at one centre, so the findings may not apply to women or to other migrant groups.

    Limitations of Berry's model

    Schwartz et al. (2010) raised three problems with the model.

    • Where "high" ends and "low" begins is a choice. Studies often split at the sample's average, so "low" in one population may not mean the same as "low" in another. That makes comparisons between groups questionable.
    • Marginalisation is rare. Later studies found the marginalised group to be very small, or missing altogether (Schwartz and Zamboanga, 2008).
    • One model for everyone. The model assumes the same two dimensions and the same four strategies for every kind of migrant, every host country and every group.

    Real life: which host culture?

    Close to nine in ten UAE residents are expatriates, from around 200 nationalities. That makes acculturation part of daily life here. It also makes it harder to measure.

    Take years of residence. A student born in Dubai to parents from Kerala or Cairo has lived here their whole life, so a proxy measure would score them as highly acculturated. Acculturated to what, though? Emirati culture, the culture of their international school, or the mix of cultures they meet every day? A tool built for one host culture and one heritage culture may not fit a city with two hundred.

    During the reading: which culture would you say you have acculturated to, and what would a questionnaire need to ask to capture your answer?

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to acculturation
    MeasurementAcculturation cannot be seen; it is inferred from questionnaire answers or proxies such as years of residence, and the theory built into each tool decides which strategies it can find.
    CausalityStrategies are measured, not assigned, so links between integration and adaptation, or between residence and health, are correlations that leave other explanations open.
    BiasMany tools were built for one host culture and one heritage culture, and most large studies were run in Western host societies.
    ChangeAcculturation is a process over time; a cross-sectional study measures people once and cannot follow how a person's strategy changes.
    PerspectiveThe two-dimensional model replaced the one-line view; whether one model fits every migrant, host country and group is still debated.
    ResponsibilityCalling one strategy "best" can lead to blaming people for how they adapt, when their options depend on the host society and on how much choice they had.

    HL extension

    Technology: remote acculturation

    You no longer have to move country to acculturate. Ferguson, Ferguson and Ferguson (2015) studied 83 adolescents at a private school in Lusaka, Zambia. Through television, social media and imported goods, many had taken on behaviours, values and identities linked to the USA, the UK and South Africa, cultures they had never lived in. The researchers called this remote acculturation, and they measured behaviours, values and identity separately. A cluster analysis split the sample into two groups: one more traditionally Zambian, and one more oriented to the remote cultures.

    Video calls and messaging work in the other direction too. A student can keep daily contact with grandparents overseas while building a new social life here. Does that make integration easier, or does it make separation easier to keep up?
