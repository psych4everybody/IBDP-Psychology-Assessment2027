    Health, well-being and the disorder we will study

    The IB guide takes a holistic view of health: mental and physical health affect each other all the time. Well-being is linked to both but is not the same thing. It is a person's own sense that their life is pleasant and satisfying, so it is self-reported, while mental health can also be assessed with clinical tools such as diagnostic interviews.

    The disorder we will use throughout this unit is major depressive disorder (MDD), so you can look at one condition through biological (this chapter), cognitive and sociocultural lenses, and then at its treatment.

    In the DSM-5, a person is diagnosed with MDD when they have five or more of the nine symptoms below during the same two-week period, and this is a change from how they were before. At least one of the five must be depressed mood or loss of interest or pleasure. The symptoms must also cause significant distress or problems in daily life, for example at school, at work or with family. Diagnosis is made by a trained professional, never by a checklist alone, and never by friends.

    #Symptom (in plain words)
    1Low or depressed mood most of the day, nearly every day (in teenagers this can show as irritability).
    2Much less interest or pleasure in almost all activities.
    3A clear change in weight or appetite (loss or gain) when not dieting.
    4Sleeping too little or too much, nearly every day.
    5Moving or speaking more slowly, or being restless and agitated, in a way other people can see.
    6Tiredness or loss of energy nearly every day.
    7Feeling worthless, or feeling too much guilt.
    8Difficulty thinking, concentrating or making decisions.
    9Recurring thoughts about death, including thoughts of suicide.

    MDD is common. The World Health Organization estimates that about 4% of the world's population experienced depression in 2023. It is a health condition, not a weakness or a choice, and it can be treated.

    Mental health in the UAE: a contextual note

    In 2016 the UAE government approved the National Programme for Happiness and Wellbeing, which aims to build well-being into government policy, services and everyday life. A study of young people using a child psychiatry service at Rashid Hospital in Dubai (Barbato et al., 2021) noted that earlier UAE research had reported reluctance to seek help because of stigma, and that in the wider Arab region people often first seek help from religious or traditional leaders. They also noted that some families use private clinics or seek care abroad, so hospital records may undercount the people affected. Stigma is a barrier to seeking help in many countries, not only this one. We will return to this gap in the chapter on cultural differences.

    What a biological explanation tries to do

    A biological explanation looks for the cause of a disorder in the body. For MDD, researchers have looked at three levels: chemical messengers in the brain (neurotransmission), the genes a person inherits (genetic inheritance), and the structure of the brain. Each level is studied with different tools, and each has its own problems of measurement and causality.

    Neurotransmission and the serotonin hypothesis

    Neurotransmission

    Neurotransmission is how neurons communicate. When a signal reaches the end of the presynaptic neuron, it releases a neurotransmitter into the synaptic cleft, the tiny gap between neurons. The neurotransmitter binds to receptors on the postsynaptic neuron and changes how likely that neuron is to fire. Afterwards, the neurotransmitter is taken back into the presynaptic neuron by a transporter protein. This is called reuptake.

    The monoamine hypothesis, developed in the 1960s, proposed that depression is linked to low availability of monoamine neurotransmitters, especially serotonin, noradrenaline (norepinephrine) and dopamine. Serotonin is involved in regulating mood, sleep and appetite, which are all affected in MDD. The idea gave rise to the popular phrase "chemical imbalance".

    One reason the hypothesis became popular is the way some antidepressants work. Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) block the serotonin transporter. This slows reuptake, so serotonin stays in the synaptic cleft for longer and can keep binding to receptors. If a drug that increases synaptic serotonin can reduce symptoms, it seemed to follow that low serotonin must be the cause.

    That last step is a weak argument. It is sometimes called the treatment aetiology fallacy: a treatment working does not tell you what caused the problem. Paracetamol can relieve a headache, but headaches are not caused by a lack of paracetamol.

    The serotonin hypothesis today

    In 2022, Moncrieff et al. published an umbrella review: a review of existing systematic reviews, meta-analyses and large studies. They included 17 studies across six areas of evidence, such as serotonin levels in body fluids, serotonin receptors, the serotonin transporter, experiments that temporarily lower tryptophan (the building block the body uses to make serotonin), and genetic studies of the serotonin transporter gene. They found no consistent evidence that depression is caused by lower serotonin activity or concentration, and concluded that the serotonin theory of depression is "not empirically substantiated".

    Two points matter when you use this study. First, the review tested the cause of depression, not whether antidepressants help. That is a different question. A large network meta-analysis by Cipriani et al. (2018), of 522 trials with 116,477 participants, found that all 21 antidepressants it studied were more effective than placebo for short-term treatment of depression in adults, with effects ranging from small to moderate. Second, the review was challenged. Jauhar et al. (2023) argued that it left out relevant evidence, for example on tryptophan depletion in people who are vulnerable to depression, and on brain imaging. They concluded that the serotonin system is involved in depression. They did not claim that low serotonin is the simple cause.

    So a fair summary is this: the simple "low serotonin causes depression" story is not supported, but serotonin may still be one part of a much more complex picture. When you write about neurotransmission, explain the mechanism at the synapse, then evaluate the hypothesis. Do not present it as settled fact.

    A note on medication: you will study biological treatments in a later chapter. Decisions about medication are made with a doctor, and nobody should start or stop a medication without medical advice.

    Genetic inheritance: what twin studies show

    Genetic inheritance

    Genetic inheritance is the passing of genes from parents to children. If MDD has a genetic component, close relatives of people with MDD should be more likely to develop it. But families share homes, habits and stressors as well as genes, so a family pattern on its own cannot separate genes from environment.

    Twin studies try to solve this. Monozygotic (MZ, identical) twins share almost all of their genes. Dizygotic (DZ, non-identical) twins share about half, like ordinary siblings. Both kinds of twins usually grow up in the same home at the same time. If MZ twins are more alike in whether they develop depression than DZ twins, the extra similarity is taken as evidence of genetic influence. This comparison produces a heritability estimate.

    Heritability is easy to misread. It is the proportion of the variation in a trait, in a particular population, that can be linked to genetic differences. It is not the chance that one person will develop the disorder. A meta-analysis of five twin studies by Sullivan, Neale and Kendler (2000) estimated the heritability of MDD at about 37%. The same analysis found that environmental factors shared by twins (such as growing up in the same family) explained almost none of the variation, while experiences unique to each person explained most of it. In other words, genes matter, but most of the variation in who becomes depressed is not genetic.

    Researchers then went looking for the genes involved. Early studies focused on candidate genes: single genes chosen because they seemed biologically relevant, such as the serotonin transporter gene. Later, much larger studies did not support most of these findings (see Key study 1 and its "What happened next" row). The current view is that MDD is polygenic: many genes are involved, each with a very small effect. There is no single "depression gene".

    The diathesis-stress model

    Diathesis-stress model

    The diathesis-stress model says that a disorder develops when a diathesis (a pre-existing vulnerability) meets enough stress (difficult experiences in the environment). A diathesis can be biological, such as genetic risk, or psychological, such as a habit of negative thinking. Stress can be a single event, such as losing a job, or a build-up of smaller pressures over time.

    So a person with a strong vulnerability may not develop depression if their life is relatively free of major stress, and a person with little vulnerability may cope with a stressful year. The disorder is most likely when both are present. This explains claim D in the warm-up. It also ends the "nature or nurture" argument: the model's answer is both, and the real question is how they interact.

    Brain structure, briefly

    Brain imaging studies have also compared people with and without MDD. The ENIGMA consortium pooled MRI scans from 15 research samples, including 1,728 people with MDD and 7,199 healthy controls (Schmaal et al., 2016). People with MDD had a slightly smaller hippocampus, a region involved in memory and in regulating stress responses. The difference was small and was found mainly in people with repeated episodes or an early onset (at or before age 21). People in their first episode did not differ from controls. Functional imaging studies have also often reported changed activity in the amygdala, which is involved in emotional responses, and in parts of the prefrontal cortex, which help regulate emotion.

    These findings are correlations. A smaller hippocampus could be a risk factor for depression, or repeated episodes of depression (and the stress that comes with them) could affect the hippocampus. Imaging alone cannot tell us which came first.

    Real-life examples

    • The family history question. When a doctor at a clinic in Abu Dhabi assesses a patient's mood, they usually ask whether anyone in the family has had depression. This reflects genetic inheritance as a risk factor. It does not mean the patient will develop depression, because heritability is about risk across a population, not destiny for one person.
    • "Chemical imbalance" messages. A wellness video shared widely online claims that a supplement "fixes your chemical imbalance". After this chapter you can ask two questions: what evidence is there that the problem is a chemical imbalance (Moncrieff et al., 2022), and has the product been tested against a placebo?
    • Same stress, different outcomes. Two colleagues at a Dubai company both lose their jobs when a project ends. One feels low for a few weeks and recovers; the other develops symptoms that last for months and is later diagnosed by a doctor. The diathesis-stress model predicts this: the same stressor meets different vulnerabilities.

    Key study 1: Caspi et al. (2003), the serotonin transporter gene and life stress

    AimTo investigate whether a variation in the serotonin transporter gene (5-HTT) moderates the effect of stressful life events on depression. In other words, does the same amount of stress affect people differently depending on their version of the gene?
    Participants847 Caucasian New Zealanders from the Dunedin study, a birth cohort born in the early 1970s and followed from birth into adulthood. The analysis was limited to participants of European descent. 17% had two short versions (alleles) of the gene, 51% had one short and one long, and 31% had two long alleles.
    ProcedureA prospective longitudinal cohort study. The researchers did not manipulate any variable. Participants were genotyped. At age 26 they reported stressful life events they had experienced between the ages of 21 and 26, in five areas: employment, money, housing, health and relationships. Depression in the past year was assessed at age 26 with a standardised diagnostic interview.
    Findings17% of participants met the criteria for major depression in the past year. More stressful life events predicted more depression, but much more strongly for people with a short allele. Among those with four or more stressful events, 43% of people with two short alleles developed depression, compared with 17% of people with two long alleles. Carrying the short allele did not predict depression on its own, without stress.
    ConclusionA person's response to stress may be moderated by their genes. This is a gene-environment interaction, and it supports the diathesis-stress model: the short allele acts as a diathesis that matters only when stress is present.
    What happened nextLarger studies did not replicate the finding. Risch et al. (2009) pooled 14 studies with 14,250 participants: stressful life events were strongly linked to depression, but the gene showed no effect, alone or in interaction with stress. Karg et al. (2011) included 54 studies and did find support, mainly for childhood maltreatment, with only a marginal effect for stressful life events. Culverhouse et al. (2018) then pooled 31 data sets with 38,802 participants and found no evidence of a strong interaction. Border et al. (2019) tested 18 popular candidate genes for depression, including this one, in samples of up to 443,264 people, and found they were no more linked to depression than randomly chosen genes. Most researchers now treat the original result as not replicated. What survived is the finding that stress is a strong risk factor.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example of the diathesis-stress model or genetic inheritance); Paper 1 Section B; Paper 1 Section C on bias, causality or measurement; Paper 2 Section B practice (a longitudinal cohort study).
    Evaluate it withBias: a striking finding in a top journal drew enormous attention, while the larger null results came later; the sample was limited to people of European descent in one city. Measurement: life events were self-reported at age 26, so recall may be affected by current mood. Causality: genes were not manipulated and stress was not assigned, so the design shows an association, not a cause. The small subgroups (people with two short alleles and four or more events) make chance findings more likely.

    Key study 2: Kendler et al. (2006), a Swedish national twin study

    AimTo estimate how much genetic and environmental factors contribute to lifetime major depression, and to test whether these contributions differ between women and men and across birth cohorts.
    Participants42,161 twins from the Swedish Twin Registry, including 15,493 complete pairs (both twins took part). The twins were born between 1886 and 1958.
    ProcedureTwins were interviewed by telephone between 1998 and 2003 using a computer-assisted short form of a standardised diagnostic interview, adapted to ask about depression at any point in their lives. Depression was defined using modified DSM-IV criteria, and twins taking antidepressants were counted as affected. The researchers compared how similar MZ and DZ twin pairs were, using statistical twin models, to estimate heritability.
    FindingsHeritability was 42% in women and 29% in men, a significant difference. When the estimate was set to be equal for both sexes, it was about 38%. Environment shared by twins (such as growing up in the same family) made no significant contribution. The genetic risk factors for women and men overlapped only partly. The balance of genetic and environmental factors did not change significantly across birth cohorts.
    ConclusionLifetime major depression is moderately heritable, more so in women than in men, and some genetic risk factors may act differently in the two sexes. Most of the variation is linked to environmental experiences unique to each person.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example of genetic inheritance); Paper 1 Section B; Paper 1 Section C on measurement or bias; Paper 2 Section B practice (a large correlational twin study).
    Evaluate it withMeasurement: lifetime depression was based on a short telephone interview and memory of past decades, and the criteria were modified, so some cases may be missed or misclassified. Bias: the twins were Swedish, so the estimate describes one population; heritability can differ in other places and times. Causality: twin studies assume MZ and DZ twins share environments to the same degree; if MZ twins are treated more alike, heritability may be overestimated.

    Biological reductionism

    Biological reductionism

    Biological reductionism is the attempt to explain complex behaviour by reducing it to its simplest biological parts, such as one neurotransmitter or one gene. The approach has value. It produces precise, testable hypotheses (for example, "people with the short allele will be more vulnerable to stress"), and it has led to research on treatments.

    But MDD shows its limits. The two most reductionist ideas in this chapter, a serotonin deficit and a single gene that interacts with stress, have fared worst under testing. A biological explanation also says little about what a loss means to someone, how they think about it, or how culture shapes the way distress is expressed. The diathesis-stress model is less reductionist, because it puts biology and environment in the same explanation.

    How useful are biological explanations of depression?

    Strengths. Very large twin studies, such as Kendler et al. (2006), show that MDD is moderately heritable, which helps explain why it can run in families. Biological research is testable and self-correcting: the 5-HTT story was tested in tens of thousands of people and largely set aside. Biological explanations can also reduce blame, by showing that depression is a health condition, not a weakness.

    Limitations. Much of the evidence is correlational, so cause and effect are hard to separate. Early findings from small samples were often not replicated. The samples in this chapter come mostly from Europe and New Zealand, so we know little about whether the findings apply in the Gulf region. And biological explanations on their own leave out cognitive and sociocultural factors, which you will study next.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to biological explanations of depression
    MeasurementSerotonin in the living brain is very hard to measure directly, so researchers use indirect markers. Depression in Caspi and Kendler was measured by interview, and life events by self-report.
    CausalityAntidepressants working does not show that low serotonin causes MDD. Imaging and genetic studies show associations; twin studies estimate influence across a population, not causes in one person.
    BiasExciting candidate gene findings were published and cited widely before larger studies failed to replicate them. Most samples are of European descent.
    ChangeScientific understanding has changed: from a "chemical imbalance" and single genes towards many small genetic effects interacting with stress.
    PerspectiveA biological perspective explains part of MDD; cognitive and sociocultural perspectives explain what biology leaves out. The diathesis-stress model combines perspectives.
    ResponsibilitySimple "chemical imbalance" messages may shape how people see their condition and their treatment choices. Researchers and the media have a responsibility to describe the evidence accurately.
