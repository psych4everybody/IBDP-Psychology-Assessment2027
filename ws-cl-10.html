    Why the strategies that feel best often work worst

    Most students revise by rereading notes, highlighting key points and copying out summaries. These strategies feel productive. When you read a page for the third time, the words look familiar and the ideas seem clear. That feeling is called fluency, and it is easy to mistake for learning.

    The problem is that recognising information is not the same as being able to recall it. In an exam, you have to pull the information out of long-term memory with a blank page in front of you. Rereading practises recognition. It does not practise recall. Highlighting has a similar problem: it can feel active, but choosing which line to colour takes little thought about what the line means.

    The psychologist Robert Bjork used the term desirable difficulties for learning conditions that feel harder and slower at the time but lead to better long-term memory. The two strategies in this chapter with the strongest evidence, retrieval practice and spaced practice, are both desirable difficulties. They feel worse during revision, which is one reason students avoid them.

    A large review by Dunlosky et al. (2013) looked at the research on ten learning techniques that students commonly use. It rated practice testing and distributed (spaced) practice as having high utility. Rereading, highlighting and summarising were among the techniques rated as having low utility.

    Retrieval practice: the testing effect

    Retrieval practice means bringing information back to mind from memory, without looking at it. Examples include answering quiz questions, using flashcards, writing down everything you know about a topic on a blank page, or explaining a study to a friend without notes.

    The testing effect is the finding that taking a test on material leads to better long-term memory than spending the same time studying it again. This is surprising, because we usually think of tests as measuring learning, not causing it. Researchers suggest that each act of retrieval strengthens the route back to the memory, so it becomes easier to find next time. Retrieval also shows you what you do not know, which tells you what to revise next.

    Two points matter for revision. First, the effort is part of the benefit. Second, the testing effect usually shows most clearly after a delay of days or weeks. Straight after a session, rereading can look just as good or even better, which is why it is so convincing.

    Spaced practice: little and often

    Spaced practice (also called distributed practice) means spreading study of the same material over several sessions with gaps between them. The opposite is massed practice, which is studying it all in one block, as in cramming the night before a test. The spacing effect is the finding that, for the same total study time, spaced practice leads to better long-term retention than massed practice.

    Two terms help you describe spacing precisely. The gap (or inter-study interval) is the time between study sessions on the same material. The retention interval is the time between the last study session and the test. Research suggests that the best gap depends on how long you need to remember the information. If the test is tomorrow, a short gap is enough. If the exam is months away, the gaps should be longer.

    One explanation is that a gap allows a little forgetting to happen. Coming back to the material then takes more effort, and this effortful relearning makes the memory more durable.

    Cognitive load theory

    Cognitive load theory

    Cognitive load theory was developed by John Sweller in the late 1980s. It starts from two features of human memory. Working memory, where we consciously think about new information, can only handle a few new elements at once. Long-term memory has no known limit and stores knowledge as schemas, organised units of knowledge that can be handled as one chunk. Learning means building schemas in long-term memory. Because new information has to pass through limited working memory first, teaching works best when it does not overload working memory.

    The theory describes three types of cognitive load.

    Type of loadWhat it isExample in a Psychology lessonWhat a teacher or student can do
    Intrinsic loadThe difficulty that comes from the material itself: how many elements have to be held in mind together.Evaluating a study using three concepts at once is harder than recalling its aim.Break complex tasks into steps, and learn the parts before combining them.
    Extraneous loadLoad caused by the way information is presented, which does not help learning.A slide packed with text, read aloud at the same time, while a video plays in the corner.Reduce it: remove clutter, put labels next to diagrams, avoid repeating the same words in speech and on screen.
    Germane loadThe working memory effort that goes into processing the material and building schemas.Comparing two studies and working out what the difference between them means.Free up working memory for this by cutting extraneous load.

    The worked example effect. A worked example is a problem shown with a complete, step-by-step solution. The worked example effect is the finding that novices often learn more from studying worked examples than from solving the same problems on their own. Solving a new problem without a schema forces a beginner to search for a method by trial and error, and this search uses up working memory that could have been spent learning the steps. In early experiments with students learning algebra, Sweller and Cooper (1985) found that students who studied worked examples later solved similar problems faster and with fewer errors than students who had solved the problems themselves. The advantage did not carry over to problems with a different structure, which suggests the students had built narrow schemas.

    Worked examples are not always better. As learners become more expert, the steps they already know become extra information to process, so solving problems themselves becomes more useful. This is called the expertise reversal effect (Kalyuga et al., 2003). For you, this means a model answer is most useful early in a topic. Later, you should practise writing answers from a blank page.

    Real-life examples

    • Learning Arabic vocabulary. A student in Abu Dhabi uses a flashcard app that shows each word again after a gap, and shows words she gets wrong sooner. The app combines retrieval practice (she has to produce the word) with spaced practice (the gaps grow as she improves).
    • The driving theory test. A learner driver in Dubai who answers practice questions on road signs over several weeks is using retrieval and spacing.
    • A maths lesson in Sharjah. A teacher shows one fully worked example of a new type of equation, with each step labelled, before the class tries a similar one. Labels placed next to each line, not in a separate box, reduce extraneous load.

    Using these strategies for IB revision

    What the research means for a DP1 student:

    • Start with a blank page. Before you open your notes, write down everything you remember about a topic, for example the key study tables in this chapter. Then check and fill the gaps. The gaps are your revision list.
    • Space it. Plan to return to each unit several times before your exams, not once in the final week. Short, regular sessions are better than one long session.
    • Mix old and new. Add five questions from last month's chapter to this week's revision. This is both spaced and retrieval practice.
    • Use model answers early, then fade them. Study a model Section B answer to see the structure. Next time, write your own and only check the model afterwards.
    • Cut the clutter. Revise with your phone in another room. Notifications add extraneous load.
    • Do not trust the feeling. If revision feels easy, ask whether you are recognising or recalling.

    Key study 1: Roediger and Karpicke (2006), test-enhanced learning

    AimTo test whether taking memory tests on a prose passage, without feedback, leads to better long-term retention than spending the same time restudying it.
    ParticipantsUndergraduates aged 18 to 24 at Washington University in St Louis, USA, who took part for course credit: 120 in Experiment 1 and 180 in Experiment 2.
    ProcedureParticipants learned short prose passages from a reading comprehension guide, on topics such as "The Sun" and "Sea Otters". Recall was scored as the number of idea units (out of 30) written down. In Experiment 1, each participant studied two passages. One passage was then restudied and the other was tested with a free recall test, with no feedback. They took a final recall test after 5 minutes, 2 days or 1 week. In Experiment 2, participants had four short learning periods in one of three conditions: study four times (SSSS), study three times then test once (SSST), or study once then test three times (STTT). They took a final test after 5 minutes or 1 week. Before leaving, they rated how well they expected to remember the passage in a week.
    FindingsIn Experiment 1, restudying was better after 5 minutes (81% recalled against 75% for the tested passage). After 1 week the pattern reversed: 56% for the tested passage against 42% for the restudied one. In Experiment 2, after 5 minutes the SSSS group recalled most (83%, against 71% for STTT). After 1 week, the STTT group recalled most (61%), then SSST (56%), then SSSS (40%). The SSSS group read the passage about 14 times on average and the STTT group about 3 times, yet the SSSS group predicted they would remember it best.
    ConclusionRetrieval is a way of learning, not only of checking learning. Restudying gives a short-term boost and false confidence; testing gives better long-term retention.
    What happened nextThe testing effect has been tested many times since. A meta-analysis by Rowland (2014) supported it and found that the benefit was larger when the practice test required recall rather than recognition (for example, writing an answer rather than choosing from options). Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated practice testing as one of the two most useful techniques.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section B (a scenario about improving memory); Paper 1 Section C (potential for improving a cognitive process); Paper 1 Section B; Paper 1 Section C on causality, measurement or responsibility.
    Evaluate it withCausality: a laboratory experiment with controlled time and counterbalanced passages, so testing can be identified as the cause of better retention. Measurement: memory was measured as free recall of idea units from one short passage, which is narrower than learning a whole IB unit. Bias: participants were American university students, and no feedback was given, so results may differ for younger students or in real classrooms.

    Key study 2: Cepeda et al. (2006), a meta-analysis of spaced practice

    AimTo combine the results of many studies of distributed practice, and to find out how the gap between study sessions and the retention interval together affect how much is remembered.
    ParticipantsThis is a meta-analysis, so the "participants" are studies: 839 assessments of distributed practice from 317 experiments in 184 published articles. Children and older adults were included, but 85% of the data came from young adults. Clinical populations were excluded.
    ProcedureThe researchers searched for studies of verbal recall tasks, such as learning word lists, word pairs, facts and paragraphs. To be included, a study had to give at least two learning opportunities for the same material (or one massed presentation) and use a recall test at the end. They compared spaced with massed practice, and analysed how the length of the gap related to the length of the retention interval.
    FindingsAcross 271 comparisons, average recall was 47.3% after spaced practice and 36.7% after massed practice. Only 12 of the 271 comparisons showed no benefit or a negative effect of spacing. The best gap became longer as the retention interval became longer. For retention intervals of six months or more, gaps of at least one month gave the best retention.
    ConclusionSpacing study sessions improves recall, and the gap should be matched to how long the information needs to be remembered.
    What happened nextThe authors pointed out a gap in the evidence: 80% of the comparisons used retention intervals of less than a day, and only 4% used intervals longer than a month. They called for more research with educationally relevant intervals, the kind students face before exams.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section B (a scenario about revision habits); Paper 1 Section C (potential for improving a cognitive process); Paper 1 Section B; Paper 1 Section C on measurement, bias or responsibility.
    Evaluate it withMeasurement: most studies measured recall of simple verbal material, such as word lists, not understanding or essay writing. Bias: most data came from young adults, and a meta-analysis can only include studies that were published, which raises the risk of publication bias. Change: few studies measured memory over months, which is the timescale of IB revision.

    How useful is research on strategies to improve memory?

    Strengths. The main findings are consistent. The testing effect and the spacing effect have been found in many experiments, and meta-analyses such as Rowland (2014) and Cepeda et al. (2006) support them. Controlled experiments allow researchers to claim that the strategy caused the improvement. The strategies are cheap: a teacher needs no equipment to start each lesson with a quiz. Cognitive load theory gives teachers clear, practical guidance, such as removing clutter from slides and using worked examples with beginners.

    Limitations. Many laboratory studies use short passages, word lists and retention intervals of a day or less, which are far from learning a two-year IB course. Most participants have been university students, so findings may not generalise to younger learners or to every culture. Cognitive load is hard to measure directly. It is often measured by participants rating their own mental effort, and critics argue that the three types of load are hard to separate in practice. Some students find frequent quizzes stressful, and the research rarely measures this. Finally, the expertise reversal effect shows that the same strategy can help one learner and hinder another, so there is no single method that works for everyone.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to strategies to improve memory and learning
    MeasurementLearning is measured as recall of idea units or words, and cognitive load is often measured by self-rated effort. Both may miss deeper understanding.
    CausalityRoediger and Karpicke controlled study time and used counterbalancing, which supports the claim that testing caused better retention.
    BiasStudents judge strategies by how they feel (the SSSS group's false confidence), and research samples are mostly university students.
    ChangeThe benefit of testing over rereading only appears after a delay, and the value of worked examples changes as a learner gains expertise.
    PerspectiveThe research takes a cognitive perspective. It says less about motivation, anxiety, sleep or the culture of a school.
    ResponsibilitySchools, teachers and app designers who adopt a strategy for thousands of students must judge whether laboratory evidence is strong enough for their setting.
