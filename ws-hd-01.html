    How the brain is built: maturation

    Brain maturation is the genetically guided growth and organisation of the brain over time. It follows a broadly similar timetable in every healthy child, in every culture. Maturation does not just mean the brain gets bigger: it becomes more organised and faster at sending signals.

    The basic unit is the neuron. Each neuron has dendrites, which receive signals, a cell body, and an axon, which carries signals to other neurons. The gap between two neurons is a synapse, where chemical messengers (neurotransmitters) pass the signal on. Many axons are wrapped in myelin, a fatty layer that makes signals travel faster.

    Brain maturation involves several overlapping processes:

    ProcessWhat happensWhen it mostly happens
    NeurogenesisNew neurons are produced.Mostly before birth
    MigrationNew neurons move to their place in the brain.Before birth
    SynaptogenesisNeurons form huge numbers of synapses with each other.Starts before birth; very rapid in infancy; the peak comes at different ages in different brain areas
    MyelinationAxons gain a myelin layer, so signals travel faster.Starts before birth and continues through adolescence
    Synaptic pruningSynapses that are used less are removed; well-used ones are kept and strengthened.Through childhood and adolescence, at different times in different areas

    The surprising part is pruning. The young brain makes far more synapses than it will keep. Huttenlocher and Dabholkar (1997) counted synapses in brain tissue from people who had died at different ages. Synapse density in the auditory cortex (hearing) peaked at about 3 months after birth, but in the prefrontal cortex (planning and decision-making) it peaked later, after 15 months. Synapses were then lost. In the auditory cortex the loss was finished by about age 12, while in the prefrontal cortex it continued into mid-adolescence. A simple way to remember pruning is "use it or lose it". Connections that experience keeps active survive, and the rest are removed. Like cutting back a rose bush, this makes the strongest connections work better.

    Different areas mature at different times. Gogtay et al. (2004) used MRI to scan 13 healthy children every two years, for 8 to 10 years, covering ages from about 4 to 21. They made "time-lapse" maps of grey matter. Grey matter decreased as the brain matured, and this happened in a clear order: areas for basic senses and movement matured first, and higher-order areas that combine information matured later. Areas at the front of the brain, including parts of the prefrontal cortex, were among the last to mature. This is why psychologists often say the adolescent brain is "still under construction".

    Why this matters for behaviour. The prefrontal cortex supports planning, impulse control and weighing up consequences. If it is still maturing in adolescence, this could help explain why some teenagers take more risks than adults, especially in front of friends. Be careful with this claim. It links brain data with behaviour, but it does not prove the brain difference causes the behaviour. Peers, family rules, culture and opportunity all matter too, and many adolescents are careful decision-makers.

    Critical periods and sensitive periods

    Maturation sets the timetable, but the brain also needs the right experience at the right time. A critical period is a window of time in development when a particular experience is needed for a skill or function to develop normally. If the experience is missing during that window, the function may never develop normally, even if the experience comes later. A sensitive period is a softer version of the idea: a window when learning is easiest and most efficient, but learning later is still possible, just harder and often less complete.

    The strongest evidence for critical periods comes from vision in animals. In research reviewed by Greenough, Black and Wallace (1987), closing one eye of a young monkey during a fairly brief period after birth left the animal with a severe and lasting impairment in that eye. Closing an eye outside this window does not have the same effect. This is the classic pattern of a critical period: the timing of the experience, not just the experience, decides the outcome.

    Language is the most debated human example. Children who learn a second language young usually end up with a native-like accent, while adults who learn the same language often keep an accent for life. Babies seem able to hear differences between speech sounds from many languages, and this ability narrows during the first year of life towards the sounds of the language they hear around them (Key study 1). This is sometimes called perceptual narrowing.

    A tragic case is often used here. A girl known in research as Genie was kept in severe isolation, with almost no language around her, until she was found in early adolescence. She learned many words afterwards, but she did not fully master grammar. Her case is often used to support a critical period for language. However, it is a single case, and she had also experienced extreme neglect, so missing language at the right time cannot be the only explanation. It also raises serious ethical questions about how she was studied, so use it with care and respect.

    So how far do critical periods explain development? For some basic functions, such as parts of vision and the ability to hear certain speech sounds, the evidence for time-limited windows is strong. For most complex human skills, such as vocabulary, reading, music or social skills, "sensitive period" is the better term: earlier is often easier, but later learning still happens. That is where neuroplasticity comes in.

    Neuroplasticity: the brain changes with experience

    Neuroplasticity is the brain's ability to change its structure and function as a result of experience. It can involve forming, strengthening or removing synapses, and changing how much brain space is used for a function. Plasticity is highest in childhood but continues across the lifespan.

    Greenough, Black and Wallace (1987) wrote an influential paper on this topic. It is important to know what kind of publication it is: it is a theoretical review, not an experiment. The authors brought together many earlier studies, mostly animal research on visual deprivation and on rats raised in different environments, including some of their own. From this evidence they proposed two ways in which experience is stored in the brain.

    • Experience-expectant plasticity. Some experiences happen to almost every member of a species, such as seeing patterned light or hearing speech. The brain "expects" them. It overproduces synapses at a certain point in development, and then the expected experience decides which synapses are kept and which are pruned. Greenough and colleagues suggested that this process probably underlies many critical and sensitive period effects.
    • Experience-dependent plasticity. Other experiences are unique to each individual, such as the layout of your home, the words in your vocabulary, or a skill like playing the oud. Here the brain forms new synapses in response to the experience, and this can happen at any age.

    One line of evidence they reviewed came from rats housed in a complex environment, with other rats, toys and objects to explore, compared with rats housed alone in plain cages. The rats from the complex environment had about 20% more synapses per neuron in part of the visual cortex. This is animal research, so we must be careful when we generalise to children, but it shows that experience can change the physical structure of the brain.

    Together, the two ideas explain why some windows matter so much and why learning never stops. Greenough and colleagues argued that these categories fit what neurons actually do better than the terms "critical period" or "sensitive period" alone.

    Real-life examples

    • Growing up with two or three languages. Many children in Dubai and Abu Dhabi hear Arabic, English and a home language such as Urdu or Malayalam from birth, and pronounce all of them with ease. Their parents, who learned English or Arabic as adults, may speak fluently but keep an accent. This fits a sensitive period for speech sounds, not a closed door: the parents still learned the language.
    • Early hearing checks. Many hospitals, in the UAE and elsewhere, check a baby's hearing soon after birth. The logic is a sensitive period: if a hearing difficulty is found early, support can begin while the brain is most ready to learn the sounds of language.
    • Learning the oud or the piano. A Grade 10 student in Sharjah who has practised the oud since age six uses the fingers of her left hand in fine, separate movements every day. Elbert et al. (1995) suggests her brain is likely to give more space to those fingers. Her grandfather, who starts lessons at 60, can still improve, which is experience-dependent plasticity in action.

    Key study 1: Werker et al. (1981), Hindi speech sounds

    AimTo investigate whether infants from English-speaking homes can tell apart speech sounds from Hindi that English does not use, and whether this ability declines with age and language experience.
    ParticipantsThree groups: English-speaking adults, Hindi-speaking adults, and infants of about 7 months (6 to 8 months old) from English-speaking homes.
    ProcedureA quasi-experiment comparing natural groups. All groups heard pairs of Hindi syllables. Two contrasts were tested: a retroflex versus a dental "t" sound (made with the tongue curled back, or touching the teeth), and a voiceless aspirated versus a breathy voiced sound. Neither difference is used to separate words in English. Infants were tested with a head-turn procedure: one syllable was played again and again, and the infant learned that if they turned their head when the sound changed, a toy display lit up as a reward. Correct head turns at a change showed that the infant had noticed the difference. Adults were tested with an adapted version of the same procedure.
    FindingsThe infants discriminated both Hindi contrasts, and did much better than the English-speaking adults. The Hindi-speaking adults discriminated the contrasts of their own language. The English-speaking adults did poorly, and the decline with age was clearest for the retroflex versus dental contrast, which is the rarer sound difference across the world's languages.
    ConclusionInfants can hear speech contrasts that are not used in the language around them. With age and experience, this ability declines for sounds that are not part of the person's own language.
    What happened nextThe 1981 study could not show when the decline happens. Werker and Tees (1984) tested English-learning infants at 6 to 8, 8 to 10 and 10 to 12 months on the Hindi contrast and on a contrast from Thompson, a Salish language of Canada (a little like the difference between Arabic ك and ق). On the Hindi contrast, 11 of 12 infants aged 6 to 8 months succeeded, but only 2 of 10 aged 10 to 12 months. A small longitudinal group of 6 infants, tested three times, showed the same decline in the same babies. A small number of Hindi- and Thompson-learning infants aged 11 to 12 months could still hear the contrast from their own language. The decline happens in the first year of life.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example of neuroplasticity or a sensitive period); Paper 1 Section B; Paper 1 Section C on change, measurement or causality.
    Evaluate it withMeasurement: an infant cannot tell us what they hear, so discrimination was inferred from head turns. This is a clever measure, but a baby who is tired or bored may not turn even if they hear the change. Causality: the groups differed in age and in language experience at the same time, so the 1981 study alone cannot separate the two. The Hindi-speaking adults help: they kept the ability, which points to experience rather than age alone. Change: the study measured behaviour, not the brain. Synaptic pruning is a possible explanation of the decline, but it was not observed in this study.

    Key study 2: Elbert et al. (1995), the string players' brains

    AimTo investigate whether long-term practice on a string instrument changes how much of the brain's touch area (the somatosensory cortex) represents the fingers of the left hand, the hand that presses the strings.
    ParticipantsA small group of adult string players, such as violinists, who had started playing at different ages, and a control group of people who did not play string instruments.
    ProcedureA quasi-experiment with natural groups, plus a correlation. Light taps were delivered to the fingers by an air-pressure device. The brain's response was recorded with magnetic source imaging (a form of magnetoencephalography, MEG), a brain imaging technique that measures the tiny magnetic fields produced when neurons are active. This showed where, and how strongly, each finger was represented in the somatosensory cortex. The researchers compared the left hand with the right hand, and musicians with controls, and related the results to the age at which each musician had started to play.
    FindingsThe cortical representation of the fingers of the left hand was larger in the string players than in the controls. The difference was smallest for the left thumb, which does less of the fingering work. There was no difference for the fingers of the right hand, which (for a violinist) holds the bow and does not make separate finger movements. The size of the change was related to the age at which the person began to play: it was larger in those who started young, before about the age of 12.
    ConclusionThe way the brain represents the body depends on use, and changes to fit the needs and experiences of the person. Early experience may produce larger changes.
    What happened nextImai et al. (2003) trained 16 adult men on a touch task using the left ring finger, with a similar brain imaging device. Their performance improved, but the brain representation of the finger did not change. The authors suggested that the age at which training starts and the amount of training may explain why Elbert found a change and they did not. This supports the idea that plasticity continues in adults but is greater earlier in life.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (neuroplasticity, localization of function or brain imaging techniques); Paper 1 Section B; Paper 1 Section C on change, causality or measurement.
    Evaluate it withCausality: musicians were not randomly allocated, so people with a larger finger area may have been more likely to take up and keep playing a string instrument. The right hand acts as a useful comparison inside each person, which makes practice a more likely cause. Measurement: MEG shows brain activity in real time, but it infers the location and size of the representation from magnetic fields recorded outside the head. Bias: the sample was small and specialised, so the findings may not generalise to other skills or to children.

    How useful are these models of brain development?

    Strengths. The three ideas fit together into one picture: maturation provides the timetable and the oversupply of synapses, experience-expectant plasticity uses common experiences at the right time, and experience-dependent plasticity allows learning across the lifespan. Very different methods, from infant behaviour (Werker) to brain tissue (Huttenlocher), MRI (Gogtay) and MEG (Elbert), point in the same direction. The ideas also have practical value, for example in early hearing checks and language-rich care for young children.

    Limitations. Much of the strongest evidence for critical periods comes from animals, and generalising to children is risky. Human evidence is mostly correlational or quasi-experimental, because children cannot be randomly allocated to experiences, so age and experience are hard to separate. Some human "evidence" comes from rare and tragic cases, such as Genie, where many causes are mixed together. Finally, it is easy to over-read brain data: a late-maturing prefrontal cortex does not mean every teenager is reckless, and the term "critical period" is often used for what is really a sensitive period.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to brain development
    MeasurementBabies cannot report what they hear, so perception is inferred from head turns. Brain changes are inferred from MEG, MRI or tissue counts, each of which measures something slightly different.
    CausalityResearchers cannot randomly allocate children to deprivation or to years of music practice, so most human studies compare natural groups. Age, experience and personal choice are hard to separate.
    BiasSmall, specialised samples (a few string players, infants from one language background) and a reliance on rare cases may give a distorted picture. Striking cases are also more likely to be repeated in textbooks.
    ChangeThis whole chapter is about change: synapses are added and pruned, abilities narrow, and new skills reshape the brain. The key question is how much change is still possible after early windows.
    PerspectiveA biological perspective focuses on maturation and synapses. A sociocultural perspective reminds us that the experiences the brain "expects" (such as language) are provided by families and cultures.
    ResponsibilityIf early experience matters, families, schools and governments share responsibility for early care, hearing checks and language-rich environments. Researchers also have a responsibility to describe cases such as Genie ethically and not to exaggerate claims about "windows closing".

    The finding: In Werker and Tees (1984), 11 of 12 English-learning infants aged 6 to 8 months discriminated a Hindi contrast, but only 2 of 10 aged 10 to 12 months did.

    A first reaction: "So by one year old the window has closed, and the brain can never hear these sounds again." Before you accept that, ask one more question.

    • The infants at 10 to 12 months did not turn their heads to the change. Does that tell us the brain cannot detect the difference, or only that the baby no longer treats it as important?
    • The babies were not given any Hindi experience during the study. What kind of study would you need to find out whether the ability can come back?
    • How large were the groups, and what does that mean for how confident we can be about the exact age?
