    It is not only what happens, it is how we read it

    A cognitive model of a mental health disorder explains the disorder through the way a person processes information: what they notice, how they interpret it, what they remember and what they believe. Cognitive models of major depressive disorder (MDD) argue that the way a person thinks about events plays a central part in how they feel and act. In the warm-up, the same unanswered message could mean "everyone is busy" or "nobody likes my ideas". A cognitive model asks why some people reliably reach for the second reading.

    The best-known cognitive model of depression comes from the American psychiatrist Aaron Beck. Beck trained as a psychoanalyst in the 1950s. Psychoanalytic theory claimed depression was anger turned inwards, so Beck expected his depressed patients to dream about hostility. Instead, their dreams were mostly about loss and personal inadequacy. He also noticed that patients reported quick, unplanned negative thoughts about themselves that seemed to pop up before a drop in mood. His book Depression: Clinical, Experimental, and Theoretical Aspects (Beck, 1967) set out the cognitive model. It is a theory built from clinical observation, not a single experiment.

    Beck's cognitive triad

    Cognitive models

    Beck noticed that the negative thinking of depressed patients fell into three linked areas. He called this the cognitive triad.

    Part of the triadWhat it involvesExample thought
    Negative view of the selfThe person sees themselves as inadequate, unlovable or worthless. This is a stable, general belief, not a passing moment of self-criticism."I am not good enough."
    Negative view of the worldExperiences and other people are read as unfair, rejecting or defeating. Neutral events are interpreted as failures or slights."Everyone at school is against me."
    Negative view of the futureThe person expects their difficulties to continue and believes nothing they do will change things. This is linked to hopelessness."It will always be like this."

    The three parts feed each other. If I believe I am not good enough (self), I will expect people to reject me (world), and I will see no point in trying again (future). Beck argued that this pattern explains symptoms such as low mood, loss of motivation and withdrawal.

    Negative schemas and early experience

    Schema theory

    Why would someone develop these views? Beck's answer uses schema theory. A schema is a mental framework of knowledge and beliefs, built from experience, that guides how we process new information. We have schemas for places, people, events and ourselves. Schemas let us process information quickly, but they also make us see what we expect to see.

    Beck proposed that some people develop negative self-schemas through early experiences, for example repeated criticism, the loss of a parent, or being rejected or bullied. A child might form the core belief "I am only acceptable if I succeed" or "people will leave me". Beck also described dysfunctional attitudes: rigid rules such as "If I fail partly, it is as bad as being a complete failure."

    A key point is that these schemas can be latent: present but inactive. The person may cope well for years. The schema is activated later by a stressful event that matches it, such as failing an important exam or a relationship ending. This makes Beck's model a type of diathesis-stress model: the schema is the vulnerability (diathesis) and the life event is the trigger (stress). Miranda, Persons and Byers (1990) found support for this idea. Among people who had been depressed in the past, scores on a dysfunctional attitudes questionnaire rose as their current mood got worse; among people who had never been depressed, mood made little difference.

    Automatic negative thoughts and cognitive distortions

    Once a schema is active, it produces automatic negative thoughts (often shortened to ANTs). These are fast, involuntary thoughts that appear in response to a situation. The person usually does not notice them as thoughts; they simply feel true. An essay handed back with comments might trigger "I am stupid" before the comments are even read.

    Beck argued that these thoughts come from systematic errors in reasoning, which he called cognitive distortions. Everyone makes these errors sometimes. In MDD they are frequent, they point in a negative direction, and they are hard to step back from.

    DistortionWhat it meansExample
    Arbitrary inferenceReaching a negative conclusion without evidence."My friend did not reply, so she must be angry with me."
    Selective abstractionFocusing on one negative detail and ignoring the rest.A report has six positive comments and one suggestion; only the suggestion is remembered.
    OvergeneralisationDrawing a broad rule from a single event."I failed this quiz, so I fail at everything."
    Magnification and minimisationMaking bad events seem bigger and good events seem smaller than they are. Later writers on cognitive therapy often call the extreme form of magnification catastrophising."Winning the debate was luck. Forgetting one point was a disaster."
    PersonalisationTaking responsibility for events that have little to do with you."The team lost because I was playing."
    Dichotomous (all-or-nothing) thinkingSeeing things in only two categories, with nothing in between."If I do not get a 7, I have failed."

    How negative beliefs keep themselves going: confirmation bias

    Confirmation bias

    If negative beliefs are false, why does new experience not correct them? The answer lies in confirmation bias: the tendency to search for, notice, interpret and remember information that fits what we already believe, and to overlook information that does not. A negative self-schema works like a filter at each stage of processing:

    • Attention: the person notices the frown in the audience but not the people nodding.
    • Interpretation: ambiguous events, like an unanswered message, are read in the most negative way.
    • Memory: failures are recalled more easily than successes, so the past seems to confirm the belief.

    Behaviour then closes the loop. A person who expects rejection may stop going to social events. They then get fewer chances to have positive experiences that might challenge the belief, and the isolation itself lowers mood. The result is a vicious cycle: schema, then automatic thought, then low mood, then withdrawal, then less contrary evidence, then a stronger schema.

    This is why "just think positively" rarely helps: the schema filters out the evidence the person would need to change their mind. Change is possible, but it takes deliberate work to notice and test the thoughts. That is the basis of cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), which you will study in a later chapter.

    Real-life examples

    These are everyday thinking patterns, not diagnoses. Having a negative thought does not mean someone has MDD.

    • Report day in Sharjah. A Grade 10 student gets a report with strong comments in five subjects and "needs to revise more consistently" in maths. That evening she can only remember the maths comment. This is selective abstraction, and confirmation bias at the memory stage.
    • The job application in Abu Dhabi. A young graduate is turned down for a position after one interview and tells himself, "No company will ever want me." One rejection has become a rule about the future: overgeneralisation feeding the negative view of the future in the triad.
    • Football trials in Dubai. A player misses one penalty at an academy trial and decides, "The coach thinks I am useless," even though the coach has said nothing. This is arbitrary inference. If he skips the next trial, he loses the chance to find out he was wrong.

    Key study 1: Alloy et al. (2006), the Temple-Wisconsin Cognitive Vulnerability to Depression Project

    AimTo test whether negative cognitive styles come before depression and predict who develops it, both for first episodes (first onsets) and for later episodes (recurrences).
    ParticipantsFirst-year university students at Temple University (Philadelphia) and the University of Wisconsin-Madison in the USA. 5,378 students were screened. The final sample was 347: 172 at high cognitive risk (HR) and 175 at low cognitive risk (LR), with an average age of about 19. None had a current psychiatric disorder at the start, although some had been depressed in the past.
    ProcedureA prospective (forward-looking) longitudinal study. Students completed two questionnaires: the Dysfunctional Attitude Scale, based on Beck's theory, and the Cognitive Style Questionnaire, which measures how people explain negative events. Students who scored in the most negative quarter on both were placed in the HR group; those in the most positive quarter on both were placed in the LR group. Students were then interviewed every 6 weeks for 2.5 years, using structured diagnostic interviews, to record any new episodes of depression. Depressive symptoms at the start of the study were controlled for in the statistics.
    FindingsAmong students with no history of depression, 16.2% of the HR group developed a first episode of major depression, compared with 2.7% of the LR group. For minor depression the figures were 45.9% and 14.4%. Among students who had been depressed before, 28.6% of the HR group had a new episode of major depression, compared with 9.4% of the LR group. Overall, HR students had odds of depression between 3.5 and 6.8 times higher than LR students. The groups did not differ in anxiety disorders that occurred without depression.
    ConclusionNegative cognitive styles act as a vulnerability factor: they are present before depression and predict both first episodes and recurrences. The effect was specific to depression rather than to mental health problems in general.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section B (evidence that schemas and dysfunctional attitudes come before depression); Paper 1 Section C on causality or change; Paper 2 Section B practice (a prospective correlational study).
    Evaluate it withCausality: cognitive style was measured before depression began and starting symptoms were controlled, which supports the claim that negative thinking comes first; but cognitive style was not manipulated, so a third factor, such as early adversity or genetic risk, could cause both. Measurement: cognitive style was self-reported, but depression was diagnosed with structured interviews every 6 weeks, so episodes were unlikely to be missed. Bias: the sample was American university students, mostly aged 18 to 19, so the results may not generalise to other ages, cultures or people not in higher education. Change: most HR students did not develop major depression during the study, so a negative cognitive style raises risk; it does not decide a person's future.

    Key study 2: Joormann, Talbot and Gotlib (2007), attention bias in girls at risk for depression

    AimTo test whether girls at high risk of depression, who had never been depressed themselves, show an attention bias towards negative information, as cognitive models predict.
    Participants41 girls aged 9 to 14 in the USA (average age about 12). 21 were at high risk: their mothers had had at least two episodes of MDD since the girl was born. 20 were controls whose mothers had no history of a mental health disorder. No girl had a past or current disorder herself, which was checked by structured interviews with each girl and her mother.
    ProcedureA quasi-experiment, because risk group was not assigned by the researchers. First, each girl watched a short sad film clip about loss and then used guided imagery, to bring on a mild sad mood. Cognitive models predict that schemas show up when low mood activates them. Then the girls did a dot-probe task on a computer. Two photos of the same person's face appeared side by side for 1.5 seconds: one emotional (sad or happy) and one neutral. A dot then replaced one face and the girl pressed a key to show its side. Faster responses when the dot replaced the sad face suggest attention was already there. At the end of the session the girls watched a cheerful film.
    FindingsHigh-risk girls showed an attention bias towards sad faces and no bias towards happy faces. Control girls showed the opposite: a bias towards happy faces and none towards sad faces. This pattern remained when the girls' current depressive symptoms and anxiety were taken into account.
    ConclusionA bias to attend to negative information can be present before a person has ever been depressed, which supports the claim of cognitive models that biased processing is a vulnerability factor and not only a symptom of the disorder.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section B (attention as the first stage of the confirmation bias filter); Paper 1 Section C on causality, measurement or responsibility.
    Evaluate it withCausality: the bias was found in girls who had never been depressed, which supports the bias coming first; but it was measured at one point in time, so we do not know whether these girls later developed depression. Perspective: the girls may have learned the bias from their mothers or inherited a biological risk; the study cannot separate cognitive, social and biological explanations. Measurement: attention was inferred from reaction times, not observed directly. Bias: a small sample of girls only, all from one country. Responsibility: the researchers made children feel sad on purpose; they reduced harm by using mild film clips and ending with a cheerful film.

    Cause or symptom?

    This is the central debate about cognitive models. Depressed people clearly think more negatively than non-depressed people. But that fact alone cannot tell us which came first. There are three possibilities:

    • Cause: negative schemas and thinking come first and make depression more likely. This is Beck's claim.
    • Symptom: depression comes first, and negative thinking is part of what being depressed is like. When the depression lifts, the thinking improves.
    • Both: negative thinking raises the risk of depression, and depression in turn makes thinking more negative, which keeps the cycle going.

    Studies that compare depressed and non-depressed people at one moment (cross-sectional studies) cannot separate these options. The two key studies get closer: Alloy et al. (2006) measured thinking before depression appeared, and Joormann, Talbot and Gotlib (2007) studied girls who had never been depressed. Both suggest biased thinking can come before depression. Neither proves it is a cause, because neither changed people's thinking. The findings of Miranda, Persons and Byers (1990) add another problem: if people only report dysfunctional beliefs when their mood is low, a questionnaire completed in a good mood may miss them. What the measure shows depends on when it is used.

    The strongest evidence that changing thinking changes depression comes from treatment research. Hollon et al. (2005) found that people who had recovered from depression with cognitive therapy were less likely to relapse after treatment ended than people whose antidepressant medication was stopped. This fits the model, but it is indirect: CBT also changes behaviour and gives support, so changing thoughts may not be what did the work. Decisions about medication are always made with a doctor, and medication should only be stopped with medical advice.

    How useful are cognitive models of depression?

    Strengths. Cognitive models explain the content of depressive thinking, which biological explanations do not: why a person feels worthless, not just why they feel low. They make testable predictions, for example that people with negative cognitive styles will be more likely to become depressed after stress, and prospective research such as Alloy et al. (2006) supports this. They led to cognitive therapy, which has good evidence of effectiveness. They fit the diathesis-stress model, so they can be combined with biological and environmental explanations.

    Limitations. Causality is hard to establish, as explained above. Schemas are inferred from questionnaires and reaction times, and self-report scores shift with mood. The model was developed by a psychiatrist working with patients in the USA, and much of the research uses Western university students, so we should ask whether the triad describes depression in the same way in every culture. Cognitive models also say little about biological factors, such as genetic risk or neurotransmitters (Chapter 01). Finally, there is a responsibility issue: a cognitive model can be misread as blaming people for "thinking wrongly". Beck's point was the opposite. The thinking patterns are learned and automatic, not chosen.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to cognitive models of depression
    MeasurementSchemas and attention biases are never seen directly; they are inferred from questionnaires such as the Dysfunctional Attitude Scale and from reaction times in tasks such as the dot-probe.
    CausalityProspective studies show negative thinking can come before depression, but without manipulating thinking they cannot rule out third factors or show that thinking is the cause.
    BiasThe model explains depression through confirmation bias. Research on it also has sampling bias: many studies use American or Western university students.
    ChangeIf schemas are learned, they can be changed, which is the basis of CBT; but schemas filter out contrary evidence, which explains why change is slow and effortful.
    PerspectiveThe cognitive perspective focuses on thinking. It needs biological and sociocultural perspectives to explain where vulnerability comes from and why rates of depression differ between groups.
    ResponsibilityPsychologists must explain the model without suggesting people are to blame for their thoughts. Researchers who induce sad moods, especially in children, must protect participants from harm.
