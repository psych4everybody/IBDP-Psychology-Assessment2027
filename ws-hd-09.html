    Part A: Observations, a recap

    What is an observation?

    In an observation, the researcher watches and records behaviour as it happens. The researcher does not change any variable, so an observation is not an experiment. It answers questions like "What do people do?", "How often?" and "For how long?" It cannot tell us why they do it, and it cannot show cause and effect.

    Back to the warm-up. Most people are not good at remembering how often they did something ordinary, like picking up a phone. They may also give a number that sounds better than the truth. An observation avoids both problems because the researcher records what people do, not what they say they do. This is the main reason psychologists use observation.

    Four choices every observer makes

    Each observation can be described with four pairs of terms. Use all four when you describe our class practical in the exam.

    ChoiceOne optionThe other option
    SettingNaturalistic: behaviour is observed where it normally happens, such as a home, a park or a cafe. Ecological validity is usually high, but the researcher has little control.Controlled: behaviour is observed in a setting the researcher has set up, such as a playroom with chosen toys. There is more control, but behaviour may be less natural.
    Role of the observerNon-participant: the observer watches from outside the group and does not join in. This makes it easier to record accurately.Participant: the observer joins the group being observed. This can give deeper insight, but the observer may influence the group or lose objectivity.
    AwarenessOvert: the people observed know they are being observed. This is more ethical, but they may change their behaviour.Covert: the people observed do not know. Behaviour is more natural, but informed consent is not possible.
    RecordingStructured: the observer records behaviour in categories decided in advance, using a checklist or tally sheet. This gives quantitative data.Unstructured: the observer writes detailed field notes on everything that seems relevant. This gives qualitative data.

    Behaviour categories and operational definitions

    A structured observation needs behaviour categories: the list of behaviours the observer will record. Each category needs an operational definition, which says exactly what counts and what does not. "Uses their phone" is too vague. Does glancing at a notification count? Does a phone lying face up on the table count? If two observers answer these questions differently, they will record different data from the same scene. Clear operational definitions make an observation repeatable and allow different observers to agree.

    Sampling behaviour

    Observers cannot write down everything, so they decide in advance when and what to record.

    • Event sampling: the observer records every time a target behaviour happens, for example a tally mark each time a person starts using a device. This gives a frequency.
    • Time sampling: the observer records what is happening at set intervals, for example every 60 seconds. This is useful when there is too much behaviour to record all of it.
    • Continuous recording: the observer records how long a behaviour lasts, by noting when it starts and when it stops. This gives a duration.

    Sampling participants is a separate decision. The target population is the group the researcher wants to learn about. An opportunity sample uses whoever is available in the chosen setting at the time. It is quick, but the people observed may not represent the population.

    Reliability and bias in observation

    Inter-observer reliability is the extent to which two observers watching the same behaviour at the same time record the same data. To check it, two observers record the same people independently, then compare their records. A simple way to measure it is percentage agreement:

    Percentage agreement = number of agreements, divided by the total number of comparisons, multiplied by 100.
    Example: two observers compare 20 records and agree on 17 of them. 17 divided by 20 is 0.85, multiplied by 100 is 85%. A common rule of thumb is that 80% or more is acceptable.

    Low agreement usually means the operational definitions are unclear, or that the observers need more practice.

    Observer bias happens when the observer's expectations affect what they record. An observer who expects teenagers to use phones more might notice every teenage glance at a screen but miss an adult's. Clear definitions, two independent observers and keeping the hypothesis from the observers all reduce observer bias.

    The observer effect (also called reactivity) happens when people change their behaviour because they know they are being watched. It is a problem in overt observations. A family that knows you are counting their phone use may put their phones away. Observers reduce this by observing covertly where it is ethical, or by spending time in the setting first so that people get used to them (habituation).

    Ethics in observation

    Observation raises its own ethical questions, because the people observed often do not know.

    • Public places. Psychologists generally accept covert observation only in public places where people would expect to be seen by strangers, and only of behaviour anyone there could see.
    • Privacy. In the UAE, the law protects personal privacy: it is against the law to photograph or record people without their consent. In student research, observers record anonymous tallies only. No names, no photographs, no audio or video, and no notes that could identify a person.
    • Consent. In private settings, such as a home, observers need permission first. For children, a parent or guardian gives consent and the child should also agree.
    • No interference. A non-participant observer does not start conversations, change the setting or do anything that affects the people observed.
    • Confidentiality. Records are kept securely, reported only as group totals and destroyed when the analysis is finished.

    Analysing observation data

    A structured observation gives quantitative data: counts (frequencies) and times (durations). These are analysed with descriptive statistics, such as totals, means and ranges, and shown in a table or a bar chart. An unstructured observation gives qualitative data: field notes, which are analysed by looking for themes. In both cases, keep two steps apart. First describe what the data show. Then interpret what they might mean.

    How useful is observation?

    Strengths. Observation records what people do, not what they say they do, so it avoids memory errors and social desirability bias. Naturalistic observation has high ecological validity, because the behaviour happens in a real setting. It is often the only practical method for studying young children, who cannot fill in a questionnaire, and for behaviour people do without thinking, such as picking up a phone.

    Limitations. Observation cannot explain why people behave as they do, and it cannot show cause and effect. Observer bias and the observer effect can both distort the data. In a naturalistic setting, the observer cannot control extraneous variables, so a second observation may not find the same pattern. Covert observation raises ethical concerns about consent and privacy.

    Key study 1: Fagot (1978), a structured observation in the home

    You met this study's topic in the enculturation chapter. Here we use it for its method.

    AimTo investigate whether parents react differently to the same behaviours in their toddler depending on whether the child is a girl or a boy.
    Participants24 families in the United States, each with one child aged 20 to 24 months. Half of the children were girls and half were boys.
    ProcedureA naturalistic, non-participant, overt observation in each family's home. Each family was observed for five 60-minute sessions over five weeks. The observer used a checklist of 46 child behaviours (such as playing with blocks, playing with dolls, asking for help) and a list of parent reactions, coded as positive, negative or neutral. Afterwards, parents rated how appropriate each behaviour was for girls, boys or both, and completed a questionnaire on their views about raising children.
    FindingsAcross most behaviours, parents treated girls and boys in a very similar way. For behaviours that one sex did more often, parents' reactions differed: for example, parents reacted more positively to girls playing with dolls and to boys playing with blocks. Parents did not rate asking for help as typical of either sex, yet in the observations they reacted more positively when girls asked for help than when boys did.
    ConclusionParents may shape their children's behaviour in ways they are not aware of. The observation showed differences that the parents' own ratings did not.
    Use it forPaper 2 Section A, Questions 1 and 3 (an example of a structured home observation, and of the gap between what people do and what they say); Paper 2 Section B (evaluating an observational study).
    Evaluate it withMeasurement: a behaviour checklist with fixed categories turned everyday family life into data that could be counted and compared. Bias: the observation was overt, so parents may have behaved more carefully while being watched; five sessions over five weeks allowed families to get used to the observer. The sample was small and from one culture in the 1970s, so the findings may not apply to families today or in the UAE.

    Real-life examples: observation outside the classroom

    • Early years classrooms. Kindergarten teachers in many UAE schools keep observation records of young children at play, noting skills such as sharing, turn-taking or holding a pencil. Young children cannot fill in a questionnaire, so watching is the best way to track their development.
    • Road safety. Using a handheld phone while driving is a traffic offence in the UAE. Road safety researchers can stand at a junction and tally how many drivers are holding a phone. Asking drivers would not work, because few people admit to breaking the law.
    • Shopping malls. Store designers in Dubai and Abu Dhabi malls watch where shoppers walk, stop and look, then change the layout. They record movements, not identities.

    Part B: Our class practical, a device use observation

    Read this part after you have handed in your tally sheet.

    Why this topic matters for human development

    The HL technology extension suggests the role of technology on the development of self as one area of inquiry. Children learn who they are partly through everyday interactions: conversations at meals, shared attention during play, a parent noticing what they do. Phones, tablets and laptops now sit in the middle of many of these moments, for children and for the adults around them. Before we can ask whether device use affects development, we need accurate descriptions of how much people of different ages actually use devices in everyday settings. That is a measurement question, and it is what our observation set out to answer. It cannot tell us whether device use helps or harms development, because an observation does not test cause and effect.

    Key study 2: Radesky et al. (2014), devices at family meals

    AimTo describe how caregivers use mobile devices while eating with young children, and how device use relates to their interactions with the children.
    Participants55 caregivers eating with one or more young children in fast food restaurants in the Boston area, United States.
    ProcedureA naturalistic, non-participant, covert observation. Trained observers sat near families during their meal and wrote detailed field notes on how caregivers used devices and how they interacted with the children. Notes were written discreetly, for example on laptops with privacy screens. The field notes were then analysed qualitatively for themes. The families were not told they were observed, and the university's ethics board judged the study exempt from full review.
    Findings40 of the 55 caregivers used a mobile device during the meal. Some used it briefly and put it away; others used it for most of the meal. The main theme was how absorbed caregivers became in their devices. Highly absorbed caregivers were slower to respond to children and sometimes responded harshly when children tried to get their attention. Some children made louder or more insistent bids for attention.
    ConclusionDevice use by caregivers is common at family meals and may reduce how responsive caregivers are to children. The authors treated this as a starting point for further research, not as proof of an effect.
    What happened nextThe authors described the study as a first step. The sample was small, the analysis was qualitative, and observers interpreted what they saw. They called for further research that measures parent and child behaviour more precisely, for example recorded sessions in a laboratory, with consent.
    Use it forPaper 2 Section A, Question 1 (a published model for our practical) and Question 3 (comparing covert public observation with other methods); Paper 2 Section B (evaluating an observational study).
    Evaluate it withMeasurement: field notes captured rich detail about absorption and children's reactions, but the data were not counted in fixed categories, so they are hard to compare across studies. Bias: observers interpreted what they saw, so observer bias is possible. Responsibility: covert observation of families raises the question of whether public settings justify observing people without consent.

    Our practical borrows Radesky's setting and topic, but makes three changes. We used a structured tally sheet instead of field notes, so our data are quantitative. We observed people of all ages, not only caregivers. And we recorded only whether and how long a device was used, never what was on the screen.

    Our practical: the write-up

    AimTo observe and record how often and for how long people of different ages use digital devices in an everyday setting (a home or a public cafe) in the UAE.
    Research questionHow do the number of device uses and the total duration of device use in a 15-minute period differ between age bands?
    Type of observationNaturalistic: people were observed in a home or a cafe, where they normally are. Non-participant: the observer did not join in or interact. Structured: behaviour was recorded on a tally sheet with fixed categories. Overt at home (the family gave permission) and covert in the cafe (people were not told, which is acceptable in a public place with anonymous records and no interference).
    Behaviour samplingEvent sampling for the number of uses (one tally each time a new use starts) and continuous recording for duration (start and stop time of each use), within one 15-minute observation block.
    Operational definition: "device"A smartphone, tablet, laptop, handheld games console or smartwatch that the person is holding or operating. Televisions and other fixed screens do not count.
    Operational definition: "device use"The person is looking at the screen of a device, or touching it to operate it (tapping, typing, swiping), for 3 seconds or more. A phone call with the device held to the ear also counts. A device lying on the table untouched does not count, even if the screen lights up.
    Operational definition: "a use"One continuous period of device use. It starts when device use begins and ends when the person looks away from the screen or puts the device down for 10 seconds or more. If they go back to the device within 10 seconds, it is the same use.
    Age bandsApproximate age, judged by the observer: Child (under 13), Teenager (13 to 19), Adult (20 to 59), Older adult (60 and over). Wide bands were chosen because exact ages cannot be judged by eye.

    Sampling

    Target population: people of all ages in everyday settings in the UAE. Setting: each student chose either their own home or a public cafe, so the settings were an opportunity sample of places we could reach. People observed: an opportunity sample of up to four people who were in clear view at the start of the 15-minute block. Up to four was the limit because one observer cannot track the start and stop times of more people accurately. Only people who stayed for the full 15 minutes were included in the analysis; anyone who left early was marked "left" and removed.

    Inter-observer reliability

    Before the fieldwork, the class checked that the operational definitions worked. Two volunteers acted out a scripted five-minute scene in the classroom: chatting, checking phones, putting them down, picking them up again. Every student tallied the scene independently using the tally sheet. Pairs then compared their records for each person on age band, number of uses and duration (counted as agreeing if within half a minute), and calculated percentage agreement. Where pairs disagreed, the class discussed why and tightened the definitions. The 3-second and 10-second rules in the definitions above came from this step.

    In the field, students who observed in the same cafe at the same time worked as a pair: both tallied the same people independently, then compared. Students who observed at home worked alone, so their reliability rests on the classroom check. That is a limitation to discuss.

    Procedure, step by step

    1. The class practised with the tally sheet in the classroom role-play and checked inter-observer reliability (see above).
    2. Home: a few days before, the student gave the family permission note to the adults at home, explained the study in general terms, and asked each person (and a parent or guardian for younger siblings) whether they agreed to be observed. Anyone who said no was not observed. Cafe: the student went with a parent or another adult, bought a drink and sat where they could see nearby tables without staring.
    3. The observer sat quietly for about five minutes before starting, so that people got used to them being there.
    4. At the start time, the observer chose up to four people in clear view, gave each a code (P1 to P4) and ticked an age band.
    5. For 15 minutes, the observer added a tally each time a new use started and wrote down its start and stop time, using the operational definitions.
    6. At the end of the block, the observer added up the duration of each person's uses, to the nearest half minute, and marked anyone who had left.
    7. Home only: the observer thanked the family and offered to show them the anonymous tallies. Pairs in a cafe compared their sheets and recorded their percentage agreement.
    8. Tally sheets were handed to the teacher. The class combined the data by age band and setting.

    Ethics in our practical

    Ethical issueWhat we did
    Consent at homeAdults in the family gave permission in advance. For younger siblings, a parent or guardian gave permission and the child also agreed. Anyone could say no, and their row was left blank.
    Public settingIn the cafe, consent was not asked, because the observation was covert. This was acceptable because it was a public place where people expect to be seen, we recorded only behaviour anyone could see, and nothing could identify anyone.
    Privacy and UAE lawNo names, no photographs, no audio or video recordings, no notes on appearance, and no looking at screen content. Only anonymous tallies were recorded.
    No interferenceObservers did not talk to, follow or approach anyone they observed, and did not change anything in the setting.
    If someone askedThe observer explained politely that it was an anonymous school project, and offered to tear up the sheet. The person's choice was respected.
    Right to withdraw and debriefFamily members could withdraw their data until the class combined the results, and were offered a look at the anonymous tallies.
    ConfidentialitySheets were handed only to the teacher, reported only as group totals and means, and destroyed after the analysis.
    Teacher supervisionThe teacher approved each student's plan before the fieldwork, because the guide requires class practicals to be supervised by a teacher and any practical outside lessons to have the teacher's approval.
    Student safetyStudents observing in public went with a parent or another adult.

    How good was our observation?

    Strengths. We recorded what people did, not what they said, so the data do not depend on memory or on people wanting to look good. The settings were real homes and cafes, so ecological validity was high. Clear operational definitions, the same tally sheet and the same 15-minute block for everyone made the observation standardised and repeatable. The classroom check, and the paired cafe observations, gave evidence of inter-observer reliability. Recording only anonymous tallies kept the study within UAE privacy law.

    Limitations. Age was judged by eye, so some people will be in the wrong age band. Home observations were overt, so family members may have used their devices less than usual (the observer effect). Home observers worked alone, so their data were not checked by a second observer. The settings and people were opportunity samples, and some age bands may contain very few people, so a mean based on two people can change a lot with one more. Fifteen minutes is a short snapshot: a person observed during a quiet moment may use devices much more at another time. And we cannot say why people used devices, or whether age causes the difference, because nothing was manipulated and other variables (such as the setting or the time of day) were not controlled.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to our class practical
    Measurement"Device use" and "a use" were operationalised with the 3-second and 10-second rules, so that a vague idea became something that could be counted and timed. Age was measured only approximately, in bands.
    CausalityAn observation cannot show cause and effect. If teenagers used devices more, age might not be the cause: setting, time of day or who they were with could explain it.
    BiasObserver bias is possible, especially when observers expect teenagers to use phones most. The observer effect may bias home data. Opportunity sampling of settings may make some age bands unrepresentative.
    ChangeDevice habits change across the lifespan and across years. A pattern recorded now may not hold for the same age bands in ten years' time.
    PerspectiveCounting device use takes an outside view. The people observed may see their own use very differently: a parent reading a message from school is not the same as scrolling, but our tally treats them alike.
    ResponsibilityObservers have a responsibility to protect privacy, especially under UAE law: anonymous tallies only, no screen content, no recordings, and consent wherever the setting is private.
