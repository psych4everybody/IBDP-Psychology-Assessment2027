    What is a chemical messenger?

    Chemical messengers

    This chapter opens the second half of the human relationships unit. So far we have looked at how groups influence individuals. Now we turn to interpersonal relationships: the bonds between two people, such as friendships, family relationships and romantic relationships. Much of the biological research in this area has studied romantic relationships, so this chapter describes that research, along with research on friendship. We start with biology.

    A chemical messenger is any substance that carries a message from one cell in the body to another. There are two main types: neurotransmitters and hormones. Pheromones are sometimes added to the list, but whether they exist in humans is still debated.

    FeatureNeurotransmittersHormones
    Where they workBetween neurons. They are released from the axon terminal into the synaptic gap and bind to receptors on the next neuron.They travel in the bloodstream, so they can reach organs and tissues all over the body.
    Released byNeurons.Endocrine glands, such as the pituitary gland, the hypothalamus and the adrenal glands.
    What they regulateFairly rapid processes, such as movement, emotion and decisions.Longer-term, ongoing processes, such as growth, metabolism, the stress response and reproduction.
    Examples linked to relationshipsDopamine, studied most often in relation to romantic relationships and friendship.Oxytocin and vasopressin (bonding and attachment); testosterone (linked in research to the sex drive). Adrenaline and cortisol are also hormones.

    Neurotransmitters are either excitatory, making the next neuron more likely to fire, or inhibitory, making it less likely to fire. Dopamine is involved in pleasure and reward-seeking. It is released in rewarding situations and motivates us to seek more of them.

    Exam tip: choosing your messenger. In Section A, decide whether to write about a neurotransmitter or a hormone, and pick one. Explain what it is and how it works, then choose one specific example, such as dopamine or oxytocin, describe its function, and support it with a study or a realistic example. In Section B, the scenario may point to either one.

    Not to be confused with neurotransmission. Chemical messengers and neurotransmission are two different content points. If you choose a neurotransmitter, write about the specific messenger and what it does in behaviour. Neurotransmission is about how the signal crosses the synapse.

    A biological model: three systems

    Can a close relationship be explained by the activity of chemical messengers? The everyday phrase "love is a chemical reaction" assumes it can. Fisher, Aron and Brown (2006) proposed a biological model of romantic love based on three functions that, they argued, evolved in birds and mammals to support reproduction and the raising of young. They thought each function runs on a relatively distinct, but connected, brain system.

    SystemWhat it evolved to do, according to the modelMessengers linked to itEvidence in this chapter
    Sex driveMotivate individuals to seek a partner in general.Testosterone and other sex hormonesNot studied here; the regions active in Key study 1 did not match those other research has linked to the sex drive.
    AttractionFocus attention and energy on one preferred partner.DopamineFisher, Aron and Brown (2005), fMRI.
    AttachmentKeep partners together long enough to raise young.Oxytocin and vasopressinAron et al. (2005), and the comparison between species.

    The researchers noticed that courtship in many birds and mammals shares features with human romantic love, such as increased energy and attention focused on one particular individual. Because these responses are automatic and hard to control, they reasoned that some basic brain chemistry might be shared across species. This is an evolutionary argument, and it is the starting point for the studies below.

    Key study 1: Fisher, Aron and Brown (2005), attraction and dopamine

    AimTo investigate which brain regions are active in early-stage, intense romantic love, and whether these include dopamine-rich reward regions.
    Participants17 young adults (10 women and 7 men), aged 18 to 26 (mean about 21), who reported being intensely in love. They had been in love for between 1 and 17 months (mean about 7 months).
    ProcedureEach participant lay in an fMRI scanner and completed four stages: (1) viewing a photograph of the person they loved for 30 seconds; (2) counting backwards from a given number for 40 seconds, a filler task; (3) viewing a photograph of an emotionally neutral acquaintance for 30 seconds; (4) counting backwards again for 20 seconds. The four stages were repeated six times, so the procedure lasted 720 seconds (12 minutes). Brain activity during the two kinds of photograph was compared.
    FindingsViewing the photograph of the loved person, compared with the neutral acquaintance, was linked with increased activity in two dopamine-rich regions: the right ventral tegmental area (VTA) and the caudate nucleus. Both are part of the brain's dopamine reward system, which is linked to motivation and pleasure.
    ConclusionThe researchers inferred that dopamine plays a role in the attraction system, and that early-stage romantic love may work more like a motivation, a drive towards a reward, than like a single emotion.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example of a neurotransmitter's role in behaviour); Paper 1 Section C on causality, measurement or bias; Paper 2 Section B (a study using brain imaging).
    Evaluate it withMeasurement: fMRI does not measure dopamine. It measures changes in blood flow, which are taken to show activity in a region; the role of dopamine is then inferred from the fact that these regions are dopamine-rich. Causality: dopamine was not changed, only activity observed, so the study shows an association, not a cause. Bias: the sample was small (17) and young, and the relationships were fairly new, so the findings may say little about long-term relationships, other age groups or other cultures. Strength: each participant was compared with themselves, seeing both kinds of photograph in the same session, and the procedure was standardised and repeated six times.

    Key study 2: Aron et al. (2005), relationship length and the ventral pallidum

    The third system in the model is attachment, also called pair bonding. In animals, pair bonding shows up in behaviours such as building a nest together, sharing food, grooming and staying close. Its function is to keep parents together long enough to raise their young. The two messengers that research has linked most closely to pair bonding are the hormones oxytocin and vasopressin.

    AimTo find out whether the pattern of brain activity in romantic love changes as a relationship lasts longer.
    ParticipantsThe same 17 participants as in Key study 1. Aron et al. (2005) reported the same scanning study in a separate paper.
    ProcedureThe researchers did not split the sample into groups. They correlated each participant's brain activity with how many months they had been in love, which ranged up to 17 months.
    FindingsThe longer participants had been in love, the more activity there was in several regions, including the ventral pallidum. This matters because studies of other species have found that the ventral pallidum is rich in vasopressin receptors in some species but not in others:
    • Rich in vasopressin receptors: prairie voles, California mice and marmosets. These species form long-lasting pair bonds.
    • Not rich in vasopressin receptors: white-footed mice and rhesus monkeys. These species do not form lasting pair bonds.
    ConclusionAs a relationship lasts longer, the pattern of brain activity may shift towards regions linked in other species to pair bonding. In other words, the longer people had been in love, the more their brain activity resembled that of pair-bonding species such as marmosets.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section B (hormones and bonding in a relationship that develops over time); Paper 1 Section C on measurement, change or causality.
    Evaluate it withMeasurement: there is an assumption inside the conclusion. Activity in the ventral pallidum is taken as a sign of vasopressin activity, but vasopressin itself was not measured. Change: the study suggests that the role of chemical messengers may change as a relationship develops, but it compared different people at one moment rather than following the same people over time. Bias: the comparison group within the sample was even smaller than 17. Perspective: the link to vasopressin depends on animal research, and findings in voles or monkeys may not generalise to humans.

    Why researchers write "plays a role"

    Most conclusions in this area are worded cautiously: chemical messenger X plays a role in behaviour Y. "Plays a role" is not the same as "causes", "influences" or "determines". There are two main reasons for the caution.

    • Messengers are hard to separate from each other. Suppose an experiment increases dopamine and behaviour changes. The dopamine may have changed the level of another messenger, and it may have been that second messenger that changed the behaviour.
    • Their activity is hard to measure. A hormone in the blood has not necessarily reached the brain: not every chemical in the blood crosses the blood-brain barrier. A messenger also needs receptors in the right place. Giving vasopressin to a rhesus monkey, whose ventral pallidum lacks the receptors, would not be expected to strengthen its bond with a partner. Levels of messengers in the living human brain cannot be measured directly, so researchers use fMRI, which measures blood flow, and assume that more activity in a region rich in a messenger means more of that messenger is at work.

    The most direct route to cause and effect would be to change the messenger and watch what happens to behaviour, for example with high doses of hormones or by damaging parts of the brain. With people, this kind of invasive research is unacceptable. Animal research partly fills the gap, but it raises its own ethical questions and its own problems of generalisation.

    Quick lens: measurement. Think of a thermal camera pointed at a school hall during a concert. One corner glows hot. The picture tells you where the heat is. It does not tell you what is happening there, or why. Keep these questions open. You will use them in Check yourself and the Section C plan.

    • In Fisher, Aron and Brown (2005), what was actually measured, and what was assumed to follow from it?
    • What is the difference between a hormone being present in the blood and a hormone affecting behaviour?
    • If messengers cannot be measured directly in the human brain, how should that change the words a researcher chooses for a conclusion?

    Real life: the dopamine hit

    Chemical language has moved out of the laboratory and into everyday talk. Here are three things you might hear this week.

    1. "Every notification gives me a dopamine hit."
    2. "Oxytocin is the bonding hormone, so a long hug will make any two people close."
    3. "His oxytocin must be low. That is why he has no close friends."

    During the reading: for each one, what is it assuming, and what would you need to know before accepting it? Is the sentence about a role, or a cause?

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to chemical messengers
    CausalityCausality is inferred by the researcher, not seen in the data. Studies show that messengers are associated with, or play a role in, behaviour; showing cause would require changing the messenger itself.
    MeasurementBlood or saliva levels and fMRI are indirect measures: fMRI measures blood flow, not chemicals. Conclusions should not rest on a few studies with small samples.
    BiasThe key studies used small samples of young adults in fairly new relationships, so the findings may not generalise to other ages, cultures or kinds of relationship.
    ChangeThe role of messengers differs across species and may change as a relationship develops. Whether it differs between human populations has not been tested in the research in this chapter.
    PerspectiveCognitive and sociocultural factors matter too. Studying biology on its own is a deliberate choice; other perspectives can then explain what biology cannot.
    ResponsibilityThe research that would show cause most clearly is invasive, so it cannot ethically be done with people. Researchers and journalists are responsible for wording findings accurately, rather than turning "plays a role" into "causes".

    I do. A revision website summarises Key study 1 like this: "Scientists found the brain's love centre." Let's check that against the study. What was measured? Blood flow, in two regions, while 17 people looked at a photograph. What was assumed? That more blood flow means a more active region, and that activity in dopamine-rich regions means dopamine is at work. What was inferred? That dopamine plays a role in romantic attraction. Now compare that with the summary. "Centre" suggests one place, but there were two regions. "Found" suggests a discovery of cause, but the study observed activity. The most accurate summary would use the words "is associated with" or "plays a role in".
