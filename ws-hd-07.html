    Peers and the developing self

    A peer is someone of about the same age and status as you: a classmate, a teammate, a friend, or someone in the same online group. Peer influence is any change in a person's attitudes, values or behaviour that comes from being around peers. It can be direct, such as a friend telling you to try something, or indirect, such as noticing what everyone else is wearing and changing your own clothes without a word being said.

    Peer influence matters for the development of self because the self is partly built from other people's reactions. Charles Cooley (1902) called this the looking-glass self: we form an image of ourselves from how we believe others see us. If classmates treat a student as funny, she may start to think of herself as "the funny one" and act the part. In childhood, parents are the main mirror. In adolescence, peers become a larger part of the mirror, because teenagers spend more time with friends, care more about their opinions, and are working out who they are outside the family.

    Peer influence is not only negative. Peers can pull someone towards studying, volunteering or kindness, as well as towards breaking rules.

    Peer culture: children build their own social world

    Peer influence starts early. The sociologist William Corsaro spent about a year as a participant in an American nursery school (Key study 1). He argued that young children do not simply copy adult culture. They build a peer culture of their own: shared routines, rules and values that belong to the children rather than to the adults.

    Two features of the peer culture he observed are easy to recognise. First, children protected their play. When two or three children were playing together, they usually turned away other children who tried to join, because play is easily disrupted. Second, children worked out access strategies to get into a game: hovering nearby, copying what the group was doing, and only then asking to join. Friendship was used as a tool: "We're friends, right?" could open the door to a game. In his wider fieldwork, Corsaro also described how children found shared ways around adult rules, which he called secondary adjustments. Doing this together gave them a sense of control and of belonging to a group.

    Social comparison: "Am I doing this right?"

    Leon Festinger's (1954) social comparison theory proposes that people judge their own abilities and opinions by comparing themselves with others, especially when there is no objective standard. There is no exam that tells you how good you are at making friends or how stylish you are, so you look at the people around you.

    Upward comparison means comparing yourself with someone who seems better off ("She got a 7 and I got a 5"). It can motivate, but it can also lower self-esteem. Downward comparison means comparing yourself with someone who seems worse off, which can protect self-esteem. Festinger argued that we mostly compare ourselves with people who are similar to us, which is why peers are the usual measuring stick in adolescence.

    Conformity: changing behaviour to fit the group

    Conformity is changing your behaviour or beliefs to match those of a group, as a result of real or imagined group pressure. Asch (1951) showed that many participants would sometimes give an obviously wrong answer on a simple line-judging task when the rest of the group gave that answer first. Psychologists usually describe two reasons for conformity (Deutsch and Gerard, 1955):

    • Normative social influence: conforming to be liked and accepted, and to avoid rejection. A student laughs at a joke she does not find funny because everyone else is laughing.
    • Informational social influence: conforming because you think the group knows better, especially when you are unsure. A new student follows the others to the wrong classroom because they seem to know where they are going.

    Peers also shape behaviour through reinforcement. Dishion and colleagues (1996) recorded conversations between adolescent boys and their friends. Friends who laughed at and approved of talk about breaking rules were reinforcing it, a process the researchers called deviancy training.

    Social identity in adolescence

    Social comparison and conformity explain moment-to-moment influence. Social identity theory (Tajfel and Turner, 1979) explains why groups become part of the self. It proposes that part of our self-concept comes from the groups we belong to, and that we are motivated to feel good about those groups. It describes three processes:

    1. Social categorisation: we sort people, including ourselves, into groups (the robotics team, the Year 11s, the people who play the same online game).
    2. Social identification: we take on the identity of our group, including its norms, its style and its way of talking.
    3. Social comparison: we compare our group with other groups, and we boost our self-esteem by seeing our in-group as better than out-groups.

    Adolescents are trying out possible selves, so group memberships can feel very important. This helps explain why a teenager may adopt a group's slang or mock another group, and also why a student who joins a debating club may start to see herself as "someone who argues well".

    The adolescent brain and social reward

    Why are teenagers so sensitive to peers? One biological explanation focuses on the brain's reward system. The ventral striatum, deep inside the brain, responds to rewards such as money, food and social approval. Research suggests that this system is especially responsive in adolescence. The prefrontal cortex, which supports planning and self-control, continues to develop into the early twenties.

    Chein et al. (2011) tested this with fMRI. They scanned 40 participants while they played a driving game called the Stoplight game: 14 adolescents aged 14 to 18, 14 young adults aged 19 to 22 and 12 adults aged 24 to 29. Each played once alone and once while two friends of the same age and sex watched from the next room. The adolescents, but not the adults, took more risks when watched, and the adolescents showed more activity in reward areas (the ventral striatum and the orbitofrontal cortex) during those decisions. Self-control areas were less active in adolescents than in adults, but this did not change when friends were watching. Peers did not switch off the brakes; they made the possible reward of a risky choice feel bigger.

    This corrects a common claim that teenagers simply cannot control themselves. When tested alone, the age groups in Gardner and Steinberg's study took similar numbers of risks in the driving game. The difference appeared when peers were present.

    Online peer influence

    Social media changes peer influence in three ways. Approval becomes a number (likes, views, comments). Social comparison never stops, and most of what you compare yourself with has been edited to look good. The audience is much larger than a friendship group.

    Sherman and colleagues (2016) used fMRI with adolescents who viewed photos in a feed that looked like a photo-sharing app. Each photo appeared to have many or few likes, although the researchers had assigned the numbers. Adolescents were more likely to like photos that already seemed popular, which is conformity to an online crowd. When they saw their own photos with many likes, reward areas of the brain, including the nucleus accumbens (part of the ventral striatum), were more active. The same reward system that responds to friends in the room seems to respond to approval on a screen.

    Real-life examples

    • The accent chameleon. A student from Australia joins an international school in Dubai where most of her new friends use American words. Within a term she says "elevator" instead of "lift" and "candy" instead of "lollies". Nobody asked her to change. Copying the group's way of speaking signals "I am one of you", which is normative influence and social identity at work.
    • Friends at the cycle track. A group of Grade 10 friends ride together on a cycling track outside the city. A boy who rides at a steady speed alone starts racing on the bends when his friends are behind him. The reward of their reactions makes the risk feel worth it, as the driving game studies predict.
    • Peer influence for good. In Ramadan, one friend in a group signs up to pack food boxes for a community iftar drive. By the end of the week, the whole group has joined. Being "the kind of group that helps" becomes part of how each of them sees themselves.

    Key study 1: Corsaro (1985), peer culture in a nursery school

    AimTo describe the peer culture that young children create among themselves, from the children's point of view rather than the adults'.
    ParticipantsTwo groups of about 25 children at a nursery school run by a state university child study centre in Berkeley, California. The morning group were aged about 2 years 10 months to 3 years 10 months, and the afternoon group about 3 years 10 months to 4 years 10 months.
    ProcedureAn ethnography with about a year of fieldwork. Corsaro first watched the children from a concealed observation area. He then entered the play areas as a participant observer. He did not direct or correct the children as a teacher would; he waited for them to approach him and followed their lead, and they came to treat him as an unusual "big kid". Over five months he also video recorded peer interaction at least twice a week, collecting 27 hours of recordings and 146 episodes of interaction.
    FindingsChildren often protected their play from others. Most first attempts by another child to join a game were turned away, so getting into play took time and strategy, such as watching nearby, copying the group's activity, and then asking. Children used friendship as a tool for access and exclusion ("We're friends, right?"), and "friend" usually meant the person you were playing with at that moment. Children also built shared routines and found ways around adult rules together.
    ConclusionYoung children actively create their own peer culture rather than simply copying adults. Inside it they practise belonging, inclusion and exclusion, and gain some control over their lives, which shapes an early sense of self as part of a group.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example of conformity or social identity theory in childhood); Paper 1 Section B; Paper 1 Section C on bias, measurement or perspective.
    Evaluate it withMeasurement: naturalistic observation and video recordings capture real behaviour in its everyday setting, and recordings can be checked again. Bias: one adult interpreted the children's behaviour, and the children came from one university nursery in California, so the peer culture may not be the same in other cultures or schools. Causality: an ethnography describes what happens; it cannot show that peer culture causes later differences in the self.

    Key study 2: Gardner and Steinberg (2005), peers and risk-taking

    AimTo test whether being with peers increases risk-taking and risky decision-making, and whether this effect is stronger in adolescents than in adults.
    Participants306 people in the United States in three age groups: adolescents aged 13 to 16 (average age about 14), youths aged 18 to 22 (average about 19) and adults aged 24 and older (average about 37).
    ProcedureParticipants came to the laboratory in groups of three with two friends of the same age. Within each age group, groups were randomly assigned to complete the tasks either alone or with the two peers in the room. The behavioural task was a computer driving game called Chicken. A car moved along a road; when a traffic light turned yellow, the player decided whether to keep driving to win more points, knowing that a wall could appear and cause a crash that wiped out the points for that round. In the peer condition, the two peers watched and could call out advice. Participants also completed two questionnaires: one on risk preference, and one on risky decisions in imagined situations.
    FindingsRisk-taking and risky decision-making decreased with age. Overall, people took more risks and focused more on the benefits than the costs of risky behaviour when peers were present, but the peer effect was stronger in the two younger groups than in the adults. In the driving game, adolescents' risk-taking was about twice as high with peers as alone, youths' was about 50% higher, and adults showed no difference. When playing alone, the three age groups took similar numbers of risks.
    ConclusionPeers increase risk-taking, and the effect is strongest in adolescence and declines into adulthood. Risky behaviour in adolescence is partly a social behaviour.
    What happened nextIn this study, peers could give advice, so it could not show whether their mere presence was enough. Chein et al. (2011) kept peers in another room, watching but silent during the task, and still found more risk-taking in adolescents. Silva, Chein and Steinberg (2016) found that when one of the peers in a group of young men aged 18 to 22 was replaced by a slightly older adult (aged 25 to 30), risk-taking dropped back to the level seen when people played alone.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example of conformity); Paper 1 Section B; Paper 1 Section C on causality or measurement; Paper 2 Section B practice (a true experiment with random allocation).
    Evaluate it withCausality: random allocation to "alone" or "with peers" supports a causal claim that peers increased risk-taking, but because peers could talk, the cause could be their advice, their presence, or both. Measurement: risk-taking was measured with points in a computer game and answers to imagined situations; it is not clear how far these reflect risks on a real road. Bias: all participants were from one country, and the "adults" group covered a very wide age range.

    How useful is research on peer influence?

    Strengths. Research on peer influence uses several methods that point the same way. Ethnography (Corsaro) shows peer culture in a real setting; experiments with random allocation (Gardner and Steinberg) show that peers can cause a change in behaviour; brain imaging (Chein et al.) suggests a mechanism. Together they are stronger than any one study. The research also has practical uses: schools can use positive peer influence through mentoring and team projects.

    Limitations. Outside the laboratory, it is very hard to separate influence from selection. Friends are often similar, but that may be because people choose friends who are already like them, not because friends change each other. Laboratory tasks are short and artificial, so they may not show how peers shape the self over years. Much of the research is from the United States and Europe, where independence from parents in adolescence is encouraged; in cultures where family remains the main influence, the balance between peers and parents may be different. Research has also focused on risk, which makes peer influence sound mainly harmful.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to peer influence
    MeasurementPeer influence is inferred from game scores, questionnaire answers, observed behaviour or brain activity; each captures only part of how peers shape the self.
    CausalityRandom allocation to "alone" or "with peers" supports causal claims in the laboratory, but in everyday life selection (choosing similar friends) is hard to separate from influence.
    BiasSamples are mostly American or European, ethnographic findings depend on one researcher's interpretation, and the focus on risky behaviour can make peer influence look only negative.
    ChangeSensitivity to peers rises in early adolescence and falls into adulthood, and the main mirror for the self shifts from parents towards peers.
    PerspectiveSocial comparison is a cognitive explanation, social identity theory is sociocultural, and reward sensitivity is biological; each explains a different part of peer influence.
    ResponsibilityResearchers must not put adolescents at real risk, which is why they use games. Schools, parents and app designers also share responsibility for how peer approval, such as likes, is used with young people.
