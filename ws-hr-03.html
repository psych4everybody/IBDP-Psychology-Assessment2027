    What conformity is

    Conformity is changing your beliefs, attitudes or behaviour to match those of a group. What sets it apart from other kinds of social influence is that no one asks. There is no request, and often no intention to influence you at all. You adjust because you see what the group does.

    Psychologists distinguish two reasons for conforming.

    • Informational influence: you are unsure, so you assume the group knows better. The more uncertain you are, the stronger the pull. Yusuf in the canteen is an example.
    • Normative influence: you want to be accepted, so you go along with the group even when you privately disagree. Yusuf's emoji is an example.

    Not every group pulls equally. The groups you identify with, your in-groups, pull hardest, because disagreeing with them costs something you care about. Chapter 5, on social identity theory, explains why.

    Not to be confused with compliance. In Chapter 2, someone asked. Here, no one does. If there is a request anywhere in the situation, check whether the behaviour answers that request, which is compliance, or simply matches what others are doing, which is conformity.

    Key study 1: Asch (1951), the line judgement task

    Imagine you have signed up for a vision test. You sit at a table with a group of other participants and are shown two cards: one with a single target line, and one with three comparison lines labelled A, B and C. Your job is to say aloud which comparison line matches the target. It is easy. The answer is obvious. What you do not know is that the others all work for the researcher, and on some trials every one of them confidently gives the same wrong answer.

    AimTo find out whether people would conform to a unanimous majority that gave an obviously wrong answer on a simple perceptual task.
    Participants50 male college students in the USA in the experimental condition, and a control group of 37 who did the task alone.
    ProcedureA laboratory experiment. Each participant sat with a group of confederates (people working for the researcher) and always answered last, or next to last. There were 18 trials. On 12 of them, the critical trials, the confederates all gave the same wrong answer. The control group judged the same lines without confederates. Afterwards, Asch told participants what had really happened and interviewed them about what they had been thinking.
    FindingsIn the control group, errors were under 1%. In the experimental condition, about a third (32%) of responses on critical trials matched the wrong majority. 74% of participants conformed at least once, and 26% never conformed at all.
    ConclusionA unanimous majority can lead people to give an answer they can see is wrong. But most responses were still correct, and a quarter of participants stayed independent throughout, so the study shows independence as well as conformity.
    What Asch learned from the interviewsParticipants who conformed seemed to do so for different reasons. Distortion of perception: a few seemed unaware that the group was wrong and believed its answers were correct. Distortion of judgement: they could see the group was wrong but began to doubt themselves; perhaps the others knew better. Distortion of action: they trusted their own eyes but gave the group's answer anyway, to avoid standing out in public.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example of conformity); Paper 1 Section B (informational and normative influence); Paper 1 Section C on bias or change; Paper 2 Section B (a laboratory experiment using deception).
    Evaluate it withBias: the participants were male American college students in the early 1950s, a narrow sample from one culture and one era. Measurement: conformity was measured as a public answer on a line task with a clearly correct answer, which is a very narrow version of everyday conformity. Change: the procedure was so widely repeated afterwards that it is now called the Asch paradigm, which lets researchers compare results across decades. Responsibility: the design relied on deception and put participants in the uncomfortable position of disagreeing with a group in public; harm in research is not only physical.

    Variations on the paradigm

    Asch and others changed one feature of the procedure at a time to see what affected conformity.

    • One ally changes everything. When one other person in the group gave the correct answer, conformity dropped sharply. Participants felt warmth towards this person, but often did not recognise that the ally had changed their own behaviour.
    • Group size matters, up to a point. Conformity rose as the majority grew, then levelled off: adding more people made little further difference.
    • Privacy reduces conformity. When participants wrote their answers down instead of saying them aloud, conformity fell. This suggests that much of the conformity in the original study was normative.

    Key study 2: Bond and Smith (1996), conformity across cultures and time

    AimTo find out whether conformity on Asch's line task varies between cultures, and whether it has changed over time.
    ParticipantsNo new participants. A meta-analysis of 133 studies that used Asch's line task, run in 17 countries.
    ProcedureThe researchers combined the results of the studies and tested which features were linked to higher or lower conformity, including the culture in which each study was run and the year it was carried out.
    FindingsConformity tended to be higher in collectivist countries than in individualist ones. In the USA, where the paradigm began, conformity had declined since the 1950s.
    ConclusionConformity is not a fixed amount. It varies with culture, and it can change over time within one culture.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section C on change or bias; links to Chapter 4 (individualism and collectivism).
    Evaluate it withChange: the decline in the USA suggests that Asch's 1951 result described its own time, not people in general. Bias: most of the studies were run in Western countries, so collectivist cultures were represented by fewer studies. Measurement: every study used the same line task, so the results are comparable, but they may not tell us about conformity in everyday life. Causality: a meta-analysis shows patterns; it cannot show which feature of a culture causes higher conformity.

    You will meet individualism and collectivism properly in Chapter 4, on cultural dimensions.

    Quick lens: change. Asch's findings come from one country in the early 1950s. Keep these questions open. You will come back to them in the Section C plan.

    • Would a classroom in Dubai today behave like a college class in the USA in 1951? What might make it conform more, and what might make it conform less?

    A biological basis?

    If conformity is so common, it may once have helped our ancestors survive. Pike and Laland (2010) studied nine-spined sticklebacks, a small freshwater fish. The fish first learned from their own experience which of two feeders gave more food. Then they watched other fish feeding in a way that suggested the opposite. When they were free to choose, many followed the other fish instead of their own experience, and they were disproportionately likely to copy what the majority did. Copying the majority may be an old and useful strategy, and not only a human one. Remember that this is animal research: a fish choosing a feeder is a long way from a student sending an emoji.

    Limitations of the research

    • The line task is a very narrow version of conformity. In everyday life, conformity takes many forms, and one simple perceptual judgement may not capture them.
    • The original participants were male college students in the USA in the early 1950s.
    • Textbooks have often reported the 74% who conformed at least once more than the 26% who never conformed, which can make Asch's study look like a study of giving in to the crowd when it also showed independence (Friend, Rafferty and Bramel, 1990).
    • The design relied on deception and public discomfort.

    Real life: show of hands or anonymous poll?

    Asch found that conformity fell when people could answer privately. Your class runs a version of this every time it makes a decision. A show of hands is public: everyone sees who votes first, and everyone sees you. An anonymous poll in the group chat is private.

    During the reading: think of a decision your class or a group you belong to made recently. Would the result have been different as an anonymous poll? What would that difference tell you about informational and normative influence?

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to conformity
    BiasAsch's sample was male American college students in the early 1950s; reports of the study have also tended to stress conformity and leave out independence.
    ChangeBond and Smith (1996) found that conformity in the USA declined after the 1950s, so a finding can describe its own era more than people in general.
    MeasurementConformity was measured as a public answer on a line task with an obvious correct answer; the reasons behind it came from interviews afterwards.
    CausalityAsch's variations changed one feature at a time (an ally, group size, privacy), which shows what causes conformity to rise or fall in the laboratory.
    PerspectiveConformity can be explained socially (informational and normative influence), culturally (individualism and collectivism) and in evolutionary terms (copying the majority, as in sticklebacks).
    ResponsibilityThe Asch paradigm used deception and public discomfort, so researchers must debrief participants and weigh the harm against what is learned.

    HL extension

    Technology: conforming to machines

    Salomons et al. (2021) sat 63 participants at a table with three small robots and played a game with no single correct answer: choosing which picture card best matched a word. In each of 20 rounds, participants gave a first answer, saw information about the robots' answers, then gave a final answer. On six critical rounds, all three robots agreed on an answer that differed from the participant's. Participants who were shown more information about the robots' answers changed their answer more often, and they also reported feeling more pressure from the robots. The researchers concluded that the robots produced both informational and normative conformity.

    If three small robots can shift your answer, what about an AI chatbot that replies instantly and sounds certain?
