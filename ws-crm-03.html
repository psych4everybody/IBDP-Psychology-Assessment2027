    What makes a study an experiment?

    Experiments

    An experiment is a research method in which the researcher deliberately changes one variable, keeps other variables as constant as possible, and measures the effect on a second variable. That combination of manipulation and control is what makes experiments the method best suited to testing cause and effect.

    • The independent variable (IV) is the variable the researcher changes. It is the presumed cause. Each version of it is called a condition.
    • The dependent variable (DV) is the variable the researcher measures. It is the presumed effect.
    • Controlled variables are kept the same in every condition, such as the room, the instructions, the materials and the time allowed, so that only the IV differs.

    Back to the warm-up. The books study measured two things and found that they go together, but it changed nothing. Families with more books may also have parents who read aloud more, more money, more time and more education, and any of these could raise vocabulary. To test the headline, you would need to give books to some families and not to others, with families placed into the two groups by chance, and then compare vocabulary scores later. That would be an experiment.

    Some experiments have an experimental group that receives the treatment and a control group that does not, which gives a baseline for comparison. Others compare two or more versions of the IV, for example studying with music versus in silence, with no "no treatment" group at all.

    Operationalising variables and writing hypotheses

    A variable is operationalised when you say exactly how it will be changed or measured. "Memory" is a construct; "the number of words correctly recalled from a list of 20 in 60 seconds" is an operationalised DV. "Music" is vague; "a recording of instrumental music played at the same volume throughout the task" is an operationalised IV. Operationalising matters for two reasons: another researcher can repeat (replicate) the study exactly, and readers can see what the results do and do not tell them. Any operationalisation leaves part of the construct out, which is always worth an evaluation point.

    A research hypothesis is a testable prediction about the effect of the IV on the DV, written with both variables operationalised.

    • A directional (one-tailed) hypothesis says which way the difference will go: "Participants who study a list of 20 concrete words will recall more words in 60 seconds than participants who study 20 abstract words."
    • A non-directional (two-tailed) hypothesis predicts a difference but not its direction: "There will be a difference in the number of words recalled..."
    • The null hypothesis states that the IV will have no effect on the DV, and that any difference found is due to chance.

    Researchers usually choose a directional hypothesis when earlier research points one way, and a non-directional one when it does not.

    Experimental designs

    The design is how participants are used across the conditions of the IV.

    DesignWhat it meansStrengthLimitation
    Independent measuresDifferent participants in each conditionNo order effects; participants see only one condition, so they are less likely to guess the aimParticipant variables (ability, mood, motivation) may differ between groups; needs more participants
    Repeated measuresThe same participants do every conditionParticipant variables are the same in each condition; needs fewer participantsOrder effects: practice, boredom or fatigue; seeing both conditions may reveal the aim
    Matched pairsParticipants are matched on a variable that matters (for example prior test scores), then one of each pair goes into each conditionReduces participant variables without order effectsMatching takes time and is never perfect; if one person drops out, the pair's data is lost

    Order effects in repeated measures can be reduced by counterbalancing: half the participants do condition A first and half do condition B first, so any practice or fatigue effect is spread evenly across conditions. Some studies cannot use repeated measures at all, for example when the same word list cannot be learned twice, or when knowing about one condition would change behaviour in the other.

    Random allocation is not random sampling

    These two terms sound alike and are confused in exams more than any other pair.

    • Random sampling is about who takes part: every member of the target population has an equal chance of being chosen. It affects how far the results can be generalised. It is rare in practice.
    • Random allocation is about which condition each participant is placed in, for example by drawing names from a container or using a random number generator. It spreads participant variables across conditions by chance, so no group is systematically different at the start. It affects whether the study can claim cause and effect.

    A study can have one without the other. A teacher who randomly allocates the students in one class (an opportunity sample) to two conditions has random allocation but not random sampling. Random allocation does not make the groups identical, especially in small samples; it only removes systematic differences. When participants are not randomly allocated, the study is a quasi-experiment, which you will meet in CRM-04.

    What threatens a causal claim?

    An extraneous variable is anything other than the IV that could affect the DV: noise, time of day, how tired participants are, how the instructions are read. If an extraneous variable changes along with the IV, it becomes a confounding variable, and the researcher can no longer tell which of the two caused the change in the DV. If the music group did the task in the morning and the silence group after lunch, time of day is a confound.

    Two further threats come from the people in the study.

    • Demand characteristics: cues that tell participants what the study is about, so that they change their behaviour, often to help (or sometimes to spoil) the result.
    • Researcher effects: the researcher's expectations influence the results, for example through tone of voice when giving instructions, or through how ambiguous answers are scored.

    Researchers use controls against these threats:

    • Standardisation: the same instructions, word for word, the same materials, the same timing and the same room for everyone.
    • Random allocation to deal with participant variables.
    • Single-blind procedures, where participants do not know which condition they are in, and double-blind procedures, where the researcher running the session does not know either.
    • A placebo condition in treatment studies, so that the effect of simply expecting to improve can be separated from the effect of the treatment.
    • A pilot study: a small trial run that finds problems with instructions, timing or materials before the real study.

    Three conditions must be met for a causal claim: the IV is manipulated, extraneous variables are controlled, and the change in the IV comes before the change in the DV.

    Laboratory and field experiments

    Laboratory experimentField experiment
    SettingAn artificial setting the researcher controlsA natural setting, such as a school, shop or street
    ControlHigh: extraneous variables can be kept constantLower: many things vary that the researcher cannot control
    Do participants know?Usually yesOften not, which reduces demand characteristics but raises ethical questions
    Ecological validityOften low: tasks may be short and artificialUsually higher: behaviour happens where it normally does

    This is the central trade-off of experimental research. Internal validity asks whether the study really showed that the IV caused the change in the DV. Ecological validity asks whether the findings apply to real-life settings. Tighter control usually raises internal validity and lowers ecological validity. Neither kind of experiment is "better": the right choice depends on the question being asked.

    Key study: Loftus and Palmer (1974), leading questions and memory

    AimTo find out whether the wording of a question asked after an event changes what eyewitnesses report about it.
    Experiment 145 university students in the USA watched seven short films of traffic accidents, each between 5 and 30 seconds long. After each film they answered questions, including the critical one: "About how fast were the cars going when they ___ each other?" Groups of nine heard different verbs: smashed, collided, bumped, hit or contacted. This is an independent measures design with five conditions.
    Findings 1Mean speed estimates rose with the strength of the verb: 40.8 mph for "smashed", 39.3 for "collided", 38.1 for "bumped", 34.0 for "hit" and 31.8 for "contacted".
    Experiment 2150 students watched one film of a crash. 50 were asked the speed question with "smashed", 50 with "hit", and 50 (a control group) were not asked about speed. A week later, without seeing the film again, all were asked: "Did you see any broken glass?" There was no broken glass in the film.
    Findings 216 of the 50 in the "smashed" group said yes, compared with 7 in the "hit" group and 6 in the control group.
    ConclusionThe wording of a question can change what people report. The second experiment suggests it may also change the memory itself: the verb added information that was then remembered as part of the event.
    Use it forThis chapter (variables, conditions, control groups); CRM-02 (the worked Concept Workout); schema theory (CL-03); Paper 2 Section B practice.
    Evaluate it withCausality: the groups saw identical films and differed only in the verb, so the wording can be identified as the cause of the difference. Measurement: speed estimates may show uncertainty more than memory, which is why the broken-glass question matters. Bias: all participants were university students watching films in a calm setting; real eyewitnesses are frightened and may be questioned days later.

    Real life: experiments outside the laboratory

    Real life
    • Medicine trials. New medicines are tested in randomised controlled trials: patients are randomly allocated to the medicine or a placebo, often double-blind, before a medicine is approved for use in the UAE or elsewhere.
    • Apps and websites. A food delivery app shows two versions of its checkout screen to users chosen at random and compares how many complete an order. This is an independent measures field experiment, often called an A/B test.
    • Schools. A school in Sharjah wants to know whether a ten-minute retrieval quiz at the start of lessons improves end-of-unit scores. Classes cannot be randomly reshuffled, so the comparison may be weaker than it looks.

    During the reading: choose one example. What is the IV, what is the DV, and what is one variable that would need to be controlled?

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to experiments
    CausalityManipulation, control and random allocation are what allow an experiment to claim cause and effect. Confounding variables are what break that claim.
    BiasDemand characteristics, researcher effects and unrepresentative samples can all push results in one direction. Blind procedures and standardisation are controls against bias.
    MeasurementThe DV has to be operationalised. Whether the operationalisation captures the construct (does a speed estimate measure memory?) is a validity question.
    ResponsibilityField experiments and hidden aims raise questions of consent and deception. Participants must be debriefed and allowed to withdraw their data.

    Practice study: word type and memory

    Practice study

    This is a short practice study to learn the method. It is not one of your class practicals for Paper 2. Your class practical for the experiment is in CL-11.

    Research question: does word type (concrete or abstract) affect the number of words recalled? Concrete words name things you can see or touch (table, river); abstract words name ideas or feelings (honesty, idea). One explanation for a difference is dual coding (Paivio, 1971): concrete words can be stored both as words and as mental images.

    1. Your teacher places you in the concrete or the abstract condition.
    2. Study your list of 10 words silently for 30 seconds.
    3. Filler task: count backwards from 200 in threes until your teacher says stop. This stops you rehearsing the words.
    4. Write down as many words as you can remember in 60 seconds, then count your correct words.
    5. Your teacher collects the class results and shares the mean for each condition.
    • My condition
    • My score (out of 10)
    • Class mean: concrete
    • Class mean: abstract
