    From findings to strategies

    Research on communication does not only describe relationships. It points to something that can be done. If patterns of communication predict whether a relationship will stay happy, then people can learn to recognise the early signs of problems and replace negative patterns with positive ones. Two strategies follow directly from Chapter 9.

    • Avoid triggering negative reciprocity by recognising its early signs, and use more constructive styles of conflict resolution, such as compromising and collaborating.
    • Recognise the signs of the four horsemen, and work with the other person to replace them with more constructive patterns.
    HorsemanReplace it with
    CriticismA complaint about a specific behaviour, starting with "I".
    ContemptRespect and appreciation, expressed openly.
    DefensivenessAccepting part of the responsibility.
    StonewallingCalming down before continuing. The house below includes self-soothing, which speaks directly to the intense emotions behind stonewalling.

    Many of these methods have been packaged into intervention programmes. One example is Gottman-method couples therapy.

    The Sound Relationship House

    John and Julie Gottman described the theory behind their therapy as the Sound Relationship House. In this model, two "weight-bearing walls", trust and commitment, hold a relationship together at all times. Trust lets people rely on each other. Commitment gives a sense of certainty that their intentions are serious and that problems are temporary. On top of the walls sit seven "floors": the things two people can work on to improve their relationship. Each floor rests on the one below it, and a couple who reach the top floor have a good chance of a lasting, sound relationship.

    The Gottmans describe the house for couples. Its ideas can also be used as a lens on friendships and teams, so the third column below is our own extension, written for practice. It is not something the research has tested.

    FloorWhat the two people doA version among friends or teammates
    7. Create shared meaningBuild rituals and stories that mean something to both of them.A team's pre-match ritual, or a friendship's running joke.
    6. Make life dreams come trueSupport each other's hopes and plans.Backing a friend's plan to apply for a place on a programme.
    5. Manage conflictDeal with conflict constructively: accept the other person's influence, talk problems through, and practise self-soothing (calming yourself down).Hearing the other person out. Taking a break to calm down before replying.
    4. The positive perspectiveMake positive attributions, recognising that circumstances, and not only the other person, may be responsible for a problem.Assuming a late reply means a busy evening, not that someone does not care.
    3. Turn towards instead of awayNotice when the other person needs attention, and respond.Answering when a friend is clearly struggling.
    2. Share fondness and admirationExpress respect and appreciation clearly.Telling a teammate what they did well.
    1. Build love mapsGet to know each other's personality, worries and world.Knowing a friend's plans and pressures.

    Walls: trust and commitment hold up every floor.

    Link to the content list: cognitive dissonance

    Cognitive dissonance is the discomfort we feel when what we do does not match what we believe or have said, and the pull to reduce that discomfort by changing one of the two. It is a Section A content term, and you met it in Chapter 2 behind commitment and consistency. Applied to strategies, it is one possible reason a routine can work: once two people have agreed out loud to show appreciation, acting against that agreement feels uncomfortable, so they are more likely to keep it. This is an application written for practice, not a finding from the research in this chapter.

    What the therapy involves

    Gottman-method couples therapy usually begins with an assessment: a joint session, individual interviews with each partner, and questionnaires. The therapist then designs a plan for the couple, followed by a series of sessions with specific assignments and tasks to practise at home. The therapy also includes education: the therapist teaches the clients evidence-based principles of healthy relationships. At its core is making the clients more aware of the important parts of their relationship, and of the positive and negative patterns in how they communicate.

    Key study 1: Rajaei, Daneshpour and Robertson (2019), Gottman-method therapy in Iran

    Several studies have tested whether therapy based on the Gottman method works. Most of the evidence comes from Western samples, so a study in another culture tests how far the method travels. This study focused on emotional divorce: a couple stay married officially, but become cold and distant and live largely separate lives, sometimes in the same home.

    AimTo test whether couples therapy based on the Gottman method reduces emotional divorce and improves verbal and non-verbal communication among couples in conflict in Iran.
    Participants14 married couples, all volunteers, who were seeking therapy at a mental health clinic in Iran.
    ProcedureA quasi-experimental design with a pre-test, a post-test and a wait-list control group. One group of couples received a course of couples therapy based on the Gottman method. The control group was placed on a waiting list and received the same therapy after the study. Both groups completed two questionnaires before and after the therapy period: an Emotional Divorce Scale and the Primary Communication Inventory, which measures verbal and non-verbal communication. The groups were compared using analysis of covariance, a test that takes account of differences between the groups at the start.
    FindingsCompared with the wait-list group, couples who received the therapy showed less emotional divorce and better verbal and non-verbal communication skills. The differences were statistically significant (p < .05).
    ConclusionGottman-method therapy may be effective outside the Western samples it was mainly developed and tested with, at least for couples who seek therapy.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section C on responsibility, change, bias or perspective; Paper 2 Section B practice (an intervention study with a wait-list control group).
    Evaluate it withBias: the sample was very small, 14 couples from one clinic, and all volunteered for therapy. Couples who choose this kind of therapy may already be more open to its ideas than other couples in Iran. In small therapy studies, the people delivering the therapy often know which couples are in which group, and may be part of the research team, so their expectations could shape the results. Causality: the wait-list control group helps rule out improvement that would have happened anyway, but in a quasi-experiment the groups may not have been equivalent at the start. Measurement: the outcomes were self-report questionnaires, completed by couples who knew whether they had received therapy. Change: a pre-test and post-test design shows change straight after therapy, not whether it lasts.

    Quick lens: Change

    Therapy is an attempt to change a relationship, and research on therapy is an attempt to measure that change. Keep these questions open. You will use them in Check yourself and in the Section C plan.

    • Patterns of communication predicted how satisfied couples would be. If a pattern predicts an outcome, does changing the pattern change the outcome, or does it only reveal what was already coming?
    • The questionnaires were completed before and straight after the therapy period. How long after therapy ends can you say that a change has lasted?
    • A method built with Western samples seemed to work in another culture. Does that show it is universal, or does it show something about the couples who volunteered?

    How useful are these strategies?

    Strengths. The strategies are built on longitudinal research on communication, so they target patterns that predict later satisfaction. They are concrete and teachable: a complaint instead of a criticism, a break instead of stonewalling. Therapy based on them can be tested with control groups, and a wait-list design lets researchers do this without leaving anyone without help for good. Studies such as Rajaei et al. (2019) begin to test whether the method works outside Western samples.

    Limitations. Many studies are small, use volunteers and rely on self-report. Follow-up periods are often short, so it is not clear whether change lasts. Applying the house to friendships, families or teams is an extension that has not been tested in the same way.

    Responsibility. Testing a strategy raises questions that a study of communication alone does not. A wait-list control means some people in real difficulty wait for help. Participants talk about sensitive parts of their lives, so informed consent, a clear right to withdraw and confidentiality matter. Researchers also have a responsibility to the public, who meet many sources of advice, from self-help books to social media, and need evidence-based voices among them.

    Real life: mediation at school

    Schools already use versions of these strategies. Here are three examples.

    1. Peer mediation: trained students help two classmates in a conflict find a solution they both accept.
    2. A restorative conversation: each person says what happened, how it affected them and what they need to move on.
    3. A team debrief after a lost match: players say what went well before saying what to change.

    For each one: which floors of the house does it use? What would the school need to show before claiming it works? And who is responsible if it does not help?

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to strategies for improving relationships
    ResponsibilityTesting a strategy means asking people about sensitive topics, sometimes recording them, and sometimes making them wait for help. This calls for informed consent, a clear right to withdraw and confidential data. Researchers are also responsible for being evidence-based voices for the public.
    ChangeWhat people expect from each other changes over a relationship. Communication patterns predict whether people stay satisfied, and therapy tries to change those patterns on purpose.
    BiasSamples are small and limited, volunteers differ from other people, the people delivering therapy may know who is in which group, and outcomes are usually self-reported.
    CausalityResearchers cannot ethically assign people to have aggressive conversations, but they can assign people to therapy or to a wait-list. That brings a study like Rajaei et al. closer to an experiment, although with 14 couples it cannot settle the question alone.
    Measurement"Improvement" has to be defined and measured, usually with questionnaires such as an emotional divorce scale, completed by people who know whether they received therapy.
    PerspectiveBiological, cognitive and sociocultural factors are different ways of looking at the same process. Testing a method in a new culture is a test of how far one perspective travels, and of who decides what "improving" a relationship means.

    The finding: In Rajaei et al. (2019), couples in the control group were put on a waiting list and received the same therapy only after the study.
