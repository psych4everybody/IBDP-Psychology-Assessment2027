    What is attachment?

    Attachment is a lasting emotional bond between a child and a caregiver. The child seeks to be close to that person, especially when frightened, tired or unwell. The British psychiatrist John Bowlby developed attachment theory from the 1950s onwards. He argued that attachment is an evolved system: infants who stayed close to a protective adult were more likely to survive, so behaviours that keep the caregiver near, such as crying, clinging, following and smiling, were selected over time. The caregiver who responds to these signals is the attachment figure. This is often a mother, but it can be a father, grandparent, older sibling or any adult who provides regular care.

    Bowlby also proposed that there is a sensitive period in the first years of life when attachments form most easily. You met the idea of critical and sensitive periods in an earlier chapter. Today most psychologists see this period as sensitive rather than critical: early bonds matter, but later experience can still change the picture.

    Two signs show that an attachment has formed: separation distress (upset when the attachment figure leaves) and stranger wariness (caution towards unfamiliar people). Both usually appear in the second half of the first year.

    Animal research: Harlow (1958). Before Bowlby, many psychologists assumed that infants become attached to whoever feeds them. Harry Harlow tested this with infant rhesus monkeys, which were raised with two artificial "mothers": one made of bare wire and one covered in soft cloth. In one group the wire mother held the milk bottle; in the other group the cloth mother did. Whichever mother gave milk, the infants spent far more time clinging to the cloth mother, and when they were frightened they ran to her. Harlow concluded that contact comfort, not food, was central to the bond. The work supported Bowlby's view, but it is animal research: monkeys are not humans, and the deprivation used in Harlow's later studies raised serious ethical concerns.

    The secure base and the internal working model

    Two ideas from attachment theory are central to this chapter.

    A secure base is the role an attachment figure plays when a child explores. The child moves away to play and investigate, checks back by looking or returning, and then explores again. If something frightening happens, the child returns to the attachment figure for comfort (a safe haven). Noura in the warm-up was using her mother as a secure base. Mary Ainsworth, who worked with Bowlby, described this pattern. The idea is that confident exploration depends on feeling safe.

    The internal working model (IWM) is Bowlby's term for the mental model a child builds from repeated experience with attachment figures. It is a kind of schema with two parts. The model of others answers the question "Will people be there when I need them?" The model of the self answers the question "Am I worth caring for?" A child whose distress is met with comfort, again and again, may come to expect that others are reliable and that they are worth caring for; a child whose distress is often ignored, or met unpredictably, may expect the opposite. Bowlby proposed that the IWM acts as a template that guides behaviour in later relationships. This is the main way attachment theory links attachment to the development of self.

    Ainsworth's attachment types

    Ainsworth developed a way to observe attachment under controlled conditions: the Strange Situation. A one-year-old and their mother spend about 20 minutes in an unfamiliar playroom while observers record the infant's behaviour across eight short episodes. A stranger enters, the mother leaves twice, and she returns twice. The episodes add a little stress, step by step, so that attachment behaviour becomes visible. Researchers pay most attention to reunion behaviour: what the infant does when the mother comes back.

    Ainsworth believed that the main cause of these differences was maternal sensitivity: how well a caregiver notices an infant's signals, reads them correctly and responds promptly and appropriately. This is a causal claim, and it has been tested many times (see below).

    How attachment may shape the developing self

    Attachment theory makes three predictions about the self. First, a secure child explores more, so they gain more experience of mastering new things, which may support a sense of competence. Second, the IWM gives the child a basic view of their own worth, which may underlie self-esteem. Third, the IWM shapes expectations of others, so it may influence how a child approaches friendships, teachers and later family relationships.

    These are predictions, and the evidence for them is mostly correlational. A child's attachment classification may be linked with later outcomes, but the same home that produced a secure attachment usually goes on providing warm care for years, so it is hard to know whether the early bond or the continuing environment matters more. Researchers have also extended the idea to adults. Hazan and Shaver (1987) used a questionnaire to ask whether adults' descriptions of their close relationships fit Ainsworth's three categories, and their findings suggested the categories may carry into adult close relationships, although the study relied on self-report and adults' memories of childhood.

    Real-life examples

    • Settling in at nursery. A nursery in Dubai might ask a parent to stay in the playroom for the first few mornings and then leave for longer each day. This lets the child use the parent as a secure base while getting to know a new caregiver, rather than facing a sudden separation.
    • Many attachment figures. In many families in the UAE, grandparents, aunts, older siblings or a nanny share the daily care of young children. A child can form attachments to several of these people. This raises a question for research that observes only the mother and infant: whose relationship is being measured?
    • Children in hospital. In the 1950s, the work of James Robertson and John Bowlby showed how distressed young children became when separated from their parents during hospital stays. This helped change hospital practice, and many children's wards today let a parent stay with a young child.

    Key study 1: Ainsworth and Bell (1970), the Strange Situation

    AimTo observe how one-year-olds balance attachment behaviour and exploration when the stress of an unfamiliar situation increases step by step.
    Participants56 white, middle-class infants in the USA, aged 49 to 51 weeks (about one year), and their mothers.
    ProcedureA controlled observation in an unfamiliar playroom with toys. There were eight short episodes, most lasting about three minutes: (1) mother, infant and observer enter; (2) mother and infant alone, infant free to explore; (3) a stranger enters, talks with the mother, then approaches the infant; (4) mother leaves, stranger stays; (5) mother returns, stranger leaves; (6) mother leaves the infant alone; (7) stranger returns; (8) mother returns. Observers behind a one-way mirror recorded behaviours such as exploring, crying, searching, seeking closeness, keeping contact, resisting contact and avoiding the mother.
    FindingsInfants explored most when the mother was present. Exploration dropped when she left, and crying and searching increased during the separations. When the mother returned, seeking closeness and keeping contact increased. In many infants, resisting contact also increased at reunion, alongside seeking it, which the authors read as ambivalence. Some infants avoided the mother when she returned.
    ConclusionThe mother acts as a secure base for exploration, and a mild stressor activates attachment behaviour. Infants differ in how they organise their behaviour at reunion, which suggests that attachment relationships differ in quality.
    What happened nextAinsworth, Blehar, Waters and Wall (1978) used these reunion patterns to define the three types. In their American samples, roughly 70% of infants were classed as secure, 20% as avoidant and 10% as resistant. The Strange Situation became the standard measure of infant attachment around the world.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example for the etic approach); Paper 1 Section B (secure base, separation and reunion behaviour); Paper 1 Section C on bias, measurement or causality; Paper 2 Section B (a controlled observation).
    Evaluate it withMeasurement: coding the same behaviours in the same eight episodes makes the procedure standardised and replicable, but it measures one relationship, with one caregiver, in about 20 minutes. Bias: the sample was 56 white, middle-class American families, and the idea that a secure infant explores independently may reflect American values. Causality: the study describes how infants behave; it does not show what caused the differences between them.

    Attachment across cultures

    Bowlby and Ainsworth argued that attachment is universal: all human infants are born ready to form attachments. Ainsworth's own early observations were made in Uganda, where she watched infants use their mothers as a base for exploration. So the theory has always claimed to apply everywhere.

    This is where the etic and emic approaches matter. An etic approach studies behaviour using concepts and tools that are assumed to apply across all cultures, often developed in one culture and then used in others. Using the Strange Situation, designed in the USA, to classify infants in Japan or Germany is an etic approach. An emic approach studies behaviour from inside a culture, using concepts that are meaningful to its members. When a tool is carried into a culture it was not designed for, researchers call this an imposed etic.

    Rothbaum and colleagues (2000) argued that three core ideas of attachment theory, sensitivity, the secure base and later competence, are understood differently in Japan. In the USA, sensitive care is often seen as care that supports a child's independence. In Japan, sensitive care is more often seen as anticipating a child's needs and keeping them close. The authors described the Japanese concept of amae, depending on and presuming on another person's care, and called for an indigenous (emic) approach to attachment. If they are right, the same behaviour in the Strange Situation may mean different things in different cultures.

    Key study 2: van IJzendoorn and Kroonenberg (1988), attachment across cultures

    AimTo compare the distribution of Strange Situation classifications (Types A, B and C) across countries, and within countries.
    ParticipantsNo new participants. A meta-analysis of 32 samples from eight countries, giving 1,990 Strange Situation classifications in total. The countries were the USA, the Netherlands, West Germany, Japan, Israel, Sweden, Great Britain and China. More than half of the samples came from the USA.
    ProcedureThe researchers collected the published percentages of each attachment type from every sample and used a statistical technique (correspondence analysis) to compare the patterns between countries and between samples from the same country.
    FindingsSecure attachment (Type B) was the most common classification in all eight countries, though not in every single sample: in one German sample, for example, avoidant attachment was the most common. Western European samples had relatively more avoidant classifications than expected, while Israeli and Japanese samples had relatively more resistant classifications. The USA fell between the two. The variation within countries was nearly 1.5 times the variation between countries.
    ConclusionThe general pattern, with secure attachment most common, appeared across cultures, which supports the idea of universality. Differences between samples within one country can be larger than differences between countries, so no single sample should be treated as representing a whole culture. The authors suggested that mass media spreading similar ideas about parenting might partly explain why differences between countries were fairly small, and they called for data from cultures in Africa, South America and elsewhere.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example for the etic approach or cultural dimensions); Paper 1 Section C on bias, perspective or measurement; arguments about whether attachment is universal.
    Evaluate it withBias: eight countries, mostly Western, with more than half the samples from the USA; many regions of the world are missing. Measurement: every sample used the Strange Situation, an American tool, so the study cannot tell whether the same behaviour means the same thing in every culture (an imposed etic). Causality: a meta-analysis describes patterns; it cannot show why Japanese or German samples differ, for example whether it is child-rearing practices or how unusual separation is for the infants.

    Does sensitivity cause secure attachment?

    Correlational evidence. De Wolff and van IJzendoorn (1997) combined 66 studies with more than 4,000 participants. In the studies that used the Strange Situation and observed sensitivity directly, the correlation between sensitivity and attachment security was about r = .24. That is a real but modest link. The authors concluded that sensitivity is an important but not exclusive condition for security: other parts of parenting matter too. A correlation also leaves alternative explanations open. An infant's temperament, for example, could affect both how easy the infant is to care for and how they behave in the Strange Situation.

    Experimental evidence. van den Boom (1994) worked with 100 lower-class Dutch mothers whose babies had been identified as irritable shortly after birth. Mothers were randomly assigned to an intervention group, who received three months of home visits designed to improve sensitive responding when the babies were 6 to 9 months old, or to a control group. At 12 months, 62% of the intervention infants were classed as securely attached, compared with 28% of the control infants. Because sensitivity was changed on purpose and families were randomly assigned, this result supports a causal claim much more strongly than a correlation can. It applies, however, to one particular group: irritable infants in lower-income families in the Netherlands.

    How useful is attachment theory?

    Strengths. It explains a pattern seen in infants across many cultures: closeness-seeking under stress and exploration from a secure base. It gave researchers a standardised, replicable observation, the Strange Situation, that has been used in many countries. Its central causal claim has experimental support, since improving sensitivity has increased secure attachment in randomised studies. It has changed hospital and nursery practice.

    Limitations. The link between attachment and the later self relies mostly on correlational and self-report evidence, so it is hard to separate the effect of the early bond from the effect of a home that stays warm or difficult over many years. The theory may underplay temperament and the other relationships that shape a child. The Strange Situation measures one relationship, usually with the mother, and ideas such as "sensitivity" and "competence" may reflect Western values more than universal ones.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to attachment
    MeasurementAttachment cannot be seen directly; it is inferred from coded behaviour in a short, standardised observation, especially reunion behaviour.
    CausalityLinks between sensitivity, attachment and the later self are mostly correlational; van den Boom (1994) used random assignment to test whether sensitivity causes security.
    BiasEarly samples were white, middle-class Americans, and most cross-cultural samples are Western; the Strange Situation may be an imposed etic.
    ChangeAttachment can change when caregiving or family circumstances change, which is why Bowlby's sensitive period is no longer seen as fixed.
    PerspectiveThe theory combines an evolutionary view with a cognitive one (the IWM as a schema); cultural psychologists argue for an emic view of what security means.
    ResponsibilityAttachment research has shaped advice to parents, nurseries and hospitals; researchers must avoid blaming caregivers for a child's difficulties when evidence of cause is limited. Harlow's animal research raises questions about the treatment of animals.
