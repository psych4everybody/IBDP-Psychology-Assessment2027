    Learning by watching

    Social learning theory (SLT) proposes that people learn by watching other people, not only by doing things themselves and being rewarded or punished. Albert Bandura developed the theory from the 1960s onwards. Learning by watching is called observational learning. The person who is watched is the model. Modelling is the process of showing a behaviour that someone else can observe and copy. When the observer copies it, we call this imitation.

    Models can be live, such as a parent, a teacher or an older cousin, or symbolic, such as a person in a film or a video. People learn skills, habits, emotional reactions and attitudes this way.

    SLT is a sociocultural theory because it places learning in our relationships with other people. It is also cognitive: Bandura argued that what happens in the observer's mind, between watching and copying, decides whether learning takes place.

    How social learning theory differs from conditioning

    Classical and operant conditioning both explain learning through the learner's own direct experience. In operant conditioning, for example, a behaviour becomes more likely when it is followed by a reward. SLT accepts that conditioning happens, but it adds three ideas.

    • Learning without direct reinforcement. A person can learn a new behaviour just by watching it, even if nobody rewards them. Rashid in the warm-up is an example.
    • Consequences for someone else matter. Seeing a model rewarded or punished can change what the observer does. This is vicarious reinforcement, explained below.
    • Learning is not the same as performance. A person can learn a behaviour and not show it until there is a reason to.

    The four mediating processes

    Bandura (1977) described four mental processes that sit between observing a model and imitating the model. They are called mediating processes because they mediate (come in between) the stimulus and the response. If any one of them fails, the behaviour is not copied. The example follows Hamdan, a Grade 10 student in Dubai, watching a video of a professional player's free kick.

    ProcessWhat it meansHamdan and the free kick
    AttentionThe observer has to notice the behaviour. Models who stand out, have high status or seem similar to the observer get more attention.Hamdan watches the player's run-up closely instead of scrolling past it.
    RetentionThe behaviour has to be stored in memory, as a mental image or as words, so it can be used later.Hamdan tells himself: "Three steps, plant the left foot, strike low."
    ReproductionThe observer has to be physically and mentally able to perform the behaviour. Practice and feedback improve this.Hamdan can copy the run-up but cannot yet curl the ball, so he practises after school.
    MotivationThe observer needs a reason to perform the behaviour. This depends on the consequences they expect, including consequences they have seen happen to the model.Hamdan sees the player's teammates celebrate the goal, and he wants the same in his school team.

    The first three processes are about learning the behaviour. The fourth is about performing it.

    Vicarious reinforcement: learning from what happens to others

    Vicarious reinforcement happens when an observer sees a model being rewarded for a behaviour and becomes more likely to perform that behaviour. Vicarious punishment works the other way: seeing a model punished makes the observer less likely to perform the behaviour.

    Bandura (1965) tested this. Children watched a film of an adult model being aggressive towards a Bobo doll, an inflatable clown that rocks back up when it is knocked over. In one condition the model was rewarded, in another the model was punished, and in a third there were no consequences. Children who saw the model punished copied fewer of the aggressive acts. Then every child was offered attractive rewards for showing what the model had done. Now the differences between the groups mostly disappeared: the children who had seen the model punished had learned the behaviour all along. Vicarious punishment had changed performance, not learning.

    Watch out: Bandura, Ross and Ross (1961), the key study below, had no reward or punishment condition. Vicarious reinforcement comes from the 1965 study.

    Identification: who do we copy?

    We do not copy everyone we see. Identification is when an observer feels similar to a model, or wants to be like them, and so is more likely to adopt the model's behaviour. SLT predicts that people are more likely to imitate a model who:

    • is similar to them, for example the same sex or a similar age;
    • has high status or seems competent, such as a team captain or a respected teacher;
    • is seen being rewarded for the behaviour.

    In Question 2 of the warm-up, the unknown boy is not someone Rashid identifies with, and he was told off, so SLT predicts Rashid is less likely to copy him than Omar.

    Self-efficacy

    Bandura (1977) later added the idea of self-efficacy: a person's belief that they can succeed at a particular task. It affects whether people try a behaviour, how much effort they put in, and how long they keep going. Bandura named four sources of self-efficacy:

    • Performance accomplishments: succeeding at the task yourself.
    • Vicarious experience: watching someone like you succeed.
    • Verbal persuasion: being told by others that you can do it.
    • Physiological states: how calm or anxious your body feels when you think about the task.

    Vicarious experience links self-efficacy to modelling: watching a similar person succeed says "if they can do it, so can I". Changing behaviour often depends on raising this belief, not just showing someone how.

    Real-life examples

    • The football academy. At a weekend academy in Dubai, young players copy the warm-up routine and goal celebration of a famous player they admire. His status gets their attention, and they identify with him because they want to be like him.
    • The seatbelt. A child in Abu Dhabi watches a parent fasten their seatbelt every time before starting the car. Years later, when that child learns to drive, they reach for the seatbelt without thinking. The habit was learned long before it was performed.
    • The assembly. A Grade 12 student is praised at assembly for organising a beach clean-up in Fujairah. Younger students who see the praise are more likely to volunteer the next time a clean-up is announced. This is vicarious reinforcement: they were not praised, but they saw someone else praised.

    Key study 1: Bandura, Ross and Ross (1961), the Bobo doll study

    AimTo test whether children would imitate aggression they had seen an adult model perform, in a new setting where the model was no longer present, and whether the sex of the model made a difference.
    Participants72 children (36 boys and 36 girls) from the Stanford University Nursery School in the USA, aged 37 to 69 months (mean age 52 months, a little over four years).
    ProcedureA laboratory experiment with matched groups. Before the study, the experimenter and a nursery teacher rated each child's usual aggressiveness. Children were matched in threes on these ratings and randomly allocated to conditions. 24 children saw an aggressive model, 24 saw a non-aggressive model, and 24 formed a control group who saw no model. Within the two model conditions, half the children saw a same-sex model and half saw an opposite-sex model. Each child spent about 10 minutes in a playroom with the adult model. The aggressive model attacked a Bobo doll in distinctive ways (sitting on it, punching it on the nose, hitting it with a mallet, tossing it in the air and kicking it) and made aggressive comments. The non-aggressive model played quietly with construction toys and ignored the doll. Next, every child was mildly frustrated: they played with very attractive toys for about two minutes and were then told that these toys were being kept for other children. This gave every child some reason to feel aggressive. Finally, each child spent 20 minutes in another room with aggressive toys (including a Bobo doll and a mallet) and non-aggressive toys (such as crayons and dolls). Observers watched through a one-way mirror and recorded behaviour every 5 seconds.
    FindingsChildren who had seen the aggressive model copied many of the model's specific physical and verbal aggressive acts. Children in the non-aggressive and control conditions showed almost none of these imitative acts; about 70% of them scored zero. Boys showed more imitative physical aggression than girls, but there was no sex difference in imitative verbal aggression. Boys imitated the male aggressive model more than the female one.
    ConclusionObservation alone can produce new behaviour, without any reinforcement for the child or the model. The sex of the model, and whether the behaviour seemed typical for that sex, affected how much was copied.
    What happened nextBandura, Ross and Ross (1963) found that children also imitated aggression shown by an adult on film and by a cartoon character, so symbolic models work too. Bandura (1965) then added rewards and punishments for the model and showed vicarious reinforcement. All three used a similar short task with a toy made to be hit.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section B (observational learning, identification); Paper 1 Section C on causality or responsibility; Paper 2 Section B practice (a laboratory experiment with matched groups).
    Evaluate it withCausality: matching on aggressiveness, random allocation and a control group mean the model is the most likely cause of the difference in imitation. Measurement: aggression was measured as acts against a doll that is designed to be knocked over, so some of it may have been play rather than aggression. Bias: all the children came from one university nursery school, so the sample may not represent other children. Responsibility: the researchers deliberately showed young children aggression and then frustrated them, which raises ethical questions about protecting children from harm.

    Applying social learning theory to change behaviour

    If behaviour can be learned by watching, it can also be changed by watching. Psychologists use modelling to teach new behaviour and to reduce fear. There are three main forms:

    • Live modelling: the person watches a real model, such as a therapist calmly holding a feared animal.
    • Symbolic modelling: the person watches a filmed or recorded model.
    • Participant modelling: the person watches a model, then tries the behaviour step by step with the model's support.

    With fears, the observer sees a model approach the feared thing with no bad consequences. This weakens avoidance and raises self-efficacy. Bandura, Blanchard and Ritter (1969) compared treatments for adults with a strong fear of snakes and found that participant modelling was the most effective. It combines two sources of self-efficacy: vicarious experience and performance accomplishments.

    A good behaviour change plan uses all four mediating processes:

    • Attention: a model the learner identifies with, such as a peer.
    • Retention: repeat the behaviour in clear steps.
    • Reproduction: graded practice, starting with an easy step.
    • Motivation: show the model rewarded and build self-efficacy.

    Key study 2: Bandura and Menlove (1968), modelling to reduce fear of dogs

    This is the application study. It shows social learning theory being used on purpose to change behaviour.

    AimTo test whether filmed models could reduce young children's avoidance of dogs, and whether several models worked better than one.
    ParticipantsYoung children who showed a strong fear of dogs.
    ProcedureThe children's avoidance was measured by how far they would go in approaching and interacting with a real dog. Each child was then placed in one of three conditions and watched a series of eight short films. In the single-model condition, the films showed one child interacting more and more boldly with one dog: approaching it, petting it, feeding it and playing with it. In the multiple-model condition, the films showed several different children with several different dogs. In the control condition, the children watched films that did not include dogs. Approach behaviour with a real dog was measured again after the films and a month later.
    FindingsChildren who watched the modelling films became more willing to approach and interact with a real dog than children in the control condition. Watching several models with several different dogs produced the strongest effect. The improvements were still present a month later.
    ConclusionSymbolic modelling can reduce avoidance behaviour, and seeing the behaviour modelled by different people in different situations makes the change stronger. Social learning theory can be applied to change behaviour.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section B (applying SLT to change behaviour); Paper 1 Section C on change or causality.
    Evaluate it withCausality: the control group also watched films, so the change cannot be explained just by watching films or by time passing; the content of the films is the likely cause. Measurement: fear was inferred from approach behaviour in a test, not from what the children felt; a child might approach a dog because adults were watching. Change: the follow-up shows the change lasted at least a month, but not whether it lasted longer or carried over to dogs in the street. Perspective: it studied young children and one fear, so applying it to teenagers or to fear of public speaking needs other evidence.

    How useful is social learning theory?

    Strengths. It explains learning that conditioning cannot: children in the Bobo doll studies learned new behaviours without being reinforced themselves. It is supported by experiments with control groups, which allow causal claims. It makes testable predictions, for example about which models are copied most. It has practical uses, such as modelling therapy and peer mentoring. It also combines social factors (the model) with cognitive factors (the mediating processes).

    Limitations. The mediating processes cannot be observed. Researchers infer attention or retention from what people later do. The classic studies used short, artificial tasks and measured behaviour minutes after watching, so they say little about long-term change. Outside the laboratory, people watch many models at once and differ in temperament and biology, so it is hard to show which model caused a behaviour. SLT also does not fully explain individual differences, such as why boys in the 1961 study showed more physical aggression.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to social learning theory
    MeasurementMediating processes and fear are never observed directly; they are inferred from what people do, such as hitting a doll or approaching a dog.
    CausalityControlled experiments show that watching a model caused imitation in a test room; showing this in everyday life is much harder.
    BiasThe classic studies used young children from one university community, and the task (a toy made to be hit) may have pushed children towards aggression.
    ChangeModelling can change behaviour on purpose, as in Bandura and Menlove's work on fear of dogs, and self-efficacy helps explain why change lasts or fades.
    PerspectiveSLT combines a sociocultural view (learning from others) with a cognitive one (mediating processes), but says little about biological factors such as temperament.
    ResponsibilityIf people learn from models, parents, teachers and media producers are responsible for what they show. Researchers must not expose children to harm.

    Rung 4: One more question

    The finding: In Bandura and Menlove (1968), children who were afraid of dogs became more willing to approach a real dog after watching films of other children playing with dogs, and the gains were still there a month later.

    A first reaction: "So watching other children with dogs cures fear of dogs." Before you accept that, ask one more question.

    • The control group also watched films. What possible causes does that rule out, and what does it leave open?
    • Approaching a dog in a test, with adults watching: does it show the child feels less afraid when meeting a dog in the street?
    • The films might have taught the children how to approach a dog, or made them believe they could. Can this study tell which of these caused the change?
