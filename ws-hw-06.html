    The health problem: social media addiction

    In this workbook, the health problem we study is social media addiction. It is important to be accurate about the term. Social media addiction is not a diagnosis in the DSM-5. Researchers study it as a possible behavioural addiction, and many prefer the term problematic social media use. The question is not how many hours a person spends online. It is whether the use has started to cause problems that the person finds hard to control.

    Many researchers describe these problems using Griffiths' (2005) components model of addiction. It has six signs: salience (social media dominates your thoughts), mood modification (you use it to change how you feel), tolerance (you need more and more of it), withdrawal (you feel restless or irritable when you cannot use it), conflict (it causes problems with school, sleep or family) and relapse (you try to cut down and go back to old habits). Questionnaires such as the Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale ask about these six signs.

    Research links problematic social media use with poorer sleep, lower mood and worse concentration. Most of this research is correlational, so it cannot tell us on its own which comes first. In this chapter we ask a narrower question: how much of the problem is learned from other people?

    A quick recap of social learning theory

    Social learning theory

    You met social learning theory (SLT) in Chapter 2 of the Cognition and Learning workbook. Albert Bandura proposed that people learn by observing others, not only through their own rewards and punishments. You also met Bandura, Ross and Ross (1961), in which children copied aggression they had only watched, which showed that observation alone can produce new behaviour. Four ideas from the theory matter most for this chapter.

    • Modelling. A model shows a behaviour that an observer can copy. Models can be live (a parent at the dinner table) or symbolic (a creator on a screen). Copying only happens if the four mediating processes work: attention, retention, reproduction and motivation.
    • Vicarious reinforcement. Seeing a model rewarded makes the observer more likely to perform the behaviour. Bandura (1965) showed this by rewarding or punishing a filmed model.
    • Identification. We are more likely to copy a model we feel similar to, admire, or want to be like.
    • Self-efficacy. Bandura (1977) defined this as a person's belief that they can succeed at a particular task. Its four sources are performance accomplishments, vicarious experience, verbal persuasion and physiological states.

    SLT also separates learning from performance. A child can learn a behaviour by watching it years before they perform it. Many children watch adults use phones long before they own one.

    Who are the models?

    Social media use is very easy to observe. Phones are used in public, at home and in front of children, and the rewards (likes, comments, laughter at a video) are often visible to everyone. SLT predicts three main groups of models.

    ModelWhat they modelSLT ideaExample
    Parents and older family membersWhen, where and how much phones are used: at meals, in the car, in bedLive modelling over many years; learning before performanceA child who sees a parent check messages at every red light learns that the phone comes first, even before they own one
    Peers and siblingsWhich apps, trends and posts are "normal", and what gets approvalIdentification with similar others; vicarious reinforcementA Grade 9 student sees a classmate's post get hundreds of likes and copies the same style of video
    Influencers and creatorsA lifestyle in which constant posting is normal and rewardedSymbolic modelling; high status; vicarious reinforcement through follower countsA teenager follows a creator's daily routine, which includes posting several times a day

    Influencers are a special case. Followers often feel they know a creator personally, even though the creator does not know them. Horton and Wohl (1956) called this one-sided bond a parasocial relationship. In SLT terms, it strengthens identification. A model you feel close to gets more of your attention, and you are more motivated to copy them.

    Likes as reinforcement

    A like is a small, fast social reward. SLT and operant conditioning together suggest it works in two ways.

    • Direct reinforcement. When your own post gets likes, the behaviour of posting (and checking) is reinforced. This is operant conditioning, which you met in Chapter 1 of the Cognition and Learning workbook.
    • Vicarious reinforcement. When you see a friend's post collect likes, you see a model being rewarded. SLT predicts you become more likely to post in the same way. Like counts make this easy, because the reward is public and comes with a number.

    Likes are also unpredictable. You do not know whether the next check will bring a new like or nothing. Because the reward depends on checking, and an unpredictable number of checks pays off, this works much like a variable ratio schedule of reinforcement, which produces a high rate of responding that is hard to extinguish. This helps explain why checking is so hard to stop.

    SLT adds one point that operant conditioning misses. A person does not need to post anything to learn that posting is rewarded. Watching others is enough to set up the expectation, and expectations drive motivation, the fourth mediating process.

    Self-efficacy: believing you can cut down

    Social learning also helps explain why some people find it hard to change. Many teenagers say they want to use social media less. Whether they try, and whether they keep going, depends partly on their self-efficacy for controlling their use. A student who has tried to cut down three times and failed has low self-efficacy from performance accomplishments. A student whose whole friend group is online all evening has no vicarious experience of anyone like them managing with less.

    This links SLT to the relapse component of addiction. Low self-efficacy makes relapse more likely, and each relapse lowers self-efficacy further. It also points to prevention: if self-efficacy has social sources, it can be built through social means, such as a peer who models cutting down successfully. You will look at prevention and treatment in a later chapter.

    Real-life examples

    • Influencers need a permit. From 1 February 2026, people who advertise on social media from within the UAE, paid or unpaid, must hold an Advertiser Permit from the UAE Media Council and show their permit number on their profile. There are some exemptions, and permit holders must follow set content standards. In SLT terms, the law treats influencers as powerful models whose behaviour reaches many observers.
    • An age limit for accounts. In June 2026, a UAE Cabinet resolution under the Child Digital Safety Law (Federal Decree-Law No. 26 of 2025) set rules that children under 15 may not create or operate social media accounts, with extra protections for older children. Platforms were given a transition period of up to 12 months to comply. This places part of the responsibility on platforms, not only on families.
    • The phone basket. Imagine a family in Sharjah who decide that everyone, parents included, puts their phone in a basket during dinner. If the children see their parents keep to the rule, the parents are modelling the behaviour they want, not only setting a rule. Hefner et al. (2019), below, suggests this difference matters.

    Key study 1: Sherman et al. (2016), the power of the like

    AimTo investigate how the number of likes on a photo affects adolescents' own liking behaviour and their brain activity, in a setting that looked like real social media.
    Participants32 adolescents aged 13 to 18, scanned at the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) in the USA.
    ProcedureA laboratory experiment using fMRI. Participants were told they were joining a small social network similar to Instagram, and they submitted their own photos. In the scanner, they viewed 148 photos over 12 minutes: 40 of their own photos, plus neutral photos (such as food and friends) and risky photos (such as people smoking). Each photo showed a number of likes, supposedly from other teenagers in the network. In fact the researchers set the numbers, so that half the participants saw a given photo with many likes and the other half saw it with few. For each photo, participants could like it or move on. At the end, they were told the like counts had been assigned by the researchers.
    FindingsParticipants were more likely to like a photo when it showed many likes. This happened for neutral, risky and their own photos, and the effect was strongest for their own photos. Seeing their own photos with many likes was linked to more activity in the nucleus accumbens, part of the brain's reward circuitry, and in regions linked to social thinking and visual attention. Viewing risky photos, compared with neutral ones, was linked to less activity in regions involved in cognitive control and response inhibition.
    ConclusionLikes act as a reward in the adolescent brain, and visible peer approval changes what teenagers themselves approve of, even when the "peers" are strangers.
    What happened nextSherman et al. (2018) added a sample of 27 university students. They also liked photos with many likes more often and showed the same nucleus accumbens response to their own popular photos, so the main findings replicated in an older group.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section B (likes as reinforcement, peer influence); Paper 1 Section C on causality, measurement or responsibility.
    Evaluate it withCausality: the researchers manipulated the like counts, so the like count caused the change in liking, but only in a 12-minute laboratory task. Measurement: reward was inferred from brain activity; activity in the nucleus accumbens is not the same as addiction. Bias: 32 teenagers at one American university is a small sample from one culture. Responsibility: participants were deceived about the likes and shown risky photos, so the researchers had to debrief them at the end.

    Key study 2: Hefner et al. (2019), rules, role model or relationship?

    AimTo investigate which parental factors are linked to children's problematic mobile phone involvement: the rules parents set, the example parents set with their own phone use, or the quality of the parent and child relationship.
    Participants500 children aged 8 to 14 in Germany, each surveyed together with one parent (500 parent and child pairs, recruited by quota sampling).
    ProcedureA correlational survey. Children and parents answered questionnaires. These measured four things: the parent's own problematic phone involvement; how the parent manages the child's phone use (for example, rules, restrictions and using the phone together); how secure the child's attachment to the parent is; and the child's own problematic phone involvement, such as feeling a growing need to use the phone. The researchers then tested which parental factors were linked to children's problematic involvement.
    FindingsParents who showed more problematic phone involvement themselves tended to have children with more problematic phone involvement. A secure, positive parent and child relationship went with less problematic involvement. In the wider research project this paper comes from (Knop et al., 2015), restrictive rules mainly affected whether children could use the phone at all, and did much less to prevent problematic use. Overall, parents' own example and the relationship mattered more than the rules they set.
    ConclusionParents may shape children's phone habits more through modelling and relationships than through rules, which fits the social learning explanation.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section B (parents as models); Paper 1 Section C on causality or responsibility; Paper 2 Section B practice (a correlational survey with parent and child pairs).
    Evaluate it withCausality: the data are correlational, so they do not show that parents' phone use causes children's problematic use; the influence could run both ways, or families could share other factors such as stress or genes. The research team called for longitudinal studies to test cause and effect. Measurement: it measured problematic mobile phone involvement, which overlaps with social media addiction but is not the same thing, and everything was self-reported. Bias: parents may underreport their own phone problems. Responsibility: it suggests parents carry some responsibility through their example, not just their rules.

    How useful is social learning theory for understanding social media addiction?

    Strengths. It explains how social media habits spread through families and friend groups without anyone deliberately teaching them. It fits the evidence: Sherman et al. showed experimentally that visible peer approval changes teenagers' behaviour, and Hefner et al. found that parents' own phone habits go with their children's. It explains why some models matter more than others, through identification and status. It also has practical uses: if the problem is partly learned from models, prevention can use models too, such as parents keeping to their own rules or older students modelling healthy use.

    Limitations. Most of the evidence outside the laboratory is correlational. A parent and child who both overuse their phones may share genes, stress or a busy home, not only a model. SLT also struggles to explain individual differences: many teenagers watch the same models and see the same likes, but only some develop problematic use. Other factors are involved, including biological reward sensitivity, low mood, stress and loneliness. SLT also says little about app design. Endless feeds, notifications and like counts are built by companies, and they reinforce use directly through operant conditioning, whether or not anyone is watching a model. Finally, the classic SLT studies measured copying minutes after watching, while addiction develops over months.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to social learning and social media use
    MeasurementProblematic use is measured by self-report questionnaires, and reward is inferred from brain activity. Phone involvement and social media addiction overlap but are not identical.
    CausalitySherman et al. manipulated like counts, so they can make a causal claim about liking in the laboratory. Hefner et al. is correlational, so it cannot show that parents' use causes children's use.
    BiasBoth key studies used Western samples (American teenagers, German families), and parents may underreport their own phone problems.
    ChangeAdolescence is a period when reward circuitry is especially sensitive. Habits learned from models can also be changed by new models and by building self-efficacy.
    PerspectiveSLT is sociocultural and cognitive. A biological perspective points to the reward system, and an operant perspective points to app design.
    ResponsibilityIf social media habits are learned, responsibility is shared between parents, peers, influencers, platforms and the user. Researchers who deceive participants, as Sherman et al. did, must debrief them.
