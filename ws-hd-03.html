    Development happens in a social world

    Some theories describe the child as a "little scientist" who works out the world mostly alone, by exploring and testing. Jean Piaget's stage theory (Chapter 2) gives social interaction a smaller role than this. The sociocultural approach, which began with the Russian psychologist Lev Vygotsky (1896 to 1934), starts from a different idea. It argues that thinking develops through interaction with other people, inside a particular culture, using the tools that culture provides.

    Vygotsky argued that skills first appear between people and only later appear inside the child. A parent helps a child count the plates for dinner, pointing and saying the numbers out loud. Later the child counts alone, still saying the numbers. Later still, the child counts silently. The skill has moved from the social level to the individual level. This process is called internalisation.

    A sociocultural factor is anything in a person's social and cultural surroundings that can shape development: parents, siblings, peers, teachers, language, schooling, everyday family routines, and the values of the community. This chapter looks at how these factors work, and at the claim that culture shapes not only how children learn, but also what "being well developed" looks like.

    The zone of proximal development

    The zone of proximal development (ZPD) is the gap between what a child can do alone and what the child can do with help from an adult or a more capable peer (Vygotsky, 1978). "Proximal" means "next" or "nearest". The ZPD is the set of skills a child is close to mastering.

    • A task the child can already do alone is below the ZPD. Repeating it adds little.
    • A task the child cannot do even with help is above the ZPD. Help does not make it learnable yet.
    • A task the child can do with help is inside the ZPD. This is where Vygotsky said development is pushed forward.

    The helper can be a parent, a teacher, an older sibling or a classmate. Many textbooks call this person the more knowledgeable other (MKO). The term came later: Vygotsky wrote about adults and "more capable peers". Notice two points. First, the ZPD is different for each task: a child can be far ahead in reading and behind in maths. Second, two children who score the same when tested alone may have very different ZPDs. One may need a single hint; the other may need the whole task broken down. Vygotsky thought that what a child can do with help tells you more about development than a test taken alone.

    Scaffolding

    Vygotsky did not use the word "scaffolding". The term comes from Wood, Bruner and Ross (1976), who watched an adult tutor help young children build a pyramid from interlocking wooden blocks. Scaffolding is temporary support that lets a learner complete a task they could not complete alone, and which is removed as the learner becomes more able. Like the scaffolding on a building site, it holds things up while the structure is built, and then it comes down.

    Wood and colleagues described several things a good tutor does. These include getting the child interested, simplifying the task so it is manageable, keeping the child on track, pointing out the important features, controlling frustration, and demonstrating. Good scaffolding is also contingent: the tutor gives more help when the child is struggling and less help when the child succeeds. Removing support step by step is called fading. A swimming coach who moves from holding a child, to armbands, to a float, to nothing is fading the scaffold.

    Guided participation

    Barbara Rogoff, an American psychologist, extended Vygotsky's ideas in her book Apprenticeship in Thinking (Rogoff, 1990). She argued that much learning does not look like a lesson at all. Children learn by taking part in the real activities of their families and communities, with more experienced people guiding them. She called this guided participation. Like an apprentice working beside a skilled worker, the child is active: watching, joining in, and taking on more responsibility over time.

    Guided participation is broader than scaffolding. The guidance can be silent: a look, a gesture, handing over a tool at the right moment, or simply letting the child stay close enough to watch. In her later work, Rogoff described a way of learning common in many Indigenous communities of the Americas, which she calls learning by observing and pitching in. Children keep close attention on what adults are doing and join in when they can, without being called over for a lesson.

    Language and culture as tools

    Vygotsky argued that every culture hands its children tools for thinking. Some are physical, such as a ruler, a calendar or a calculator. Others are psychological, such as language, number systems, writing and maps. A child who learns the word "half" can think about sharing in a way that a child without the word cannot. A child who learns to write can store a thought and come back to it later.

    For Vygotsky, language was the most important tool. Young children often talk to themselves out loud while they work: "No, not that one. The blue one goes here." This is called private speech. Vygotsky saw it as a step between social speech (a parent talking the child through a task) and inner speech (silent thinking in words). Private speech helps children plan and control their own behaviour. Over time it goes quiet, but it does not disappear. Many adults still mutter instructions to themselves when a task is hard.

    Culture also passes on values and habits. Enculturation is the process by which a person learns the norms, values and practices of their own culture. It happens through direct teaching, through observation, and through taking part. A child in the UAE learns how to greet elders, how to behave in a majlis, and which language to use with which relative, mostly without a formal lesson. Chapter 6 looks at enculturation of social norms in more detail. Social learning theory (Bandura) helps explain part of this: children learn by observing models and imitating behaviour that they see others carry out. Guided participation adds that children are not only watching from outside. They are taking part, and the adults around them shape how far they can join in.

    Parenting and schooling practices across cultures

    If development is social, then the way adults support children should differ between communities, because communities have different goals for children. Rogoff and colleagues found that middle-class mothers in the United States and Turkey more often engaged their toddlers in language lessons and school-like quizzes about objects than mothers in a tribal community in India and in a Mayan community in Guatemala, where schooling was not widespread. Rogoff and colleagues (2007) also point out that mothers with more years of Western-style schooling tend to use school-like ways of teaching their own children, such as praise, lessons and breaking a task into parts, even within the same community.

    This suggests that schooling is itself a sociocultural factor. In school, children are often separated from adult work and taught skills out of context, using talk, questions and tests. Families that have been through many years of schooling may bring these habits home. In communities where children spend more time near adult work, learning by watching and pitching in may be more common. Neither pattern is "correct". Each fits the goals and daily life of the community.

    Culture shapes what development looks like

    Culture does not only change the speed of development. It changes what counts as a capable child. In one community, a capable three-year-old answers questions, names colours on request and talks confidently to adults. In another, a capable three-year-old watches carefully, helps with real chores and does not interrupt adults. In a chapter reporting the Guatemalan and US part of their research, Rogoff and colleagues reported household chores for 14 of the 16 toddlers.

    This matters for how we judge research. If a researcher from one culture uses their own culture's idea of "good teaching" as the standard, other cultures may look as if they are lacking something. This is ethnocentric bias. Rogoff and colleagues warned that Western research's focus on talk may reflect a cultural bias that overlooks the information given by silence, gaze, posture and touch. A sociocultural approach asks what children are learning in their own setting, and what that setting expects of them.

    Real-life examples

    • Swimming lessons in Dubai. A coach at a community pool holds a five-year-old in the water, then moves to armbands, then a float, then stands back. This is scaffolding with fading. If the coach moves the child into the deep end too early, the task is above the ZPD and the child panics instead of learning.
    • Three languages at home. A child in Abu Dhabi speaks Arabic with her grandparents, English at school and Urdu with a family helper. Each language is a cultural tool, and she learns which one fits which relationship. That is enculturation into a multilingual society, learned mostly by taking part rather than by being told.
    • Homework with an older brother. A Grade 4 student in Sharjah is stuck on long division. His Grade 9 brother does the first problem with him, then only checks the second, then just watches the third. The brother is acting as a more capable peer, and his help is contingent and fading.

    Key study 1: Freund (1990), the dollhouse sorting task

    AimTo investigate how mothers regulate (guide) young children's problem-solving, and whether working with a mother helps children perform better on their own afterwards than working alone.
    ParticipantsChildren aged 3 to 5 and their mothers.
    ProcedureThe task was to help a puppet decide where furniture should go in the rooms of a dollhouse, a sorting task. Children were first assessed on how well they could sort the furniture alone. They then practised a sorting task in one of two conditions: with their mother, or on their own. Finally, every child completed a harder sorting task alone.
    FindingsChildren who had practised with their mothers performed better on the harder task than children who had practised alone. Research by Freund and others also suggests that mothers take on more responsibility, and give more direct prompts, as tasks become harder.
    ConclusionGuided help from a more capable person, adjusted to the child, leads to better independent performance than practising alone. This supports Vygotsky's idea that learning inside the ZPD, with support, pushes development forward.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section B (guided learning in a scenario); Paper 1 Section C on causality or perspective.
    Evaluate it withCausality: both groups did the same final task after the same baseline check, so the difference can be linked to the practice condition. But "working with a mother" includes many things at once: a familiar person, talk, encouragement and demonstration. The study cannot tell us which part made the difference. Measurement: success on one sorting task measures one narrow skill, on one day. Perspective: the findings support a sociocultural view over pure discovery learning, but they do not show that children never learn by exploring alone.

    Key study 2: Rogoff, Mistry, Göncü and Mosier (1993), guided participation in four communities

    AimTo compare how toddlers and their caregivers take part in shared activities in different communities, and to examine a key cultural difference: who is responsible for the child's learning.
    Participants14 toddlers aged 12 to 24 months in each of four communities (56 in total), with their caregivers: a Mayan town in Guatemala, a middle-class urban group in Salt Lake City (United States), a tribal village in India, and a middle-class urban neighbourhood in Turkey.
    ProcedureResearchers visited each family at home. They interviewed caregivers about child-rearing and video-recorded caregivers helping toddlers to work novel objects. In the detailed Guatemala and Salt Lake City analysis, these included a wooden nesting doll, playdough to shape into a tortilla or a burger, and a plastic baby doll to "take care of". Mothers were asked to get their toddlers to work the objects. Fathers, siblings and grandparents were often present. The recordings were analysed for patterns of communication and attention.
    FindingsIn every community, caregivers and toddlers worked together: caregivers adjusted the objects, simplified tasks and handled the difficult steps. Middle-class mothers in the United States and Turkey more often gave language lessons and school-like quizzes. In the detailed comparison of 8 Mayan and 8 Salt Lake City families, Salt Lake City caregivers talked far more, used test questions, acted as teachers and playmates, and praised the toddler's performance in about half of the episodes. Mayan caregivers relied more on gaze, posture and timing, often kept their hands ready to help, and praised once across 23 episodes. Mayan toddlers attended to several events at once more often (an average of 2.1 episodes, compared with 0.4 for Salt Lake City toddlers).
    ConclusionGuided participation may be universal, but communities differ in the goals of development and in how children and adults are involved. In some communities, adults structure lessons for children. In others, children take responsibility for learning by watching and taking part in adult activities, with support. The authors linked this to how far children are separated from adult work and how much the community emphasises schooling.
    What happened nextCorrea-Chávez and Rogoff (2009) tested 120 children aged 5 to 11 in sibling pairs, in three groups: Mayan children from San Pedro, Guatemala, whose mothers had little schooling; Mayan children whose mothers had about 12 years of schooling; and European American children in California. While one sibling was taught to make a toy, the other waited nearby and was not addressed. Mayan children from families with little schooling paid sustained attention in 62.4% of the time segments, compared with 30.6% for European American children. About 10 days later, they needed less help to make the harder toy (an origami frog). This separates two factors that the 1993 study could not: Mayan heritage and family schooling.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section B (culture in a scenario); Paper 1 Section C on bias, perspective or causality; Paper 2 Section B practice (a naturalistic observation across cultures).
    Evaluate it withBias: 14 families per community is a small sample, and families were not randomly selected. The authors themselves warned that a focus on talk may reflect Western cultural bias. Causality: culture cannot be manipulated, and the communities also differed in wealth and schooling (the Salt Lake City families were more affluent and more educated), so we cannot say which factor caused the differences. Measurement: the coding categories (for example "praise" or "attention to several events") were built by the researchers, and the published chapter describes the analysis as preliminary, without a test of whether different observers coded the videos the same way.

    How useful is the sociocultural approach?

    Strengths. It explains why children of the same age differ so much in what they can do: their social worlds give them different support and different goals. It has clear practical uses. Teachers use the ZPD and scaffolding to set tasks at the right level and to fade support, and parents can be shown how to give contingent help. It also protects against ethnocentric bias, because it asks what a skill means in the child's own community before calling it advanced or delayed. Research using different methods (an experimental comparison in Freund, cross-cultural observation in Rogoff et al., and a controlled comparison of attention in Correa-Chávez and Rogoff) points in the same direction.

    Limitations. The ZPD is hard to measure precisely, so some claims are difficult to test. The approach can underplay biological factors such as brain maturation (Chapter 1): a toddler cannot be scaffolded into reading. Cross-cultural studies compare communities that differ in many ways at once, so it is hard to say that "culture" caused a difference. Comparisons such as "Western versus Mayan" can also hide large differences within each community. Correa-Chávez and Rogoff (2009) found that Mayan children from families with more schooling sat between the other two groups on attention.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to sociocultural factors in development
    MeasurementThe ZPD and guided participation cannot be seen directly. Researchers infer them from performance after help (Freund) or from coded behaviour on video (Rogoff et al.).
    CausalityFreund could compare practice conditions, but culture cannot be assigned. Cross-cultural differences are confounded with schooling and wealth, which is why Correa-Chávez and Rogoff compared two Mayan groups.
    BiasEthnocentric bias: judging one culture's way of teaching by another culture's standard. Small, non-random samples limit how far findings generalise.
    ChangeSkills move from the social level to the individual level (internalisation). Practices also change across generations as schooling spreads.
    PerspectivePiaget's "little scientist" and Vygotsky's "apprentice" are different perspectives on the same child. A sociocultural perspective may say less about biology.
    ResponsibilityRogoff's key question is who is responsible for learning: the adult who structures lessons, or the child who observes and joins in. Researchers also have a responsibility not to label a community's parenting as lacking.
