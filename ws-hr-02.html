    What compliance is

    Compliance techniques

    Compliance is a change in behaviour in response to a request. The request is what makes it compliance. When you conform, you change your behaviour because the people around you behave a certain way, but no one has asked you to. When you comply, someone has asked.

    The request can be explicit, like a call for donations, or implicit, like an advertisement. You may not even know you are the target. A compliance technique is a specific strategy for getting the response someone wants. Foot-in-the-door and low-ball are the two you will study here, but there are many others.

    Not to be confused with conformity. Conformity is Chapter 3, and the two are easy to blur. Use one test: was there a request? If someone asked, it is compliance. If you changed to match the group without anyone asking, it is conformity. Mixing them up is an accuracy error, and accuracy counts in every question.

    Cialdini's six principles

    Robert Cialdini spent years working undercover in sales, fundraising and advertising training programmes, watching how professionals got people to say yes. From those observations and the research around them, he proposed six principles that sit underneath most compliance techniques (Cialdini, 1984).

    PrincipleThe ideaWhere you might meet it
    ReciprocityWhen someone does us a favour, we feel we should return it.A free sample at a mall kiosk, followed by a pitch for the full-size product.
    Commitment and consistencyOnce we commit to something, we want our actions to stay consistent with it.Taking a car for a test drive, or clicking "remind me later" instead of "no".
    Social proofWe follow what people around us, especially people like us, are doing.A "most popular" badge on a menu, or "1,200 people bought this today".
    AuthorityWe trust people who look like experts.An advert in which an actor in a white coat recommends a toothpaste.
    LikingWe say yes more easily to people we like, or who seem similar to us.An influencer your age, from your city, recommending a brand.
    ScarcityThings seem more valuable when they seem rare."Only two left in stock", or a limited trainer release.

    Commitment and consistency connect to cognitive dissonance, the discomfort we feel when what we have said and what we then do do not match. That discomfort pushes us to bring them back into line, often by doing what we said we would.

    Key study 1: Freedman and Fraser (1966), foot-in-the-door

    In the foot-in-the-door technique, a small request comes first. Once the person has agreed, a larger target request follows. Agreeing to the small request makes agreeing to the large one more likely.

    AimTo test whether agreeing to a small request makes people more likely to agree to a larger request later.
    ParticipantsWomen in California, chosen at random from the telephone directory.
    ProcedureA field experiment. The women were randomly allocated to four conditions and were telephoned by a researcher who said he worked for a consumers' group. The target request was large: to let a team of five or six men into their home for about two hours to list every household product they used, with freedom to look through cupboards and storage. Whether each woman agreed was the dependent variable.
    Performance: a few days before the large request, a small request to answer a few questions about the soaps they used. They answered them.
    Agree-only: the same small request. They agreed, but the questions were never asked.
    Familiarisation: a first call to introduce the caller and the group, with no request.
    One-contact: no first call; the large request came straight away.
    FindingsAgreement to the large request was 52.8% in the performance condition, 33.3% in agree-only, 27.8% in familiarisation and 22.2% in one-contact. Performance was significantly higher than one-contact. Its lead over familiarisation fell just short of the usual level of significance (p < .07), and familiarisation hardly differed from one-contact. Agree-only fell between performance and one-contact and was not significantly different from either.
    ConclusionCarrying out a small request more than doubled compliance with a large one. Simply knowing the caller is unlikely to explain the whole effect, and actually doing the small task seemed to matter most.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example of a compliance technique); Paper 1 Section B (applying foot-in-the-door); Paper 1 Section C on causality or responsibility; Paper 2 Section B (a field experiment).
    Evaluate it withCausality: random allocation and the familiarisation condition make familiarity an unlikely full explanation, although that comparison only just missed significance. Measurement: compliance was measured as saying yes on the phone; the team never actually visited, so we do not know how many women would have let them in. Bias: only women at home in one part of the USA in the 1960s took part. Responsibility: participants did not know they were in a study and were deceived about the consumers' group.

    Quick lens: causality. This was a true experiment with random allocation, so it can say more about cause than a correlation can. Be precise about what it shows. You will come back to these questions in the Section C plan.

    • Which condition rules out the explanation that people simply comply more with someone they have already met?
    • What does the agree-only condition tell us that the other three cannot?
    • The experiment shows that a small request first raises compliance. Does it show why? Was commitment and consistency something the study measured, or an explanation added afterwards?

    Key study 2: Cialdini et al. (1978), the low-ball technique

    In the low-ball technique, a person agrees to a request and only afterwards learns that it will cost more than they thought.

    AimTo test whether people who have already agreed to a request will stick to it when they learn it will cost them more than they first thought.
    Participants63 introductory psychology students at a large state university in the USA, chosen at random from class lists and telephoned.
    ProcedureAn experiment. Students were telephoned and asked to sign up for a study in return for course credit. In the control condition, they were told straight away that the study would start at 7 am. In the low-ball condition, they were first asked whether they would sign up. Only those who said yes were then told about the 7 am start, and asked again whether they would take part. Researchers recorded two things: who signed up (verbal compliance) and who actually turned up at 7 am (behavioural compliance).
    FindingsSigned up: 56% in the low-ball condition, 31% in the control condition. Turned up at 7 am: 53% in the low-ball condition, 24% in the control condition. In both conditions, most of the students who agreed also turned up.
    ConclusionOnce people have committed to a decision, many stick with it even when the cost goes up. Low-ball raised both what people said and what they did.
    What happened nextThe same paper reported a field experiment with 30 male graduate students living in single rooms in a university residence. A researcher posing as a charity worker asked them to display two charity posters, with low-ball, foot-in-the-door and control conditions (10 students in each). Agreement was similar (80% in the low-ball condition, 70% in the other two), but the next day 6 of the 10 low-ball students had put the posters up, against 1 of 10 in the foot-in-the-door condition and 2 of 10 in the control condition. This suggests that low-ball is not just a version of foot-in-the-door, and that it can work outside the laboratory, with people who did not know they were being studied.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section A (an example of a compliance technique); Paper 1 Section B (applying low-ball); Paper 1 Section C on causality or responsibility.
    Evaluate it withCausality: the two conditions differed only in when the 7 am start was revealed, so that timing is the likely cause of the difference. Measurement: the study measured behaviour (turning up), not only what students said, which makes the finding stronger. Bias: psychology students may have felt more pressure to help a psychology study than other people would. Responsibility: holding back the real cost of a request until after a yes is the technique itself, so applying it outside research raises questions about honesty.

    Foot-in-the-door or low-ball? Both start with something easy and end with something harder, so they are easy to confuse. The difference is the first request. In foot-in-the-door, the first request is a different, smaller behaviour. In low-ball, the first request already is the target behaviour. Only its cost changes.

    Using compliance techniques to change behaviour

    Compliance techniques can be used on purpose to change the behaviour of a group, for example to raise donations, recycling or volunteering. The research points to some conditions.

    • Foot-in-the-door tends to work best when the two requests are on the same topic and come from the same person or organisation.
    • When the goal is action, not just a yes, follow-through matters. In the residence study, low-ball led to far more posters actually going up than foot-in-the-door did.
    • Public commitments may be harder to back out of. The posters in the residence study were on public view. Could that have mattered?

    Limitations of the research

    • Many studies used psychology students. What works with them may not work with everyone else.
    • In laboratory studies, participants know they are being studied and may change their behaviour to fit what they think is expected.
    • Each study turns compliance into one particular behaviour, with one request, one requester and one time delay. Only many studies across many situations can show how far a technique holds.
    • Most of the classic studies were run in the USA. Nothing guarantees that a technique that works in one culture, or one generation, works the same way in another.

    Real life: "only two left".

    A shopping festival, a delivery app late at night, a mall promotion: all of them use Cialdini's principles. Countdown timers lean on scarcity. "Bestseller" badges lean on social proof. A free first delivery leans on reciprocity.

    Some of this is simply good selling. Some of it is not. UAE consumer protection law (Federal Law No. 15 of 2020) does not allow businesses to mislead consumers. A sign saying "only two left" when the shelf behind it is full is not a clever use of psychology. It is a misleading claim.

    During the reading: think of one offer you saw this month. Which principle was it using, and was it honest? Where would you draw the line between a good offer and a misleading one?

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to compliance techniques
    MeasurementCompliance is measured as one behaviour, such as saying yes on the phone or turning up at 7 am; what people say and what they do can differ.
    CausalityField and laboratory experiments with random allocation show that a technique raised compliance; why it works, for example commitment and consistency, is usually inferred, not measured.
    BiasMost classic studies used American participants, often psychology students, in the 1960s and 1970s.
    ChangeTechniques can be used on purpose to change group behaviour, such as recycling or donating; whether the change lasts once the requests stop is a separate question.
    PerspectiveCialdini's principles combine a social view (the request and the requester) with a cognitive one (the need to stay consistent).
    ResponsibilityThe same technique can fill a food bank or sell something people do not need; field studies also deceived participants who did not know they were being studied.

    HL extension

    Technology: dark patterns

    Online, compliance techniques run automatically and at scale. Designers call some of them dark patterns: a sale timer that starts again when you reload the page, a message that 14 people are looking at this right now, a box already ticked to sign you up for emails, a streak you will lose if you skip a day. Each one maps onto a principle from this chapter.

    No researcher is in the room when these are used, and nobody is debriefed afterwards. So who is responsible: the designer, the company, or the psychology the design was built on?
