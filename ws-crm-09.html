    Descriptive statistics: summarising numbers

    Descriptive statistics and graphs

    Descriptive statistics summarise a set of scores. They describe the data you have; they do not test anything.

    StatisticWhat it isWhen to use it
    MeanAdd all the scores and divide by how many there areMost informative when data are roughly symmetrical; pulled up or down by extreme scores
    MedianThe middle score when scores are in order (with an even number, halfway between the two middle scores)Better than the mean when data are skewed or contain outliers
    ModeThe most common score or categoryThe only average that works for categories, such as the most popular answer to a survey item
    RangeHighest score minus lowest scoreA quick measure of spread, but one extreme score can change it a lot
    Standard deviation (SD)Roughly, the typical distance of scores from the meanA small SD means scores cluster near the mean; a large SD means they are spread out
    Semi-interquartile range (SIQR)Half the distance between the lower quartile (the score a quarter of the way up the ordered list) and the upper quartile (three quarters of the way up)The spread of the middle half of the scores. Extreme scores do not affect it, so it is the measure of spread to report with the median when data are skewed

    Worked example: the scores 12, 14, 14, 16 and 19 have a mean of 15, a median of 14, a mode of 14 and a range of 7. Reporting a mean without any measure of spread hides how much people differed, which is why good results sections give "mean = 15, SD = 2.5" and not just the mean.

    Choosing and reading graphs

    Descriptive statistics and graphs
    Graph or tableUse it forWatch out for
    Frequency tableHow many people fall in each category or scoreCompare percentages, not raw counts, when groups are different sizes
    Bar graphComparing separate categories or conditions, such as mean recall in two conditions; bars do not touchBars showing means should have error bars or SDs, or you cannot judge the overlap
    HistogramThe distribution of continuous scores, in equal-width bands; bars touchThe shape tells you about skew and outliers
    Box and whisker plotThe median, the middle 50% of scores (the box, from the lower to the upper quartile) and the spread, with outliers shown separatelyA box plot can hide a distribution with two peaks
    Line graphChange over time or across ordered steps, such as recall after 0, 10 and 30 minutesDo not join points for unordered categories: the line implies a continuum
    ScatterplotThe relationship between two co-variables; each dot is one participantIn a correlational study there is no IV or DV, just two variables

    When you read any graph, check the axis labels and the scale first. An axis that does not start at zero can make a small difference look dramatic.

    Distributions and outliers

    A distribution is the shape the scores make when plotted.

    • In a normal distribution, scores cluster in the middle and tail off evenly on both sides, in a bell shape. The mean, median and mode are roughly the same.
    • In a positively skewed distribution, most scores are low and a long tail stretches to the right. This often happens when a task is too hard (a floor effect). The mean is usually pulled above the median.
    • In a negatively skewed distribution, most scores are high and the tail stretches to the left, often because a task is too easy (a ceiling effect). The mean is usually pulled below the median.

    A memory aid: the tail tells the tale. The skew is named after the side the tail is on. A skewed distribution is not "bad data"; it tells you something about the task or the participants, and it tells you to report the median.

    An outlier is a score far from the rest. It might be genuine, or it might come from a misunderstood instruction or a recording error. Outliers pull the mean and inflate the SD. One common rule treats a score as an outlier if it lies more than 1.5 times the interquartile range beyond the box. Never remove an outlier just because it is inconvenient: if a score is excluded, the reason must be stated and the decision reported.

    Inferential statistics and significance

    Inferential statistics

    Inferential statistics test whether a difference or relationship found in a sample is likely to reflect a real pattern, or could easily have arisen by chance. The test starts from the null hypothesis (no effect) and produces a p-value.

    A p-value is the probability of getting a result at least as extreme as the one found if the null hypothesis were true. In psychology, results with p < .05 are usually called statistically significant: if there were really no effect, results this strong would turn up less than 5% of the time.

    Three things significance does not tell you:

    • That the IV caused the result. Significance in a correlational study, or in a poorly controlled experiment, does not show cause.
    • That the effect is large or important. With 10,000 participants, a difference of 0.3 points on a 100-point test can be significant but educationally meaningless. The size of an effect is a separate question from whether it is significant.
    • That the result is certain. A Type I error is finding a significant result when there is no real effect; a Type II error is missing a real effect, which is more likely with small samples.

    Which test is used depends on the design and the data:

    TestWhat it looks forWhen it is used
    Unrelated t-testA difference between two conditionsTwo separate groups (independent samples) and scores on a scale, such as words recalled
    Related t-testA difference between two conditionsThe same people in both conditions (repeated measures) or matched pairs, with scores on a scale
    Mann-Whitney UA difference between two conditionsTwo separate groups, when the data are ranks or ratings, or are skewed
    Wilcoxon TA difference between two conditionsRepeated measures or matched pairs, when the data are ranks or ratings, or are skewed
    Chi-squareA difference or association in frequenciesCounts in categories, such as how many students in each group answered yes or no
    Correlation coefficientA relationship between two co-variablesTwo measures taken from each participant, such as hours of sleep and a test score

    Effect size tells you how big a difference or relationship is, which significance alone does not. One common measure for a difference between two means is Cohen's d, where about 0.2 is usually called small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large; for a relationship, the correlation coefficient itself is a measure of effect size.

    You do not need to calculate tests by hand, but HL students should be able to read a reported result: for some tests, such as the Mann-Whitney U, a result is significant when the calculated value is smaller than the critical value, so always check the rule for the test.

    A correlation coefficient (r) from -1 to +1 gives the direction and strength of a straight-line relationship between two co-variables (CRM-07). It cannot detect curved relationships, and it never shows cause on its own.

    Thematic analysis: analysing words

    Thematic analysis

    Thematic analysis finds patterns of meaning in qualitative data such as interview transcripts, focus group discussions, open survey answers or diaries, and groups them into themes that answer the research question. A widely used version, set out by Braun and Clarke (2006), has six phases:

    1. Familiarisation: read and re-read all the data, noting first impressions.
    2. Coding: attach short labels (codes) to meaningful pieces of text. "I break big tasks into smaller steps" might be coded "breaking tasks down".
    3. Searching for themes: group related codes into possible themes, such as "planning as a way of coping".
    4. Reviewing themes: check that each theme fits the coded data and the whole data set, and that themes are distinct.
    5. Defining and naming themes: say clearly what each theme captures.
    6. Writing up: report each theme with short quotes as evidence.

    Themes are built by the researcher, not simply found, so two researchers may produce different themes from the same data. This is why credibility, reflexivity and transparency matter (CRM-08). Some approaches have a second researcher code part of the data to check agreement; others treat the researcher's interpretation as part of the method and focus instead on reflexivity.

    Describe first, then interpret

    These are two different moves, in the IA and in the exam.

    DescribingInterpreting
    What it doesStates what the data show, with numbers and patternsExplains what the data mean, in relation to the research question and theory
    Example"Mean recall was higher in the concrete condition (7.2) than in the abstract condition (4.5).""This fits dual coding theory, which predicts that concrete words can be stored as images as well as words."
    Common mistakeAdding "because..." too earlyClaiming more than the design allows, such as "proves" or "always"

    Paper 3 Question 2 rewards exactly this: analyse the data accurately, then draw a conclusion that stays within what the data and the design can support.

    Real life: numbers in the news

    Real life
    • "Average" salaries. A few very high earners pull the mean salary up, which is why the median is a fairer picture of a typical worker.
    • Exam results. A school reports that its mean grade rose. Without the spread, you cannot tell whether most students improved or a few improved a lot.
    • Graphs on social media. A chart with the y-axis starting at 95% can make a 2% change look huge.

    During the reading: choose one example and explain which statistic or graph feature you would check, and why.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies
    MeasurementChoosing the right average, spread and graph is a measurement decision. Skew, outliers and missing SDs change what the numbers can tell you.
    CausalityStatistical significance is not causation. Only the design (manipulation, control, random allocation) can support a causal claim.
    BiasRemoving inconvenient outliers, reporting only significant results, or choosing a misleading graph can distort findings. Themes in qualitative analysis can reflect the researcher's expectations.
    ResponsibilityReporting results honestly, including null results and uncertainty, is part of a researcher's responsibility to the public.

    Paper 2, Section B warm-up [6 marks]

    Study (invented for practice): 40 Grade 10 students were randomly allocated to a mindfulness group (n = 20) or a control group (n = 20). The mindfulness group attended a one-hour session each week for six weeks; the control group had no sessions. Anxiety was measured before and after with a validated 20-item scale (0 to 80; higher means more anxious).

    • Mindfulness group: before, mean 38.4 (SD 6.2); after, mean 31.7 (SD 7.1). A drop of 6.7 points.
    • Control group: before, mean 37.9 (SD 5.8); after, mean 36.1 (SD 6.3). A drop of 1.8 points.

    The difference between the groups after six weeks was statistically significant (p = .04). The researchers concluded that the programme is an effective treatment for anxiety in teenagers and recommended it for every Grade 10 class.

    Evaluate this study with reference to the concepts of measurement and causality. [6]

    Look at the standard deviations, the size of the effect and what the control group did, before you begin.

    Using AI to learn

    Make AI question you, not write for you

    Try one of these after you have attempted the chapter yourself:

    • "Give me a small set of invented scores with one outlier. Ask me to calculate the mean and median and say which is more representative. Do not give me the answer first."
    • "Show me a described graph with a misleading feature. Ask me to find the problem."
    • "Here are my codes and themes for five interview answers. Ask me two questions that test whether my themes really fit the data. Do not suggest new themes."

    Remember: check every study an AI tool names against this chapter or your textbook, because these tools sometimes invent studies and figures. Never paste in your class data or anyone's name, and do not use AI to draft or mark your IA or EE.
