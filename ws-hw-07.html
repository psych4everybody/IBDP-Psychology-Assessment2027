    Stressors and the stress response

    A stressor is an event or situation that places demands on a person, such as an exam, an argument or an illness in the family. Stress is the state a person is in when those demands feel too much for them. Stress can be acute (short-term, such as the hour before a presentation) or chronic (long-term, such as months of worry about money or a family member's health).

    When the brain detects a threat, the body responds through two systems. Both start in the hypothalamus, and both use chemical messengers. A chemical messenger is a substance that carries a signal from one part of the body to another. Neurotransmitters carry signals across the small gap between neurons. Hormones are released by glands into the bloodstream and travel to organs further away.

    • The fast response (the sympatho-adrenal medullary system). The hypothalamus activates the sympathetic nervous system. This signals the adrenal medulla (the centre of the adrenal glands, on top of the kidneys) to release adrenaline and noradrenaline. Within seconds, heart rate and breathing speed up and more glucose reaches the muscles. This is the "fight or flight" response.
    • The slower response (the HPA axis). The hypothalamus releases a hormone that signals the pituitary gland, which releases a second hormone that travels in the blood to the adrenal cortex (the outer layer of the adrenal glands). The adrenal cortex releases cortisol. Cortisol keeps energy available for longer by raising blood glucose, and it dampens some functions the body does not need in an emergency, such as parts of the immune response.

    In the short term this response is useful. It sharpens attention and gives the body energy to deal with the problem. The difficulty comes when the response stays switched on for weeks or months. Chronic stress, and the long-term release of cortisol that comes with it, has been linked to weaker immune function, poor sleep and a higher risk of several physical and mental health problems. This is why "stress and health" is an area of study in its own right.

    The biology also leaves a puzzle. Two students can sit the same mock exam, with the same hormones available to them, and one feels excited while the other feels overwhelmed. Your answers in the warm-up probably showed the same thing. To explain this, we need to look at how people think about a stressor.

    Stress as a transaction: Lazarus and Folkman (1984)

    Richard Lazarus and Susan Folkman proposed the transactional model of stress and coping in their book Stress, Appraisal, and Coping (1984). Their key claim is that stress is not a fixed property of an event. It is a transaction (an exchange) between a person and their situation. A situation becomes stressful when a person judges that its demands are greater than the resources they have to deal with it, and that their well-being is at stake. The judgement is called appraisal, and the model describes two kinds.

    Primary appraisal asks: "Does this matter to me, and how?" A situation can be judged irrelevant (it has nothing to do with me), benign-positive (it is good news), or stressful. A stressful situation can be appraised in three ways:

    • Harm or loss: the damage has already happened, for example a poor mark that has already been given.
    • Threat: harm might happen in the future, for example "I am going to fail this mock".
    • Challenge: the situation is demanding, but there is something to gain, for example "this mock will show me what I need to revise".

    Secondary appraisal asks: "What can I do about it?" The person weighs up their coping resources. These include their skills, time, money, health, and social support, meaning the people who can help. A mock exam appraised as a threat feels very different to a student who believes they have a plan and a teacher to ask, compared with a student who believes they have no time and nobody to help.

    The two appraisals do not have to happen in a fixed order, and they keep changing. Reappraisal happens when new information changes the judgement. A threat can become a challenge once a student makes a revision plan. Some textbooks use the word eustress for stress that feels manageable and motivating, which fits the challenge appraisal, and distress for stress that feels overwhelming.

    Problem-focused and emotion-focused coping

    Lazarus and Folkman defined coping as the effort a person makes to manage demands that they appraise as taxing or exceeding their resources. They described two main types.

    FeatureProblem-focused copingEmotion-focused coping
    What it targetsThe stressor itselfThe feelings the stressor produces
    The question behind it"What can I do to change this situation?""How can I feel less bad about this situation?"
    Examples before a mock examMaking a revision timetable, asking a teacher which topics matter most, doing past papersTalking to a friend, exercising, deep breathing, distracting yourself, avoiding thinking about the exam
    When it tends to helpWhen the situation can be changedWhen the situation cannot be changed, or while feelings are too strong to plan
    RiskCan add pressure if the problem is out of the person's controlAvoidance can give quick relief while the stressor grows

    Neither type is "good" or "bad". Most people use both, often at the same time. Exercising after a hard day is emotion-focused coping, and it can be a healthy choice. The question is whether the coping strategy fits the situation. Emotion-focused coping becomes a problem when it is the only thing a person does about a stressor they could change, because the relief is short and the stressor is still there.

    Social media as a way of coping

    Social media is always available, it is free, and it changes how we feel within seconds. That makes it an obvious emotion-focused coping tool. A student who picks up their phone between revision sessions is often not choosing social media for its own sake. They are choosing a break from a feeling. Posting, liking and replying can also bring social support, which can help.

    Researchers have suggested several reasons why this kind of coping can become harder to control:

    • Relief works as a reward. If scrolling reduces an unpleasant feeling, the behaviour is negatively reinforced (operant conditioning), so it becomes more likely the next time the feeling returns.
    • Flow. Flow is the experience of becoming so absorbed in an activity that you stop noticing time and your surroundings. The idea comes from the psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi. Flow feels good, and in many activities it is healthy. On social media, it may explain why "five minutes" turns into half an hour.
    • Active use. Posting, commenting and replying (active use) may be more absorbing than simply scrolling past content (passive use), because each action can bring a reply, a like or a new conversation.

    The direction can also run the other way. Heavy social media use can take time from sleep and revision and lead to conflict with family, which creates new stressors. Stress and problematic use may feed each other.

    What is "social media addiction"?

    Social media addiction is not a diagnosis in the DSM-5, the manual used to diagnose mental disorders. Researchers study it as a possible behavioural addiction, and many prefer the terms problematic social media use or addictive social media use. It is usually measured with a questionnaire, such as the six-item Bergen scales, which ask about six features of addiction:

    • Salience: social media dominates your thinking.
    • Mood modification: you use it to feel better or to forget problems.
    • Tolerance: you need more and more time on it to get the same effect.
    • Withdrawal: you feel restless or troubled when you cannot use it.
    • Conflict: it causes problems with school, family or other activities.
    • Relapse: you try to cut down but cannot.

    Look closely at mood modification. It is the coping link: using social media to escape stress is built into the definition of the problem. It also means that "heavy use" and "problematic use" are not the same thing. A student who spends three hours a day on social media for a school project is not showing addiction. A student who spends one hour, cannot stop, and feels restless without it may be showing some of its features. Be careful with the word "addicted" in everyday talk: it is a research label for a pattern of problems, not a description of anyone who likes their phone.

    Real-life examples

    • Distance learning in 2020. In March 2020, schools and universities across the UAE closed their buildings and moved to distance learning. For many students, the same screen became school, social life and entertainment, and stress about the pandemic arrived at the same time. Zhao and Zhou collected their data in China in the same weeks, which is part of why their study is useful.
    • Exam season in Dubai. A Grade 12 student takes a "quick break" on her phone after every past paper. She feels calmer straight away, but she is now finishing revision an hour later each night. The break is emotion-focused coping. Asking her teacher which topics to prioritise would be problem-focused coping.
    • Changing the situation. A family in Sharjah agrees that phones stay in the living room during revision hours and at meal times. They are not relying on willpower. They are changing the situation itself, which is a problem-focused way of reducing problematic use, and it removes the cue that triggers the habit.

    Key study 1: Zhao and Zhou (2021), COVID-19 stress and addictive social media use

    AimTo test whether college students who experienced more COVID-19-related stress were more likely to show addictive social media use, and whether this link could be explained by active use and social media flow.
    Participants705 Chinese college students volunteered after an advert was shared on WeChat. 192 were removed, for example because they failed attention checks or took far too long to complete the survey. The final sample was 512 students aged 18 to 30 (mean age 22.1); 62.5% were women.
    ProcedureA correlational study using an online questionnaire of about ten minutes, completed between 24 March and 1 April 2020. Measures: a 10-item yes/no checklist of stressful COVID-19 experiences (for example, lockdown or staying alone for a long time); a six-item version of the Bergen Facebook Addiction Scale, adapted to social media in general, measuring addictive use; a four-item scale of active use (posting, liking, commenting, sharing); and an 11-item flow scale (for example, "While using social media, I'm deeply engrossed"). Daily time on social media was self-reported and statistically controlled.
    FindingsCOVID-19 stress correlated positively with active use (r = 0.15), flow (r = 0.13) and addictive social media use (r = 0.16). Active use correlated with flow (r = 0.28) and with addictive use (r = 0.25). The strongest correlation was between flow and addictive use (r = 0.46). Stress was not linked to the amount of time spent on social media (r = 0.03). Path analysis was consistent with a chain: stress, then active use, then flow, then addictive use. A model with flow and active use in the reverse order was also statistically significant.
    ConclusionStudents under more COVID-19 stress were more likely to show addictive social media use, and active use and flow may help explain the link. The authors stated that the correlational design allows only hypothetical conclusions about causality.
    What happened nextThe authors recommended longitudinal designs, which measure the same people more than once, to test the direction of the effects. Key study 2 is an example of that kind of design.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section B (stress and coping applied to a scenario); Paper 1 Section C on bias, causality or measurement; Paper 2 Section B practice (a correlational questionnaire study).
    Evaluate it withCausality: all variables were measured at the same moment, so stress could lead to addictive use, addictive use could make people notice or report stress, or a third variable could affect both. The reverse model also fitted. Measurement: the stress checklist counted events, not appraisals, and the average score was low (about 1.3 events out of 10). Everything was self-reported. Bias: volunteers who saw an advert on WeChat may not represent all students, and the sample was young Chinese college students during one unusual event.

    Key study 2: Zhou et al. (2023), stress and problematic social media use over a year

    AimTo examine how problematic social media use and mental health risks (depression, anxiety, stress and loneliness) relate to each other over time in first-year university students.
    Participants822 first-year undergraduates at three universities in South China took part at the start. 685 completed all three surveys (464 women, 221 men; mean age 19.1).
    ProcedureA longitudinal study with three waves. The same online survey was given in October 2021, then about four and eight months later, ending in May 2022. Problematic social media use was measured with the six-item Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale; a score of 24 or more was used as the cut-off. Stress was measured with the stress items of the DASS-21, a questionnaire about symptoms of depression, anxiety and stress. Participants gave consent at the start of each survey, and received a small payment after the third wave. Cross-lagged analysis tested whether each variable at one wave predicted the other variable at the next wave, while taking earlier levels into account.
    FindingsThe percentage of students above the cut-off rose from 2.5% to 3.6% to 4.7% across the year. Stress and problematic use were positively correlated at every wave (r = 0.33, 0.43 and 0.40). Stress at wave 1 predicted problematic use at wave 2 (β = 0.13). But stress at wave 2 predicted lower problematic use at wave 3 (β = -0.17). Problematic use at wave 2 predicted slightly higher stress at wave 3 (β = 0.06).
    ConclusionThe authors concluded that problematic social media use and mental health risks can predict each other "in a vicious loop". For stress, the pattern was not consistent. The authors suggested that rising workload later in the year may have left students with less free time for social media, but this explanation was not tested.
    Use it forPaper 1 Section C on causality or bias (a longitudinal design compared with Zhao and Zhou); Paper 1 Section B (stress and problematic use feeding each other).
    Evaluate it withCausality: measuring stress before problematic use gives a time order, which is stronger evidence than a single survey, but it is still not an experiment, and a third variable such as sleep or workload could explain both. Bias: the striking "vicious loop" summary is easier to repeat than the negative path; 137 students dropped out, and those who stayed may differ from those who left. Measurement: all data were self-reported, and few students passed the cut-off, so most of the sample showed mild or no problems.

    How useful is research on stress and social media addiction?

    Strengths. The transactional model explains why the same stressor affects people differently, which a purely biological account cannot do. It points to practical help: changing appraisals (seeing a mock as a challenge) and building problem-focused coping (planning, asking for support). The research on social media is consistent in one respect: stress and problematic use are positively correlated across different samples and designs. Zhao and Zhou also tested a possible mechanism (active use and flow) rather than only reporting a link.

    Limitations. Appraisal cannot be observed. It is measured by asking people, so the model depends on self-report. The correlations are small: a correlation of 0.16 means stress accounts for less than 3% of the variation in addictive use, so most of the story lies elsewhere. Both key studies used Chinese university students, which limits generalisation to adolescents in the UAE. Most research is correlational, so the direction of the link is still uncertain, and Zhou et al. found that it may even change during a single year. Finally, because social media addiction is not an agreed diagnosis, there is a risk of labelling ordinary, heavy use as a disorder.

    The concepts in this chapter

    ConceptHow it applies to stress and social media addiction
    MeasurementStress, appraisal, flow and addictive use are all measured by questionnaires. Counting stressful events (Zhao and Zhou) is not the same as measuring how stressed someone feels (Zhou et al.).
    CausalityZhao and Zhou measured everything once, so the direction is unknown. Zhou et al. measured three times and found that stress predicted later problematic use in one interval but not the next.
    BiasVolunteer samples, self-report by heavy users, drop-out between waves, and the habit of repeating a careful correlation as "stress causes addiction".
    ChangeAppraisals change through reappraisal, and problematic use rose across the first university year in Zhou et al.
    PerspectiveThe stress response is a biological account; appraisal and coping are cognitive; social support and family rules about phones are sociocultural. Each explains part of the problem.
    ResponsibilityPlatforms are designed to hold attention. Researchers, schools and families have a responsibility not to label ordinary phone use as addiction, and to help students build coping options.
